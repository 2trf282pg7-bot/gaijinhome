Guarantor Refused in Japan? How Foreigners Get Approved Without a Japanese Guarantor

Updated November 2026 · GaijinHome

You found a place you like, filled in the application, and then the agent asked: "Do you have a Japanese guarantor?" You don't. Maybe you asked a coworker and they politely declined. Maybe your company said it does not act as guarantor. Now the application is stuck and the agent has gone quiet.

Here is the good news: in today's Japanese rental market, a personal Japanese guarantor is rarely the only route. Most leases now run through a guarantor company (保証会社, hoshō gaisha), and there are several more options beyond that. The trick is knowing which door to push on, and how to present yourself so the screening goes your way.

What Actually Happens

The phrase "guarantor refused" covers a few different situations. Identify yours first, because the fix is different for each:

  • The landlord insists on a personal guarantor (連帯保証人, rentai hoshōnin) in addition to, or instead of, a guarantor company. Older or individual landlords sometimes do this.
  • The guarantor company screened you and declined. The agent usually says only "審査が通りませんでした" (the screening did not pass), with no reason given.
  • The guarantor company approved you but asked for an emergency contact (緊急連絡先) who must be a Japanese speaker living in Japan, and you have no one to list.
  • Your personal guarantor was rejected because they are a foreign national, retired, or their income was considered too low.

Why It Happens

The landlord's fear is unpaid rent and disappearing tenants

Evicting a tenant in Japan is slow, because the Act on Land and Building Leases (借地借家法) strongly protects occupants. Landlords respond by trying to make sure someone will always pay. Historically that someone was a relative or employer who signed as joint guarantor. For a foreigner, the specific worry is: "If this person leaves Japan suddenly, who do I chase?"

Guarantor companies replaced personal guarantors, but they screen

Guarantor companies pay the landlord if you don't, then recover the money from you. You pay them a fee, commonly around 50–100% of one month's rent at signing (sometimes based on total monthly cost including management fees) plus an annual renewal fee, often around ¥10,000 or a percentage of rent. Because they take the risk, they run their own screening. Some are linked to credit card industry data, some to rental-payment histories, and some do only an in-house check. Different companies, different outcomes.

Things that commonly trip up foreign applicants

  • Remaining visa period shorter than the lease. A two-year lease with eight months left on your residence card looks risky, even if renewal is routine for you.
  • Very new arrival with no Japanese income history and no Japanese phone number in your name.
  • Emergency contact who does not speak Japanese or lives abroad.
  • Past late payments on a phone contract or credit card in Japan, if the company checks credit data.
  • Job type: freelance, part-time, or a probation period can lower the score.

For a deeper look at the credit side, see how to pass a credit check in Japan as a foreigner.

Common Mistakes

  1. Re-applying through the same agent for a similar property. The agent may submit to the same guarantor company, and you get the same result. Ask which company they use.
  2. Listing a non-Japanese-speaking friend as emergency contact. Guarantor companies sometimes call the contact in Japanese to confirm. If they can't communicate, it can count against you.
  3. Hiding a short visa period. The card is checked anyway. It is better to explain upfront that you are eligible for renewal and show a supporting letter.
  4. Applying before you have a Japanese phone number. Screeners need to reach you. A prepaid or data-only SIM without a voice number can be a problem.
  5. Assuming "no guarantor" means "no screening". Even no-guarantor listings use a guarantor company. You still need to pass something.

Step-by-Step Fix

  1. Ask the agent exactly which requirement failed. Say: "保証人が必要なのは大家さんの条件ですか？それとも保証会社の審査結果ですか？" (Is the guarantor a landlord requirement, or was it the guarantor company's screening result?) This tells you whether to change landlords or change guarantor companies.
  2. If the landlord demands a personal guarantor, move on quickly. Ask the agent to filter listings with the condition 保証会社利用可 (guarantor company accepted) or 保証人不要 (no guarantor required). Most modern listings already qualify. Don't waste weeks negotiating with an owner who wants a relative's signature.
  3. If a guarantor company declined you, ask for a different one. Many management companies work with two or more guarantor companies. Ask: "別の保証会社で再審査できますか？" (Can I be re-screened with a different guarantor company?) Some guarantor companies specialize in foreign residents and offer multilingual support.
  4. Strengthen your file before re-screening. Add a certificate of employment, last three months' payslips, and a copy of your bank balance. If your visa ends before the lease, add a letter from your employer stating your contract continues and they will support renewal.
  5. Solve the emergency contact problem. Options: a Japanese-speaking coworker who agrees only to be a contact (not a guarantor — make this distinction clear to them, it is a much smaller ask), your company's HR department, or a guarantor company that offers an emergency-contact service for foreigners. Ask the agent which is acceptable.
  6. Offer something extra. A higher deposit (e.g. one extra month), paying several months upfront, or paying the guarantor fee at the higher plan level can tip a borderline decision. Ask the agent to pass this offer to the owner.
  7. Ask your employer about a corporate lease (法人契約). If your company signs the lease and you live there, your personal screening is mostly replaced by the company's. Many mid-size and large employers do this even if it is not advertised.
  8. Use routes that skip the issue entirely. UR public housing does not require a guarantor or key money (see how to apply for UR housing, with income or savings requirements). Share houses and monthly apartments are another route while you build history. [PR] CrossOneRoom offers furnished Tokyo apartments aimed at foreign residents.
Key tip: Never ask a coworker to be your joint guarantor (連帯保証人) as a first move. It makes them legally responsible for your debt and most people will refuse. Asking them to be only an emergency contact (緊急連絡先) is a small favor, and it is often all the guarantor company actually needs.

Required Documents

  • Residence card (在留カード), front and back
  • Passport, with visa page
  • Certificate of employment (在職証明書) showing start date, position and salary
  • Recent payslips (給与明細), usually the last 3 months
  • Tax certificate (課税証明書) or income certificate (所得証明書), if you have lived in Japan long enough to have one
  • Offer letter or employment contract (雇用契約書), for new arrivals
  • Student ID and admission letter (在学証明書), for students
  • Bank balance proof (残高証明書) or bank book copy, especially for freelancers or anyone offering prepaid rent
  • Japanese phone number in your name
  • Emergency contact's name, address, phone number and relationship to you
  • Personal seal (印鑑) or signature, depending on the contract

City and Region Differences

Tokyo has the largest supply of foreigner-friendly agents and guarantor companies with English support, so switching guarantor companies is easiest here. Competition for well-priced units in central wards is high, though, and landlords can afford to be picky. For specifics, see no-guarantor apartments in Tokyo.

Osaka and Kansai generally accept guarantor companies widely, and initial costs can be lower in some areas. Some older landlords still prefer personal guarantors, so ask early. Our Osaka no-guarantor guide covers the city in detail.

Regional cities (Nagoya, Fukuoka, Sendai, Hiroshima and smaller towns) may have fewer agents used to foreign applicants, and individual landlords are more common. Here, a corporate lease or a letter from your employer often carries more weight than in Tokyo, because local agents value relationships with local companies.

Frequently Asked Questions

Can a foreigner be my guarantor in Japan?

Sometimes. A foreign national with permanent residency (永住者), stable income and a long residence history is often accepted as a personal guarantor. Someone on a short-term work visa is less likely to be accepted. In practice, most landlords now prefer a guarantor company anyway.

How much does a guarantor company cost?

Typically around 50–100% of one month's rent (or total monthly cost) at the start, plus a renewal fee every year or two. Some plans charge a small monthly percentage instead. The exact amount depends on the company and plan, so ask the agent for the fee before you apply.

Why won't they tell me why the screening failed?

Guarantor companies generally do not disclose their reasons to applicants or even to agents. That's why the best response is to switch to a different guarantor company and strengthen your documents, rather than trying to argue with the decision.

Does my company have to be my guarantor?

No. Japanese employers are not required to guarantee your lease, and many will refuse. However, many will provide a certificate of employment, act as an emergency contact, or arrange a corporate lease. Ask HR about those smaller requests first.

What if I have been rejected several times already?

Step back and check the common blockers: visa period, phone number, emergency contact, and income proof. Then target routes with lighter screening such as UR housing, share houses or monthly apartments. Our rejected applications guide and the no-guarantor hub list more alternatives.
