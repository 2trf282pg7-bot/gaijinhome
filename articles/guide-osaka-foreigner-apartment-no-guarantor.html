Apartment Denied in Osaka Because You Have No Guarantor? Exact Fixes for Foreigners

Updated November 2026 · GaijinHome

Osaka has a reputation as one of the friendlier big cities in Japan, and rents are lower than Tokyo. So it stings when an agent in Umeda or Namba tells you the application can't move forward because you have no guarantor. You have a job, you have savings, and you just want a place to live.

The good news: in Osaka, "no guarantor" is almost always a solvable problem. Most rentals here run through guarantor companies, and the city has plenty of agents who regularly work with foreign tenants. What you need is to understand exactly which step failed, and to use the routes that work in Kansai specifically.

What Actually Happens

When foreigners in Osaka say they were "denied for no guarantor," it is usually one of these:

  • The listing says 保証人要 (guarantor required) and the agent asks for a relative or Japanese acquaintance to sign as joint guarantor.
  • The agent submitted you to a guarantor company and it declined, and the agent explained it as "you need a guarantor."
  • The guarantor company accepted you but requires an emergency contact in Japan, and you have no one.
  • The owner of an older building in a residential area like Ikuno or Higashinari wants a personal guarantor out of habit, even though a guarantor company is available.

Why It Happens

Osaka's rental market mixes modern and very traditional landlords

Newer apartments, especially in central wards like Kita, Chuo, Nishi and Naniwa, are mostly managed by companies that use guarantor companies as standard. But Osaka also has a large stock of older buildings run by individual owners who may still expect a personal guarantor (連帯保証人). The same agent might show you both kinds without telling you which is which.

Guarantor company screening is the real gate

For most units, your actual hurdle is the guarantor company's screening. It typically looks at your income relative to rent (a common rule of thumb is rent around one-third of monthly income or less), your employment stability, your visa period, and whether they can reach you and your emergency contact. A short visa, recent arrival, or freelance income can all lead to a decline.

The Kansai deposit system can confuse the picture

Some Osaka contracts still use the Kansai-style hoshokin (保証金) — a larger deposit — with a fixed non-refundable deduction called shikibiki (敷引き). The name "保証金" literally includes the character for "guarantee," and some foreigners misunderstand this as a guarantor requirement. It is a deposit, not a guarantor. Many newer Osaka listings now use Tokyo-style shikikin and reikin instead.

Communication worries

Owners and guarantor companies worry about reaching you if rent is late, if there is a leak, or if neighbors complain. A Japanese-speaking emergency contact eases that worry, which is why it is often requested.

Common Mistakes

  1. Accepting "you need a guarantor" without asking what kind. Ask whether the owner needs a personal guarantor or the guarantor company declined. The fixes are different.
  2. Applying for rent too high for your income. If rent is well above about one-third of your monthly income, screening often fails. Applying for a slightly cheaper unit can be the fastest fix.
  3. Using only big portal sites. Many foreigner-friendly Osaka listings come through agents who specialize in international tenants and don't advertise heavily on Japanese portals.
  4. Ignoring the move-out terms of a hoshokin contract. With shikibiki, a significant part of your deposit may never come back. Read this before signing, not after. Our deposit dispute guide explains what can and cannot be recovered.
  5. Giving up after one rejection. One guarantor company's "no" says little about the next one.

Step-by-Step Fix

  1. Clarify the cause. Ask the agent: "大家さんが連帯保証人を求めていますか？それとも保証会社の審査に落ちましたか？" (Does the owner require a joint guarantor, or did I fail the guarantor company screening?)
  2. If it's the owner, switch listings. Ask the agent to search only units marked 保証人不要 or 保証会社利用可, and 外国人可. In central Osaka, there are many. Don't spend time persuading a traditional owner.
  3. If it's the guarantor company, request a second company. Say: "別の保証会社でもう一度審査していただけますか？" (Could you screen me again with another guarantor company?) Some guarantor companies are known for supporting foreign residents and offer multilingual service; ask the agent which ones they work with.
  4. Bring a stronger application. Add your certificate of employment, three months' payslips, and a bank balance statement. If your visa expires before the lease ends, add an employer letter confirming your contract continues.
  5. Find an emergency contact. A coworker who agrees to be only an emergency contact, your company's HR, or your school's international office. Explain clearly that they take on no payment obligation; this makes people far more willing to agree.
  6. Offer to reduce the owner's risk. Propose paying an extra month of deposit or a few months' rent in advance. This can turn a borderline case into an approval, especially with individual owners.
  7. Use guarantor-free alternatives. UR public housing operates many estates around Osaka and does not require a guarantor (see how to apply for UR). Key tip: In Osaka, ask early whether the contract is hoshokin/shikibiki style or shikikin/reikin style. Two units with the same rent can have very different initial and move-out costs, and knowing this helps you choose a unit the screening (and your budget) will accept.

Required Documents

  • Residence card (在留カード), front and back
  • Passport
  • Certificate of employment (在職証明書) with salary and start date
  • Last 3 months' payslips (給与明細), or employment contract (雇用契約書) for new hires
  • Tax certificate (課税証明書), if you have lived in Japan long enough to receive one
  • Bank balance certificate (残高証明書) or bank book copy, especially for students, freelancers or prepaid offers
  • Student enrollment certificate (在学証明書), for students
  • Employer letter confirming visa renewal support, if your visa ends before the lease
  • Japanese mobile phone number in your name
  • Emergency contact's name, address, phone and relationship
  • Personal seal (印鑑) if the contract requires it

Osaka Neighborhoods, Rents and Local Quirks

Areas where foreigners often find approval easier

  • Kita-ku (Umeda, Nakatsu, Tenma): central, newer buildings, mostly company-managed with guarantor companies. Higher rents.
  • Chuo-ku and Nishi-ku (Honmachi, Shinsaibashi, Kitahorie, Awaza): popular with foreign professionals, many modern 1K/1LDK units.
  • Naniwa-ku (Namba, Daikokucho, Sakuragawa): lively and well-connected, with many studios; check noise levels near nightlife.
  • Yodogawa-ku (Juso, Nishinakajima): one stop from Umeda, often better value, with access to Shin-Osaka for shinkansen.
  • Tennoji and Abeno: southern hub with good transport and a mix of old and new buildings.
  • Suita and Toyonaka (north): quieter, near universities, popular with families and researchers.

Rough rent ranges

As a rough guide, a 1K or 1R studio in central Osaka often falls somewhere around ¥50,000–85,000 per month, and a 1LDK around ¥80,000–150,000, with new buildings near Umeda and Shinsaibashi at the top end. Outer wards and suburbs are cheaper. These are estimates only; check current listings for the exact area you want.

Kansai quirks to watch

  • Hoshokin and shikibiki contracts are still found, particularly in older buildings and some suburbs.
  • Zero-deposit, zero-key-money listings (ゼロゼロ物件) are common, but often have higher rent, cleaning fees or stricter early-termination penalties. See our hidden costs guide.
  • Renewal fees are generally less common in Osaka than in Tokyo, though not unknown.

Compared with Tokyo, Osaka typically has lower rents and slightly less competition, so a well-prepared foreign applicant has good odds. For a wider look at the city, visit our Osaka guide, and for national no-guarantor options see the no-guarantor hub.

Frequently Asked Questions

Is hoshokin the same as having a guarantor?

No. Hoshokin (保証金) is a deposit used in the Kansai region, often with a fixed portion (shikibiki) kept at move-out. It has nothing to do with a personal guarantor. You may need both a hoshokin deposit and a guarantor company in the same contract.

Can I rent in Osaka with no guarantor and no Japanese friends?

Yes. Most Osaka rentals accept a guarantor company in place of a personal guarantor. For the emergency contact, ask your employer's HR or school office, or ask the agent whether the guarantor company offers a contact service for foreign residents.

How much is the guarantor company fee in Osaka?

It's usually similar to elsewhere in Japan: typically around 50–100% of one month's rent or total monthly cost at signing, plus a periodic renewal fee. The exact amount depends on the company and plan, so ask before applying.

I'm a student in Osaka. Will I pass screening?

Students often pass with a parent's or sponsor's financial proof, a bank balance statement, and the school's enrollment certificate. Some guarantor companies accept students more readily than others, and many universities can introduce agents used to international students.

Should I take a zero-deposit apartment to avoid problems?

It can lower your initial cost, but check the total. Zero-zero listings sometimes come with higher rent, a fixed cleaning fee, or a penalty for leaving within one or two years.
