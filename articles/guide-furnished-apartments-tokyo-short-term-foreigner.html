Short-Term Furnished Apartments in Tokyo Denied? What Foreigners Actually Get Approved For

Updated November 2026 · GaijinHome

You need a furnished place in Tokyo for one to six months. Maybe you just arrived on a work visa and your company housing isn't ready. Maybe you're between leases, on an internship, or waiting to see if a job works out before signing a two-year contract. You sent inquiries, and the answers came back: "minimum two-year contract," "foreigners need a guarantor," "we can't accept tourists," or simply no reply.

The problem is usually not you. It's that you're applying to the wrong category of housing. Tokyo has several distinct types of short-term furnished housing, each with its own rules, and normal rental agents mostly don't deal with them. This guide sorts out which one fits your situation and how to get approved.

What Actually Happens

Foreigners looking for short-term furnished housing in Tokyo typically run into one of these situations:

  • A regular apartment agent says no because standard leases (普通借家契約) are designed for two years, are unfurnished, and require a guarantor company screening that takes days.
  • A monthly apartment operator declines because your visa status, stay purpose, or payment method did not fit their policy.
  • A vacation rental (minpaku) host refuses a long stay or the platform caps how long you can book.
  • You are approved but the price is shocking, because short-term furnished units charge much more per month than a normal lease.

Why It Happens

Japan's rental law makes landlords wary of short lets on normal leases

Under the Act on Land and Building Leases (借地借家法), a standard lease gives tenants strong rights to stay and renew. Landlords who want a short, fixed stay use a fixed-term lease (定期借家契約), which ends on a set date. Ordinary agents rarely offer these for a few months, so they refuse rather than explain.

Each housing type is regulated differently

  • Monthly apartments (マンスリーマンション) are usually fixed-term rental contracts, typically from one month. They come furnished with utilities often included. Screening is lighter than a normal lease but still exists.
  • Weekly apartments (ウィークリーマンション) are similar but for shorter stays; some operate more like lodging.
  • Share houses (シェアハウス) rent a furnished private room with shared kitchen and bath, often with no guarantor and a short minimum stay.
  • Vacation rentals (民泊) under Japan's private lodging law are limited in how many nights per year they can operate, and some local wards restrict them further. Hosts may therefore refuse long bookings.
  • Serviced apartments are hotel-like, accept foreigners easily, but cost the most.

Visa status and stay purpose matter

Operators want to know you are legally staying for the full period. A tourist on a temporary visitor status may be turned away by monthly apartment operators that only accept residents, while a new arrival with a residence card (在留カード) but no Japanese bank account may be blocked by payment rules. Some operators accept overseas credit cards; others require Japanese bank transfer.

Furnished units are a premium product

Short-term furnished units bundle furniture, appliances, internet, utilities and cleaning. Monthly costs commonly run noticeably above an equivalent unfurnished lease. Operators protect that margin by screening out people they think might cancel early or cause damage.

Common Mistakes

  1. Asking a regular agent for a three-month lease. Most can't help. Go directly to monthly apartment operators, share houses, or furnished-rental specialists.
  2. Booking a vacation rental for months without checking the rules. Hosts can cancel or refuse when the booking exceeds what they are allowed to offer.
  3. Not stating your visa status up front. Saying "I am on a work visa with a residence card valid until X" in your first message gets far better responses than "I need a flat."
  4. Comparing only the monthly rent. Check cleaning fees, linen fees, utilities caps, and early-termination charges. Some cheap-looking listings add significant extras.
  5. Waiting too long. Furnished units near major stations fill quickly, especially around April and September when many people move.

Step-by-Step Fix

  1. Decide your real length of stay. Under 1 month: hotel, serviced apartment or weekly apartment. 1–6 months: monthly apartment or share house. 6 months or more: a furnished rental on a fixed-term lease, or a share house with a private room. This decision tells you which providers to contact.
  2. Write one clear first message. Include: arrival date, departure date (or "at least X months"), visa type and residence card status, occupation or school, number of occupants, and how you can pay. Operators reply faster when they don't have to ask.
  3. Go direct to monthly apartment operators. Many large operators have English or simple online booking. Ask: "Do you accept foreign residents with a residence card? Is a Japanese emergency contact required?" See our monthly mansion guide for how these contracts work.
  4. Consider a share house if budget matters. Share houses are often the easiest approval for foreigners: many require no guarantor and accept people shortly after arrival. Read share houses in Tokyo for English speakers to understand room types and house rules.
  5. Use a furnished-rental specialist for longer stays. Companies that focus on foreign residents can offer furnished apartments on contracts that fit a visa period. [PR] CrossOneRoom furnished Tokyo apartments is one option aimed at international residents.
  6. Fix payment blockers early. If you have no Japanese bank account yet, ask: "Can I pay by overseas credit card or international transfer?" If not, move on to an operator that can. Opening a Japanese bank account after receiving your residence card and address registration opens more options later.
  7. Read the cancellation and extension terms. Ask what happens if your company housing is delayed and you need to extend, and what you pay if you leave early. Get the answer in writing by email.
  8. Use the stay to prepare your long-term lease. While in short-term housing, register your address, get a Japanese phone number, collect payslips, and research no-guarantor apartments in Tokyo. This makes your next application much stronger.
Key tip: Some operators allow you to register your address (転入届) at a monthly apartment or share house, but not all do. If you need an address for your residence card, bank account or phone contract, ask "住民登録はできますか？" (Can I register my residence here?) before you book.

Required Documents

  • Passport, with landing permission or visa page
  • Residence card (在留カード), if you have one; for new arrivals, the card issued at the airport
  • Certificate of employment (在職証明書) or job offer letter, for work visa holders
  • Certificate of enrollment (在学証明書) or acceptance letter, for students
  • Credit card (overseas cards accepted by some operators) or Japanese bank account details
  • Japanese phone number, if you already have one; otherwise an email address and messaging app contact
  • Emergency contact details (some operators accept overseas contacts, others require a contact in Japan)
  • Company letter confirming your housing arrangement, if your employer is paying

Tokyo Areas, Prices and Differences From Other Cities

Where short-term furnished housing is concentrated

  • Shinjuku, Shibuya, Ikebukuro: the largest supply of monthly apartments and share houses, with the most English-friendly operators. Prices are at the higher end.
  • Minato-ku (Roppongi, Azabu, Hiroo): serviced apartments and high-end furnished rentals for corporate stays.
  • Nakano, Koenji, Kichijoji (Chuo Line west): popular for share houses and mid-priced monthly units with easy access to Shinjuku.
  • Ueno, Asakusa, Kinshicho (east side): often better value, with good transport to central Tokyo.
  • Kawasaki, Yokohama, Saitama: just outside Tokyo, frequently cheaper for the same commute time.

Rough monthly costs

Prices vary widely with season, building age and location. As a rough guide, a private room in a Tokyo share house often runs around ¥40,000–80,000 per month including utilities, a monthly apartment studio commonly around ¥100,000–200,000+ per month all-in, and serviced apartments considerably more. Expect higher prices around spring moving season. Always compare the total cost, not just the headline rent; our hidden costs guide lists the extras to check.

Compared with other cities

Osaka has a solid but smaller supply of monthly apartments and share houses, generally cheaper than Tokyo for similar quality. Nagoya, Fukuoka and other regional cities have fewer foreigner-focused operators, so you may need a Japanese-language booking or local help. See our Osaka guide for Kansai options.

Frequently Asked Questions

Can I rent a furnished apartment in Tokyo for 3 months as a foreigner?

Yes. Monthly apartments and share houses regularly accept stays of one to six months. A regular two-year lease is the wrong product for this, which is why ordinary agents refuse. Contact monthly apartment operators or share house companies directly.

Can tourists rent monthly apartments?

Some operators accept guests on temporary visitor status, while others accept only residents with a residence card. Policies differ by company, so state your visa status in your first message and ask directly.

Do I need a guarantor for a monthly apartment?

Usually not a personal guarantor. Many operators instead require prepayment of the full stay or a credit card, and some ask for an emergency contact. Share houses frequently need no guarantor at all.

Why is short-term furnished housing so expensive?

The price includes furniture, appliances, utilities, internet and often cleaning, plus the operator's risk of empty periods between short stays. If you will stay six months or more, it is often cheaper to move to a standard lease once you have your documents ready.

My company housing was delayed. What should I do first?

Ask your employer whether they will cover temporary housing, then book a monthly apartment with flexible extension terms. Confirm in writing what happens if you need to stay longer or leave earlier than planned.
