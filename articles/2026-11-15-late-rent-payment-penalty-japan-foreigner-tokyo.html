Late Rent Payment in Tokyo as a Foreigner: Penalties, Guarantor Company Calls, and What to Do Now

Updated November 2026 · GaijinHome

You checked your bank app and realized the rent did not go out. Or your salary came in a few days late, or the automatic transfer you thought was set up never started. Now there is a letter in Japanese in your mailbox, your phone shows missed calls from a number you do not recognize, and you are worried that you are about to be evicted or blacklisted. Take a breath. A single late payment is very common in Japan, and it is usually fixable within days if you act quickly and communicate clearly. This article explains what actually happens when rent is late in Tokyo, why the system feels so harsh for foreigners, and exactly what to do to limit the damage.

What actually happens

Most rental contracts in Tokyo set the rent due date for the end of the previous month. In other words, December rent is often due by November 27 or November 30, not on December 1. Many foreigners miss their first payment simply because they assumed rent is paid at the start of the month it covers.

When the payment does not arrive, the sequence usually looks like this:

  • Day 1 to 5: The management company or the rent guarantor company (保証会社, hoshou gaisha) notices the missing payment. You may get an SMS, an automated call, or a phone call from a staff member speaking Japanese.
  • Around one to two weeks: A reminder letter (督促状, tokusokujou) arrives, often with a payment slip or bank details. If a guarantor company is involved, it may pay the landlord on your behalf and then demand the money from you directly.
  • Repeated or long delays: Calls become more frequent, your emergency contact may be called, and the company may send a formal written demand. Only after a longer period of non-payment, typically several months, does the landlord realistically start the process of ending the contract.

The financial penalty itself is usually modest. Many contracts include a late fee (遅延損害金, chien songaikin) calculated as an annual percentage of the overdue rent. For residential tenants, Japanese consumer protection law generally limits this kind of late-payment charge to 14.6% per year. On a ¥90,000 rent paid ten days late, that works out to only a few hundred yen. Some guarantor companies also charge a fixed handling fee for paying the landlord on your behalf, often in the range of a few hundred to a few thousand yen, depending on your guarantee contract.

The real cost is not the fee. It is the record. A late payment noted by the guarantor company can make your lease renewal harder and can follow you when you apply for your next apartment.

Important: A landlord or guarantor company is not allowed to change your locks, remove your belongings, or force you out on their own because rent is late. Eviction in Japan requires a legal process. If someone threatens this after one late payment, stay calm, pay what you owe, and keep a record of every contact.

Why it happens

Late rent is rarely about someone not wanting to pay. For foreigners in Tokyo, it usually comes from structural problems in how the Japanese rental system works.

The guarantor company stands between you and the landlord

Almost every foreigner renting in Tokyo signs up with a rent guarantor company instead of using a Japanese family member as a guarantor. The guarantor company promises to pay the landlord if you do not. That means the landlord gets paid either way, and the guarantor company becomes the one chasing you. Their staff are trained to collect quickly, they call often, and they usually do it in Japanese. This can feel aggressive even when the amount is small. Payment records are shared and remembered

Some guarantor companies belong to industry groups that share information about payment history. A pattern of late payments can lead to future screening rejections, and you may never be told the reason. This is one of the hidden causes behind the rejections described on our rejected applications page.

Automatic bank transfers take time to start

Setting up automatic debit (口座振替, kouza furikae) often takes one to two months after move-in. Until it starts, you are expected to pay manually by bank transfer or convenience store slip. Many tenants do not realize this, assume the system is already active, and miss the second or third month.

Salary timing and visa changes

If you are paid on the 25th and rent is due on the 27th, a public holiday or a delayed first paycheck at a new job can easily push you over the deadline. Job changes, visa renewals, and gaps between contracts can also create a month where money is tight.

Language barriers make silence the default

When a call comes in Japanese, many people do not answer. To the company, an unanswered phone looks like a tenant who is avoiding them. That raises the risk level they assign to you, which leads to more calls and calls to your emergency contact. Communication, not money, is often the biggest problem.

Common mistakes

  1. Ignoring the calls. Not answering is the fastest way to turn a small delay into a serious problem. The company cannot help you set a payment date if they cannot reach you.
  2. Paying the landlord when the guarantor already paid. If the guarantor company has already covered your rent, you must pay the guarantor, not the landlord or the management company. Paying the wrong party causes double payment confusion and more late notices.
  3. Promising a date you cannot meet. Missing a payment promise is worse than missing the original deadline. Give a realistic date, even if it is a week later.
  4. Assuming the late fee is the whole story. The fee may be small, but repeated late payments can affect your renewal and your future applications.
  5. Not telling your emergency contact. If your friend or coworker gets a surprise call in Japanese about your rent, it can damage the relationship. A short heads-up helps.

Step-by-step fix

  1. Find out who you owe. Look at your rental contract and the letter you received. Check whether the sender is the management company (管理会社, kanri gaisha) or the guarantor company. The company name, phone number, and bank account will be on the notice. If you cannot read it, use a translation app on the photo.
  2. Call them back yourself, today. Calling first shows good faith. If your Japanese is limited, you can say: "Yachin no shiharai ga okurete sumimasen. Itsu made ni harau ka sōdan shitai desu." (I am sorry my rent is late. I would like to discuss when I can pay.) Ask whether they have English support; some larger guarantor companies do.
  3. Confirm the exact amount. Ask for the total, including any late fee or handling fee, and the account or method to pay. Write down the name of the person you spoke with and the date.
  4. Pay as soon as possible, in full if you can. Use a bank transfer (振込, furikomi) or the convenience store payment slip they provide. Keep the receipt or a screenshot. If you used a transfer, put your name and room number in the transfer name field if they ask you to.
  5. If you cannot pay in full, ask for a plan. Say clearly how much you can pay now and when you can pay the rest. Many companies will accept a short payment plan if you stay in contact. Get the agreed dates in writing, even just by email or SMS.
  6. Fix the cause. Check whether your automatic transfer is actually active. If your bank account is the issue, our article on bank account rejections in Japan covers what to do. If your salary date is the problem, ask your landlord whether the debit date can be moved, or set a calendar reminder a week before each due date.
  7. Tell your emergency contact. Let them know there was a delay, that you have spoken to the company, and that the issue is being resolved. If you have trouble finding someone to list at all, see what to do when you have no emergency contact.
  8. If rent is no longer affordable, plan a move early. Do not wait until you are several months behind. Required documents

Keep these ready when you call the management or guarantor company. Having them nearby lets you answer questions quickly and shows that you are taking the issue seriously.

  • Rental contract (賃貸借契約書, chintai shakuyaku keiyakusho)
  • Guarantee contract with the guarantor company (保証委託契約書, hoshou itaku keiyakusho)
  • The reminder letter or demand notice (督促状, tokusokujou)
  • Residence card (在留カード, zairyu card), in case they need to confirm your identity
  • Bank passbook or banking app showing your recent payments (通帳, tsuuchou)
  • Payment receipts or transfer confirmations for past and current rent
  • Pay slip or employment contract (給与明細, kyuuyo meisai), if you are asking for a payment plan

City / region differences

Central Tokyo wards such as Minato, Shibuya, and Shinjuku have more properties managed by large companies and more guarantor companies with some English support. Collection is systematic: automated SMS and calls start quickly, but the process is predictable and payment plans are often handled by a dedicated team.

Outer Tokyo wards and the Tama area have more small, family-run landlords and local agencies. A late payment may be handled with a phone call from the agency or even the landlord directly. This can be more flexible, but communication is almost always in Japanese.

Rent levels matter too. A one-room apartment in central Tokyo can easily cost ¥90,000 to ¥130,000 per month, while similar rooms further out may be ¥55,000 to ¥80,000. If you are regularly struggling to pay on time, moving a few train stops out can make the monthly budget much more stable.

Outside Tokyo, the process is broadly similar in Osaka, Yokohama, Nagoya, and Fukuoka because the same national guarantor companies operate there. Landlords in smaller cities may rely more on personal relationships, so a polite, early phone call can go a long way. Frequently Asked Questions

Will I be evicted for paying rent one week late?

Almost certainly not. In Japan, a landlord generally needs a serious, ongoing breach, such as several months of unpaid rent, before a court will allow a contract to be ended. One short delay, followed by payment and good communication, does not usually lead to eviction. It can, however, be noted in your payment history.

How much is the late fee for rent in Tokyo?

It depends on your contract. Many contracts set an annual late-payment rate, and for residential tenants this is generally capped at 14.6% per year, so a short delay usually costs only a small amount. Guarantor companies may add a separate fixed fee if they paid the landlord on your behalf. Check both your rental contract and your guarantee contract.

Will a late payment stop me from renting my next apartment?

One late payment that was quickly fixed is unlikely to block you everywhere. Repeated late payments, or a case where the guarantor company had to pay the landlord several times, can lead to rejections from guarantor companies that share information. Paying on time from now on is the best way to rebuild your record.

Why is the guarantor company calling my friend?

Your friend is probably listed as your emergency contact. If the company cannot reach you, it may call them to find you. Your emergency contact is generally not responsible for paying your rent unless they also signed as a joint guarantor. Answer the company's calls yourself to stop this.

Can I ask to change my rent due date?

You can ask, but many landlords and guarantor companies use a fixed debit date for all tenants. If your salary date makes payment difficult, explain this politely and ask whether a different date is possible. If not, set up a manual transfer a few days after payday and keep a buffer in your account.
