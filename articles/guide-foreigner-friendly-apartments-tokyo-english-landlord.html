Why Foreigners Get Rejected for Apartments in Tokyo — And What To Do Next

Updated November 2026 · GaijinHome

You have seen a dozen listings online, emailed agents, maybe even visited a few apartments. Then the replies start coming back: "Sorry, the owner does not accept foreigners." "This property is not available." Or just silence. If you are hunting in Tokyo and getting nowhere, you are not imagining it, and you are not alone.

Tokyo has more rental stock and more foreigner-friendly agents than anywhere else in Japan, yet it is also where many foreigners face their first wall of rejections. The good news is that most of these refusals follow predictable patterns. Once you understand them, you can aim your search at landlords who will actually say yes.

What Actually Happens in Tokyo

Rejection in Tokyo usually comes in one of these forms:

  • Filtered out before viewing. The agent checks with the management company, which says the owner does not accept foreign tenants (外国籍不可). You never even see the apartment.
  • "Japanese speakers only." The listing or owner requires a tenant who can communicate in Japanese, often because the management company has no English support.
  • Screening failure. You applied, but the guarantor company or owner declined.
  • Losing to another applicant. In popular Tokyo areas, desirable apartments may receive several applications, and the owner chooses the one they see as lowest risk.
  • The agent gives up on you. Some general agents simply stop responding to foreign inquiries because they expect difficulties.

Here is what most people do not realize: a large share of these refusals happen at the owner level, before any assessment of you personally. That means changing which listings you pursue can matter more than improving your application.

Why Tokyo Landlords Reject Foreign Tenants

Many Tokyo apartments are owned by individuals

A large portion of Tokyo's smaller apartment buildings are owned by private individuals, often older owners, who hand day-to-day management to a local company. These owners can be cautious, and some still refuse foreign tenants based on past bad experiences or second-hand stories. The management company passes on that preference.

Fears about communication and rules

Owners commonly worry about garbage separation rules, noise in thin-walled wooden buildings, extra people living in the unit, and being unable to contact the tenant in an emergency. These are concerns about communication rather than nationality, which is why showing you can be reached and follow rules helps.

Leaving suddenly

Owners fear a tenant leaving Japan without notice and leaving belongings, unpaid rent, or cleanup costs behind. A short remaining visa period or a new job without track record heightens this fear.

Guarantor company screening

Most Tokyo rentals require a guarantor company (保証会社). Their criteria differ, and some are more cautious with foreign applicants who have little Japanese credit history. If this is your sticking point, see alternatives after a guarantor rejection and our guide on passing a credit check in Japan.

Competition in high-demand areas

In areas popular with young professionals, such as stations along the Yamanote and major private lines, good apartments can move quickly. Owners with several applicants will tend to pick the one they consider safest.

Common Mistakes Foreigners Make in Tokyo

  1. Using only big portal sites in English. Many listings shown in English translations are not checked for foreigner acceptance. You waste time on apartments that were never available to you.
  2. Targeting only the most popular neighborhoods. Areas like Shibuya, Ebisu and Nakameguro attract heavy competition. A short train ride further out often opens up far more owners willing to rent to you.
  3. Not asking upfront. Not asking "Is this property OK for foreign tenants?" (外国籍可ですか？) before viewing leads to repeated disappointment.
  4. Sending a bare inquiry. A one-line message with no job, visa, or income information looks risky. A short profile makes agents more willing to push your application.
  5. Insisting on a brand-new building. Newer buildings managed by large companies are sometimes easier, but older buildings run by flexible local agents can be easier too. Rule nothing out based on age alone.

Step-by-Step: What to Do Next

  1. Switch to agents who specialize in foreign tenants. These agents keep track of which owners and management companies accept foreigners, and they work with guarantor companies offering multilingual support. Ask them directly: "Please show me only properties that accept foreigners."
  2. Write a short tenant profile and send it with every inquiry. Include your job and employer, visa type and expiry, monthly income, how long you plan to stay, your Japanese level, and the fact that you have a Japanese phone and an emergency contact in Japan. Agents can forward this to owners.
  3. Widen your search area by a few stations. Look along the same train line but further from the center, or at less trendy neighborhoods next to popular ones (see the area table below). The commute difference can be ten to twenty minutes, and the number of available listings can rise significantly.
  4. Look at larger managed properties and public housing. Apartments owned by companies rather than individuals tend to apply standard screening rules. UR public housing does not discriminate by nationality and needs no guarantor; read our UR housing guide.
  5. Offer reassurance on the points owners fear. Say you will follow garbage and noise rules, give a Japanese-speaking emergency contact, and confirm your visa will be renewed (a letter from your employer helps). These small steps address the owner's real worries.
  6. Act fast when you find a match. Have your documents scanned and ready, so you can apply on the same day as a viewing. In Tokyo, a one-day delay can lose an apartment.
  7. Use a bridge option if you need a home now. Share houses and furnished apartments aimed at foreigners accept international tenants by default, and let you build a Tokyo rental record. Required Documents for Tokyo Applications

Have digital copies ready so you can apply on the spot:

  • Residence card (在留カード), front and back
  • Passport photo page
  • Certificate of employment (在職証明書) or offer letter (内定通知書) showing salary
  • Recent salary slips (給与明細) or, for freelancers, tax return copy (確定申告書の控え)
  • Tax certificate (課税証明書) if you have lived in Japan more than a year
  • Student enrollment certificate (在学証明書) and proof of funding, for students
  • Japanese mobile phone number
  • Emergency contact (緊急連絡先) in Japan, ideally Japanese-speaking
  • Bank details for rent payment, or confirmation you are opening an account
  • Short written tenant profile in English and simple Japanese

Frequently Asked Questions

Is it legal for Tokyo landlords to refuse foreigners?

Japan does not have a comprehensive anti-discrimination law that clearly covers private housing, and owner-level refusals still happen. Some courts have found refusals based solely on nationality to be unlawful in specific cases, but in practice it is usually faster to move on to a landlord who accepts you than to dispute one who does not.

Do I need to speak Japanese to rent in Tokyo?

No, but it helps. Many foreigner-focused agents and guarantor companies offer English support. If you do not speak Japanese, a Japanese-speaking emergency contact and an agent who can relay messages will reassure owners who worry about communication.

Which Tokyo neighborhoods are easiest for foreigners?

There is no official list, but areas with long-established international communities, such as parts of Shinjuku, Toshima and Minato, tend to have more owners and agents used to foreign tenants. Outer and eastern wards often have less competition. Your success depends more on the agent and building than on the neighborhood alone.

How long does it take to find an apartment in Tokyo as a foreigner?

It varies a lot. With a foreigner-friendly agent and ready documents, some people sign within a couple of weeks. With repeated rejections, it can take much longer. Using a share house or furnished apartment as a base reduces the pressure while you search.

What if I keep failing screening even with a foreigner-friendly agent?

Then the issue is probably your application rather than owner preferences: visa length, income, guarantor company type, or emergency contact. Read our guide on what to do after a rejection to pinpoint the likely cause and fix it.
