Credit Check Failed in Japan as a Foreigner? Exact Reasons and Step-by-Step Fixes

Updated November 2026 · GaijinHome

The agent calls and says, politely and vaguely, that the "screening did not pass" (審査が通りませんでした). No reason is given. You have a job, you have savings, and you have never missed a payment in your life. So what went wrong? In Japan, the "credit check" for an apartment is not one single check, and the people who make the decision almost never explain it. Once you understand who actually screened you and what they look at, you can usually find a route that works.

What Actually Happens During an Apartment Credit Check

When you apply for an apartment, your application usually goes through two layers:

  1. The guarantor company (保証会社). Nearly every rental now requires one. The guarantor company agrees to cover your rent if you do not pay, so it screens you first. This is what most people mean by the "credit check."
  2. The landlord or management company. Even if the guarantor company approves you, the owner can still decline.

Guarantor companies are not all the same. Broadly, they fall into a few groups:

TypeWhat they checkImpact on foreigners
Credit-card or consumer-finance affiliated (信販系)Can check credit bureau records at CIC and/or JICCStrictest: late phone or card payments, defaults or no history can be a problem
Companies sharing data within a rental-industry network (協会系)Rent payment history shared among member companiesPast rent arrears in Japan may show up; no Japanese history is often fine
Independent companies (独立系)Mainly their own records, income and documentsOften the most flexible for newcomers
Foreigner-focused guarantor servicesVisa, employment, Japanese contact, sometimes in EnglishDesigned for foreign residents, though fees may be higher

Which company you get is decided by the landlord or management company, not by you. That is why one application fails and the next, for a similar apartment, passes.

Why It Happens: The Real Reasons Foreigners Fail

1. A record in CIC or JICC

If you have lived in Japan for a while, check this first. Paying for a smartphone in monthly installments counts as credit. Late payments on that phone, a credit card, or a loan can be recorded in credit bureaus, and card-affiliated guarantor companies can see them. Many foreigners do not realize that a phone bill paid late while they were traveling, or a card left unpaid after a move, has become a credit record. Records of serious problems can remain for several years.

2. No credit history at all

Newcomers with no Japanese credit history do not have a bad record, but a card-affiliated company may still be cautious because it has nothing to evaluate. This is often called being a "white" applicant (スーパーホワイト) when there is no record at all.

3. Income too low for the rent

A common rule of thumb is that monthly rent should be around one-third of monthly income or less. If you are a student, on a probation period, newly employed or freelancing, guarantor companies may look closely at whether your income is stable.

4. Visa period shorter than the lease

If your residence card expires in a few months, the company may worry you will leave mid-lease. Visa type matters too: work visas tied to a stable employer often pass more easily than short student or job-seeking statuses.

5. Emergency contact and communication

Most guarantor companies require an emergency contact (緊急連絡先), and many expect someone in Japan who can speak Japanese. The company may also phone you to confirm details — in Japanese. A missed or confusing call can lead to rejection.

6. Inconsistent information

Different spellings of your name, an employer phone number that nobody answers, or an income that does not match your documents are all red flags. Guarantor companies often call your workplace to confirm employment (在籍確認).

For a broader look at rejections that are not about credit, see why apartment applications get rejected.

Common Mistakes

  1. Applying again with the same management company. If it always uses the same card-affiliated guarantor, you will likely fail again for the same reason.
  2. Not checking your own credit file. You can request your own records from CIC and JICC. Guessing is pointless when you can look.
  3. Listing an emergency contact who does not know. If the guarantor company calls your friend and they are confused, that counts against you.
  4. Ignoring the screening phone call. Calls often come from unknown numbers. Missing them repeatedly can stall or end your application.
  5. Choosing rent at the edge of your budget. Applying for a unit at half your salary almost guarantees failure, regardless of credit history.

Step-by-Step Fix

  1. Ask which guarantor company screened you. The agent may not tell you the reason, but they can usually tell you the company name: 「どちらの保証会社で審査されましたか？」 Search the name to see whether it is card-affiliated.
  2. Check your credit records. If you have used credit in Japan, request your record from CIC and JICC (disclosure is available online or by mail for a small fee). Look for late payments (異動 is the key word for a serious negative record). If there is something there, you will need to target non-card guarantor companies.
  3. Clear any unpaid balances. Pay off any overdue phone installments or card bills right away. Paying does not instantly erase a record, but ongoing arrears are worse than settled ones.
  4. Ask the agent to filter by guarantor company. Say clearly: "I would like apartments where the guarantor company is not credit-card affiliated, or a company that accepts foreign residents." 「信販系以外の保証会社の物件を探していただけますか？」 Good agents know which management companies use which guarantors.
  5. Strengthen the rest of your file. Get a certificate of employment with salary, offer to pay several months of rent in advance, show savings, or add a Japanese-speaking emergency contact who has agreed in advance. A foreigner-focused guarantor service can also help.
  6. Lower the rent target. Dropping your rent by ¥10,000–¥15,000 can change the outcome if income was the issue.
  7. Use a route that does not need a credit check. UR public housing has its own income criteria and does not use a guarantor company (see our UR guide). Share houses and monthly apartments usually skip guarantor screening — our monthly mansion guide explains the trade-offs. [PR] Oakhouse share houses do not require a guarantor.
  8. Build a clean track record. Pay your phone bill, utilities and any card on time for six to twelve months. Getting a Japanese credit card with a small limit and using it responsibly will build history for the future.
Key tip: Before applying, prepare your emergency contact. Tell them the guarantor company may call, what the company's name is, and what to say. This simple step prevents a surprising number of failed screenings.

Required Documents

  • Residence card (在留カード), front and back, with enough remaining validity
  • Passport
  • Certificate of employment (在職証明書) stating position, start date and salary
  • Tax certificate (課税証明書) or withholding slip (源泉徴収票) for income proof; new arrivals can use an employment contract or offer letter
  • Recent pay slips (給与明細)
  • Bank statement or savings balance (残高証明書) if income is new or irregular
  • Certificate of residence (住民票)
  • Credit report disclosure from CIC / JICC if you have a known issue
  • Emergency contact (緊急連絡先) details, with their agreement

City and Region Differences

Tokyo

Large management companies in Tokyo increasingly use card-affiliated guarantor companies, especially for newer buildings in central wards. This means applicants with credit problems may struggle more in popular areas. The upside is that Tokyo also has the largest number of agents and guarantor services that specialize in foreign residents. If you have a known credit issue, older buildings managed by smaller local agencies are often more flexible. For no-guarantor options in the city, see no-guarantor apartments in Tokyo.

Osaka

Osaka also relies heavily on guarantor companies, but many local agents have long experience with international residents and know which landlords are flexible. Initial cost structures differ from Tokyo, so check our Osaka guide.

Regional cities

In smaller cities, there may be fewer guarantor companies in use and fewer foreigner-focused services, but landlords may be more open to negotiation and to employer-backed contracts. If your company offers corporate housing or a corporate lease (法人契約), that can bypass the personal credit check altogether.

Frequently Asked Questions

Does Japan check my credit history from my home country?

Generally, no. Japanese guarantor companies look at Japanese records such as CIC and JICC, plus their own data and your documents. Good credit abroad does not transfer automatically, which is why newcomers often start with no record at all.

Can a late phone bill really stop me from renting an apartment?

It can, if the phone was bought on installments and the late payment was recorded. Card-affiliated guarantor companies can see these records. If you think this applies to you, request your records and look for guarantor companies that are not card-affiliated.

Will the agent tell me why I failed?

Usually not. Guarantor companies rarely share reasons. You can ask which company screened you, which helps you avoid the same one next time, and you can ask the agent whether they think income, visa period or emergency contact was the issue.

How long does a negative credit record stay in Japan?

It depends on the bureau and the type of record. Serious records such as long-term arrears can remain for several years. Check your own disclosure to see what is recorded and when it is scheduled to be removed.

Can my employer help me pass?

Yes. A strong certificate of employment helps, and some employers will sign as the contracting party (法人契約), which shifts screening to the company. Ask HR whether they support housing or corporate leases.
