Why Your Apartment Application Was Rejected in Japan: Common Reasons and Solutions

Updated November 2026 · GaijinHome

You found a place you liked, filled in the application, handed over copies of your residence card, and waited. A few days later the agent called back with a short message: "Sorry, the screening did not pass." No details, no appeal process, and often no suggestion of what to do next. If this just happened to you, you are not alone, and it is usually not personal. Rental screening in Japan is a multi-layer process, and a "no" at any layer ends the application. The good news is that most rejections have a fixable cause once you know which layer said no.

What Actually Happens When Your Application Is Rejected

In Japan, an apartment application (入居申込書, nyūkyo mōshikomisho) is not reviewed by one person. It typically passes through three checkpoints:

  1. The real estate agent (仲介会社) forwards your application. Some agents pre-filter applicants they think will fail, which is why you sometimes hear "this property is not available for foreigners" before you even apply.
  2. The guarantor company (保証会社) runs its own screening. Most rentals in major cities now require one. It checks your identity, income, employment, visa status and sometimes credit history, and decides whether it will cover you if you stop paying rent.
  3. The landlord or management company (管理会社) makes the final call. Even if the guarantor company approves you, the landlord can still decline.

When the agent says "the screening did not pass" (審査に通りませんでした), they usually know which of these layers rejected you. They may not volunteer it, but you can ask politely, and many agents will give you at least a hint.

Why It Happens: The Real Reasons Behind a Rejection

The guarantor company could not verify or approve you

Guarantor companies are the most common point of failure. They want to see stable income relative to rent (a common rule of thumb is that rent should be no more than about one third of your monthly take-home pay), a working Japanese phone number, and an employer they can call. Some guarantor companies are linked to credit information databases. If you have ever missed phone installment payments or credit card bills in Japan, that can show up. If you are brand new to Japan, the problem is the opposite: there is no record at all, and some companies treat "no history" as risky.

Your visa period is shorter than the lease

Standard leases in Japan run for two years. If your residence card shows only six months or a year remaining, the landlord or guarantor company may worry you will leave early or lose your status. This is one of the most common hidden reasons for foreigners, especially for students near graduation and people on their first one-year work visa.

The landlord simply does not accept foreign tenants

This is frustrating, but it still exists. Landlords often cite past experiences with noise, garbage sorting, subletting, or tenants leaving the country without notice. It is not something you can argue your way out of, and trying to usually wastes time. The faster fix is to target properties that are explicitly foreigner-friendly.

No reachable emergency contact

Many applications require an emergency contact (緊急連絡先) who lives in Japan and can speak Japanese. A family member overseas is often not accepted. If you left this blank or listed someone abroad, it may have stopped your application.

Incomplete or inconsistent information

Small inconsistencies matter: your name spelled differently on the application and residence card, an employer phone number that goes to voicemail, or an income figure that does not match your documents. Screeners rarely call to clarify. They just decline.

Your job type or employment status

Freelancers, people on probation at a new job, those with only part-time income, and people whose company is not yet registered in Japan often face stricter screening. Language school students without a sponsor's financial proof face a similar wall.

Common Mistakes That Lead to Repeat Rejections

  • Applying to the same type of property again. If a general rental with a strict guarantor company rejected you, applying to a similar one with the same company will likely give the same result.
  • Not asking why. Many people accept the rejection silently. A polite question to the agent can tell you whether it was the guarantor, the landlord, or your documents.
  • Overstretching on rent. Choosing a place at the top of your budget makes the income ratio fail. Dropping rent by even 10,000–15,000 yen can change the outcome.
  • Using an agent with no experience handling foreign tenants. Agents who regularly work with foreigners know which landlords and guarantor companies accept them, and they pre-match you accordingly.
  • Leaving the emergency contact blank. This alone can stop an otherwise strong application.

Step-by-Step Fix: How to Reapply and Get Approved

  1. Ask the agent which stage failed. Say something like: "Could you tell me whether the guarantor company or the landlord declined? I want to prepare better for the next application." In Japanese: 「保証会社の審査でしょうか、それとも大家さんの判断でしょうか？」 Even a vague answer helps you choose your next step.
  2. If the guarantor company failed, ask to try a different one. Some properties accept multiple guarantor companies, and screening criteria differ. Ask: "Is there another guarantor company option for this property?" Read our guide on how to pass a credit check in Japan as a foreigner for what these companies look at.
  3. If the landlord declined, move on quickly. Ask the agent to search specifically for properties marked as foreigner-OK (外国人可). Do not spend energy trying to persuade that landlord.
  4. Fix the visa-length problem. If your status expires soon, apply for renewal as early as immigration allows (generally from about three months before expiry) and bring proof that the renewal is in progress. A letter from your employer stating your contract will continue also helps.
  5. Strengthen your income evidence. Bring a certificate of employment, recent pay slips, and, if you are new, your employment contract showing salary. If income is low, a bank balance statement showing savings worth several months of rent can reassure some screeners.
  6. Arrange an emergency contact in Japan. A Japanese-speaking colleague, your company's HR department, or a friend usually works. Ask them first, because the guarantor company may call them.
  7. Consider routes that skip traditional screening. UR public housing requires no guarantor and no key money; see our guide on how to apply for UR housing. Share houses and serviced apartments usually have much lighter screening. For example, [PR] Oakhouse share houses do not require a Japanese guarantor, which can bridge the gap while you build a rental record.
  8. Reapply with a lower rent target. A slightly smaller or older place, or one a few stations further out, often passes where a newer central unit did not.
Key tip: One rejection does not go on a shared "blacklist" across all companies. Each guarantor company runs its own screening. Changing the property and guarantor company is often enough to get a different result.

Required Documents for a Stronger Application

  • Residence card (在留カード), front and back copy
  • Passport (パスポート), photo page and visa page
  • Certificate of employment (在職証明書) or employment contract (雇用契約書) if you just started
  • Recent pay slips (給与明細), usually the last 2–3 months
  • Income or tax certificate (課税証明書 or 所得証明書) from your city office, if you have been in Japan for over a year
  • Withholding tax slip (源泉徴収票) from your employer
  • Student ID (学生証) and certificate of enrollment (在学証明書) for students
  • Bank balance certificate (残高証明書) if income is low or you are self-employed
  • Emergency contact name, address and phone number in Japan (緊急連絡先)
  • Japanese mobile phone number that answers calls

City and Region Differences

Tokyo has the largest supply of foreigner-friendly rentals and the most agents who work in English, but competition is also highest. Popular units in central wards can get multiple applications within days, and landlords pick the "safest" applicant. Initial costs are high, often including deposit, key money and agent fees. See our breakdown of hidden move-in costs.

Osaka and the Kansai region are often seen as somewhat more flexible on foreign tenants, though this varies by landlord. Contracts may use different deposit conventions such as non-refundable deductions (敷引き). We cover the city in detail in our Osaka guide.

Regional cities like Fukuoka, Nagoya and Sapporo tend to have lower rents, which makes the income ratio easier to meet. On the other hand, there are fewer English-speaking agents, so preparing documents in advance and having a Japanese-speaking helper matters more.

In smaller towns, landlords may manage properties directly without a guarantor company. This can work in your favor if you meet them in person and show you are reliable, but it can also mean the decision is based on personal impressions.

Frequently Asked Questions

Will the agent tell me the exact reason for my rejection?

Usually not in full detail, because guarantor companies do not share their internal criteria. However, most agents can tell you whether it was the guarantor company or the landlord who declined, and sometimes whether income or visa length was the concern. Ask politely and frame it as wanting to prepare a better application.

Does one rejection affect my future applications?

A rejection by itself is generally not shared between unrelated companies. What can follow you is actual payment history, such as late or missed payments recorded with credit information agencies. If you have none of those, a fresh application with a different property and guarantor company starts from zero.

Can I apply to several apartments at the same time?

Agents usually process one application per property at a time, and applying to many at once can create awkward cancellations. A better approach is to ask the agent to check in advance whether the landlord accepts foreigners and which guarantor company is used, then apply to the strongest match first.

Is it illegal for a landlord to reject me for being a foreigner?

Japan does not have a broad anti-discrimination law that directly prohibits this in private rentals, and such refusals still happen. Arguing rarely changes the outcome. Your time is better spent on foreigner-friendly properties, UR housing, or share houses. See our page on what to do after being rejected.

I have no guarantor at all. Can I still rent?

Yes. Most landlords now rely on guarantor companies rather than personal guarantors, so you do not need a Japanese person to sign for you in most cases. If guarantor companies also decline you, look at UR housing, share houses or monthly apartments. Our no-guarantor guide walks through the options.
