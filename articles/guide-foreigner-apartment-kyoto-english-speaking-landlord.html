Apartment Rejected in Kyoto as a Foreigner? Finding English-Speaking Landlords Who Say Yes

Updated November 2026 · GaijinHome

Kyoto looks international from the outside. Millions of tourists, famous universities, temples everywhere. So it can be a shock when you try to rent a normal apartment and find that local agents seem far less open than in Tokyo or Osaka. Inquiries go unanswered, applications stall, and the phrase "the owner is not comfortable" comes up again and again. Kyoto is genuinely a harder rental market for foreigners in some ways, but it also has pockets where landlords actively want international tenants. The trick is knowing where those pockets are.

What Actually Happens in Kyoto

Foreign renters in Kyoto commonly describe a pattern like this:

  • Listings that look perfect online turn out to be "not available to foreigners" once you contact the agent.
  • Small local agencies near the station reply only in Japanese, or not at all.
  • Landlords ask for a Japanese emergency contact and sometimes a Japanese individual guarantor, which is less common in big cities now.
  • Older traditional properties (町家, machiya) and family-owned buildings have owners who want to meet you, or who rely completely on the agent's judgment about whether you "fit."

None of this means Kyoto is closed. It means the market is more personal, more local, and more dependent on introductions.

Why Kyoto Is Different

Many small, individual landlords

Compared to central Tokyo, more of Kyoto's rental stock is owned by individuals and families rather than large corporations. Corporate landlords tend to follow standardized rules that already account for foreign tenants. Individual owners decide case by case, and many have never rented to a non-Japanese tenant before. Their default is caution.

Neighborhood relationships matter

Kyoto neighborhoods often have active residents' associations (町内会, chōnaikai), strict garbage rules, and long-time residents who notice newcomers. Landlords worry about complaints from neighbors more than about rent. Garbage separation mistakes and noise, especially in wooden buildings with thin walls, are the classic fears.

Tourism has changed attitudes

Kyoto has dealt with heavy tourism and, in some areas, problems linked to unlicensed short-term rentals. Some landlords now include strict clauses banning subletting and are wary of any applicant they suspect might not live in the unit themselves. A clear explanation of your job or studies and how long you will stay helps separate you from that worry.

Guarantor company and emergency contact requirements

Like elsewhere in Japan, most Kyoto leases use a guarantor company (保証会社). Some local landlords also require a Japanese-speaking emergency contact. If you have only just arrived, you may not have one, and that alone can stop an application.

The student market dominates some areas

With many universities, a large share of small apartments around Sakyo, Kita, and Kamigyo wards are aimed at students. Landlords in those areas are used to young tenants and international students, which is an opportunity if you know to look there.

Key tip: In Kyoto, an introduction beats a cold inquiry every time. A referral from your university, employer, a coworker's agent, or even a previous foreign tenant of the same landlord often turns an automatic "no" into a meeting.

Common Mistakes Foreigners Make in Kyoto

  1. Searching only in the tourist core. Areas around Gion, Higashiyama, and central Shijo are popular but also have the most cautious owners and the highest rents. You compete with everyone and face the strictest landlords.
  2. Using Tokyo-style national portals only. Many Kyoto landlords list through small local agencies that never respond to English inquiries from big portals. You need local offices and foreigner-friendly agencies.
  3. Underestimating winter and old buildings. Kyoto is known for cold winters, and older wooden apartments can be poorly insulated. People sign quickly, then want to move again in a few months, which is costly and does not help your rental history.
  4. Ignoring building rules during the viewing. Asking about garbage days and quiet hours during the viewing actually reassures landlords. Silence on these topics can be read as indifference.
  5. Assuming machiya rentals are easy. Traditional townhouse rentals are attractive but often managed by owners with very particular conditions. They can be wonderful, but they are not the easiest first step.

Step-by-Step Fix: Getting a Yes in Kyoto

  1. Start with your institution. If you work at a university, research institute, or a company with international staff, ask HR or the international office for a list of agents or landlords they have used. Kyoto's universities have long histories of hosting foreign researchers and students, and many keep relationships with agencies that know which owners accept foreigners.
  2. Focus on foreigner-friendly neighborhoods. Look first in Sakyo-ku (around Demachiyanagi, Ichijoji, and Kitashirakawa), Kita-ku, and parts of Nakagyo-ku west of the main shopping streets. These areas have many academic tenants and landlords who are used to non-Japanese renters.
  3. Ask agents the filtering question directly. In Japanese: "Gaikokujin no nyūkyo jisseki ga aru ōnā-san no bukken wa arimasu ka?" (外国人の入居実績があるオーナーさんの物件はありますか？ "Do you have properties from owners who have had foreign tenants before?"). Owners who already said yes once are likely to say yes again.
  4. Write a short self-introduction for the landlord. One paragraph in simple Japanese, ideally checked by a native speaker: who you are, where you work or study, how long you plan to stay, that you will follow garbage and noise rules, and who your emergency contact is. Ask the agent to pass it on with your application. In Kyoto especially, this personal touch can matter.
  5. Line up a Japanese-speaking emergency contact. A coworker, supervisor, professor, or friend. Explain that an emergency contact (緊急連絡先) is not a financial guarantor, which makes people much more willing to agree.
  6. Look at larger management companies and UR. Corporate-managed buildings and UR housing apply standardized rules instead of personal preferences. Kyoto has UR estates in several areas, and they accept foreign residents who meet the requirements; see our UR guide.
  7. Use a short-term base while you search. A monthly apartment or share house gives you an address and time to visit local agents in person, which works far better in Kyoto than email. Our monthly mansion guide explains how they work. [PR] Oakhouse share houses are another no-guarantor option in the Kansai area worth checking for availability.
  8. Consider Osaka-side commuting. If Kyoto keeps saying no, cities along the Hankyu, Keihan, and JR lines toward Osaka, such as Takatsuki or Ibaraki, can be easier and cheaper, with a commute into central Kyoto that many people find manageable. Our Osaka guide covers that side.

Documents to Prepare

  • Residence card (在留カード), front and back
  • Passport
  • Certificate of employment (在職証明書) or letter of appointment from your university or research institute
  • Certificate of enrollment (在学証明書) if you are a student
  • Recent payslips (給与明細) or employment contract with salary
  • Tax certificate (課税証明書) if you have been in Japan more than a year
  • Certificate of residence (住民票) if already registered
  • Emergency contact details (緊急連絡先) for a Japanese-speaking person who has agreed
  • Short self-introduction letter (自己紹介文) in simple Japanese

Kyoto Neighborhoods, Rents, and Local Quirks

Rents in Kyoto are generally lower than central Tokyo but can be close to Osaka levels in popular central areas. The figures below are rough ranges for orientation only; actual rents depend heavily on building age, size, and distance to stations.

AreaWho it suitsRough feel
Sakyo-ku (Demachiyanagi, Ichijoji)Students, researchers, academicsMany small, older studios; landlords used to international tenants; cheaper end of the market

Minami-ku / around Kyoto StationCommuters to OsakaGood transport links, more modern buildings

Local quirks worth knowing: Kyoto's garbage rules and collection days differ by ward and are enforced seriously by neighbors. Many old buildings have no or limited insulation, so ask about heating and check whether air conditioners are included. Bicycles are a major way of getting around, so check whether bicycle parking (駐輪場) is available and whether it has a fee. Some older buildings also restrict musical instruments or overnight guests.

Frequently Asked Questions

Are there English-speaking real estate agents in Kyoto?

Yes, though fewer than in Tokyo. A handful of agencies focus on international clients, and some agencies near major universities have staff who can handle basic English. Your employer or university can usually point you to the ones they already use, which is the fastest way to find them.

Is Kyoto harder for foreigners to rent in than Osaka?

Many foreign residents report that it is, mainly because Kyoto has more small individual landlords and tighter neighborhood communities. Osaka has a larger, more commercial rental market and long experience with foreign residents. That said, Kyoto's university areas can be quite welcoming.

Do I need a Japanese guarantor to rent in Kyoto?

Usually not. Most leases rely on a guarantor company, though some individual landlords still prefer a personal guarantor. If an owner insists, ask the agent for another property. Our guide to renting without a guarantor explains the alternatives.

Can I rent a traditional machiya as a long-term home?

It is possible, but machiya rentals are limited and often managed by owners with specific conditions about use and maintenance. Expect higher heating costs and more upkeep. Many people rent a standard apartment first and look for a machiya once they know the city and have local contacts.

What should I do if I keep getting rejected in Kyoto?

Switch from cold inquiries to referrals, focus on university-area neighborhoods, and consider UR housing or a short-term base while you search in person. If rejections continue, our general guide on what to do after an apartment rejection walks through the other common causes.
