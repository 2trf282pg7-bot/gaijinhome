Billed for Both Shakai Hoken and Kokumin Hoken? What to Do When Japan's Health Insurance Confuses You

Updated November 2026 · GaijinHome

You started a new job, your payslip shows health insurance and pension deductions, and then a bill from the ward office arrives asking you to pay National Health Insurance as well. Or the opposite happens: you quit, assume you're still covered, and months later receive a large retroactive bill. Both situations are extremely common among foreigners in Japan, and both come from the same root problem: Japan runs two separate public health insurance systems, and moving between them is your responsibility, not something that happens automatically.

This article explains the difference between shakai hoken (社会保険, employee insurance) and kokumin hoken (国民健康保険, National Health Insurance), why the confusion happens, and exactly what to do if you are being double-billed, under-covered, or unsure which system you belong in.

What actually happens

Most foreigners run into this problem at a transition point: arriving in Japan, starting a job, changing jobs, quitting, or going freelance. Typical scenarios look like this:

  • Double billing: You joined National Health Insurance when you registered your address, then got a full-time job with shakai hoken. Your employer deducts premiums from your salary, but the ward office keeps sending NHI payment slips because nobody told them you switched.
  • Surprise back-payment: You left a job, your employee insurance ended the day after your last working day, and you didn't join NHI. When you finally visit the ward office, they enroll you retroactively from the date your old coverage ended and charge premiums for all those months.
  • Employer says "you're not eligible": You work part-time or on a contract, and your employer puts you on nothing, telling you to "join kokumin hoken yourself" even though your hours may qualify you for shakai hoken.
  • Pension confusion: You see kokumin nenkin (国民年金, National Pension) bills arriving separately and don't know if they overlap with the kosei nenkin (厚生年金, Employees' Pension) deducted from your pay.

On top of the money, gaps or unpaid premiums can cause trouble later. Unpaid social insurance can come up when you renew your visa, apply for permanent residency, or need to show proof of insurance and stable employment during an apartment application.

Why it happens

Two systems, two different offices

Shakai hoken is run through your employer. It bundles employee health insurance (usually Kyokai Kenpo or a company health insurance association) with the Employees' Pension. Premiums are split roughly 50/50 between you and the company and are deducted from your salary.

Kokumin hoken is run by your city, town, or ward office. It covers everyone living in Japan who is not on an employee plan: freelancers, the self-employed, many students, unemployed people, and some part-timers. You pay the full premium yourself, and the amount is calculated from your previous year's income and household. National Pension is billed separately by the Japan Pension Service.

These two systems do not talk to each other in real time. Your employer notifies the pension office and health insurer, but your ward office is generally not told. That's why the switch has to be reported by you.

You can only be in one system at a time

Japanese law requires everyone registered as a resident (usually anyone staying more than three months) to be covered by public health insurance. If you qualify for employee insurance, that takes priority, and you must leave NHI. If you lose employee insurance, you must join NHI (or another option, explained below). In general you're expected to report either change to the ward office within 14 days.

Eligibility rules for part-timers are complicated

Full-time employees of corporations are covered by shakai hoken. Part-time workers may also be covered if they work enough hours. The rules have been expanding in recent years to include more part-timers at larger employers, roughly those working 20 or more hours a week and earning above a monthly wage threshold. Students are often excluded from the part-time expansion. Because the thresholds have changed several times, many employers (and employees) are confused about who qualifies.

NHI premiums are based on last year's income

If you had a high salary last year and then lost your job, your NHI premium can look shockingly high because it's calculated on the previous year's income. Newcomers with no Japanese income history usually pay a low or minimum premium in their first year, which then jumps once Japanese income is on record.

Language and paperwork

The key documents, such as the certificate of loss of coverage, are in Japanese, the forms at ward offices are in Japanese, and employers don't always explain what you need to do when you leave. Many foreigners simply don't know the switch is their job.

Common mistakes

  1. Not withdrawing from NHI after starting a job. Your employer enrolling you in shakai hoken does not cancel your NHI. You'll keep receiving bills until you report the change. Withdrawing usually refunds or cancels premiums for the overlap period, but only once you visit and show proof.
  2. Assuming you're covered after quitting. Employee coverage ends the day after you leave the company. There is no automatic grace period. If you don't join NHI, you're technically uninsured, but you'll still be billed for the gap once you enroll.
  3. Ignoring pension bills. National Pension is separate from NHI. Many people pay their health insurance but ignore the yellow-and-blue pension slips, which leads to unpaid pension records.
  4. Accepting "you're not eligible" without checking. Some employers avoid enrolling eligible staff to save costs. If you work close to full-time hours, ask for the specific reason in writing.
  5. Throwing away the loss-of-coverage certificate. The document your old employer sends after you leave is exactly what the ward office needs. Without it, enrolling in NHI takes longer.

Step-by-step fix

  1. Figure out which system you should be in right now. Ask yourself: Am I currently employed by a company? Am I full-time, or do I work around 20+ hours a week? If yes, ask your HR department directly: "Am I enrolled in shakai hoken?" (社会保険に加入していますか? / shakai hoken ni kanyū shite imasu ka?). Check your payslip for lines labeled 健康保険 (health insurance) and 厚生年金 (employees' pension).
  2. If you are double-billed (joined shakai hoken but still getting NHI bills): Go to your ward or city office's National Health Insurance counter. Say: "I joined my company's insurance, so I want to withdraw from National Health Insurance" (会社の保険に入ったので、国民健康保険をやめたいです / kaisha no hoken ni haitta node, kokumin kenkō hoken o yametai desu). Bring proof of your new coverage. Since paper insurance cards were phased out in December 2024, this is usually your My Number insurance card registration or a certificate of eligibility (資格確認書) or a document issued by your employer's insurer. Ask the counter what they accept. Any NHI premiums you already paid for months covered by shakai hoken should be refunded or offset.
  3. If you just left a job: Ask your former employer for the certificate of loss of coverage (健康保険資格喪失証明書 / kenkō hoken shikaku sōshitsu shōmeisho). Take it to the ward office within 14 days and enroll in NHI and National Pension. Don't wait for a new job "just in case" — you'll be billed retroactively anyway, and you'd be paying full medical costs in the meantime.
  4. Compare your options after leaving a job. NHI isn't the only choice. You may be able to:
    • Continue your employee plan voluntarily (任意継続 / nin'i keizoku) for up to two years, if you had been insured for at least two months. You pay the full premium (both halves), but it is sometimes cheaper than NHI for people with high previous income. The application deadline is short, typically 20 days after leaving.
    • Become a dependent (扶養 / fuyō) on a spouse's or family member's employee insurance, if your expected income is below the dependent threshold. Dependents generally pay no extra premium.
    • Join NHI, and if you lost your job involuntarily (layoff, contract not renewed by the employer), ask about the premium reduction for involuntary job loss (非自発的失業者の軽減). Bring the unemployment insurance documents from Hello Work.
  5. If your employer won't enroll you: Ask HR for the reason in writing. If you believe you qualify (full-time, or a part-timer meeting the thresholds at a covered employer), you can consult your local pension office (年金事務所 / nenkin jimusho), which handles employee insurance enrollment issues. Keep your contract, payslips, and timesheets as evidence of your hours.
  6. If you received a large back-payment bill: Don't ignore it. Visit the NHI counter and ask about paying in installments (分割納付 / bunkatsu nōfu) or reductions if your income dropped. Ward offices usually prefer a payment plan to no payment at all. For pension, ask about exemption or deferment (免除・猶予) if you can't afford it.
  7. Bring help if language is the barrier. Many large city offices offer interpretation by phone or tablet, or have days with English-speaking staff. Call ahead and ask. If you're planning to deal with Japanese paperwork long term, a few focused lessons on office vocabulary can save a lot of stress: [PR] italki offers one-on-one Japanese lessons with tutors who can role-play a ward office visit with you.
  8. Keep your records together. Payment receipts, withdrawal confirmations, and certificates matter when you renew your visa or apply for permanent residency. For more on how insurance and tax records affect your status, see our visa guide and our article on visa extension rejections.
Important: Joining one system never cancels the other automatically. Every time you start or stop employee insurance, assume you need to visit the ward office within 14 days. This single habit prevents almost all double bills and back-payment surprises.

Required documents

To withdraw from NHI after joining shakai hoken:

  • Residence card (在留カード / zairyū kādo)
  • Proof of your new employee insurance: My Number insurance card registration, certificate of eligibility (資格確認書), or a certificate from your employer's insurer
  • Your NHI certificate or the NHI documents you received from the ward office
  • My Number card or notification (マイナンバーカード / mai nambā kādo)
  • Bank account details if a refund is due

To join NHI after leaving a job:

  • Residence card (在留カード)
  • Certificate of loss of employee insurance coverage (健康保険資格喪失証明書)
  • My Number card or notification (マイナンバーカード)
  • Pension handbook or Basic Pension Number notice (基礎年金番号通知書), for National Pension enrollment
  • Hello Work unemployment documents (雇用保険受給資格者証), if you're asking for the involuntary job-loss reduction

Requirements vary slightly by municipality, so call ahead or check your city's website. For a full walkthrough of joining NHI from scratch, see what to do when joining National Health Insurance as a foreigner.

City / region differences

The national rules are the same everywhere, but the details that affect your wallet are local:

  • NHI premiums vary by municipality. Each city or ward sets its own rates, so two people with the same income can pay noticeably different amounts in, for example, a Tokyo ward versus Osaka, Yokohama, or Fukuoka. Many cities offer an online premium simulator in Japanese.
  • Employee health insurance rates vary by prefecture. Kyokai Kenpo, the insurer for many small and mid-sized companies, sets slightly different rates for each prefecture. Large companies often have their own health insurance association with different rates.
  • Foreign-language support differs. Major cities like Tokyo's central wards, Osaka, Yokohama, and Nagoya are more likely to have multilingual guides and interpretation services at ward offices. Smaller towns may only offer Japanese forms, so bringing a Japanese-speaking friend or using a translation app is wise.
  • Moving cities restarts the paperwork. If you're on NHI and move to a new municipality, you withdraw at the old office when you move out and enroll at the new one when you register your new address. If you're moving because of a new apartment, budget for this alongside your other hidden moving costs.

Frequently Asked Questions

Can I choose NHI instead of shakai hoken because it's cheaper?

No. If you meet the conditions for employee insurance, enrollment is mandatory, and you can't opt out to join NHI. Shakai hoken also includes the Employees' Pension, and the employer pays about half your premiums, so it is often better value than it looks on your payslip.

I got NHI bills for months when I was already on my company's insurance. Will I get the money back?

Usually yes. Once you show proof of your employee insurance at the ward office and withdraw from NHI, premiums for the overlapping months are typically refunded or applied to other amounts you owe. The office will tell you the exact amount and timing.

Why is my NHI bill so high after I quit my job?

NHI premiums are calculated from your previous year's income. If you earned a good salary last year, your premium will reflect that even if you have no income now. Ask about the involuntary job-loss reduction, voluntary continuation of your old employee plan, or becoming a dependent on a family member's insurance.

Do unpaid insurance or pension premiums affect my apartment search?

Landlords and guarantor companies don't usually check your insurance payment history directly, but they often ask for your insurance card or proof of employment, and unpaid premiums can cause problems with visa renewals and permanent residency, which in turn affect your rental options. Staying current keeps your status, and your housing applications, stable. If you've already been turned down, see our guide on apartment rejections.

I'm a freelancer with a company contract. Which system am I in?

If you are an independent contractor rather than an employee, you're generally on NHI and National Pension. If you run your own corporation and pay yourself a salary as a director, the company is usually required to enroll you in shakai hoken. When in doubt, ask your ward office or local pension office with your contract in hand.
