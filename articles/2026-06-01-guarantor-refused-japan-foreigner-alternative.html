Guarantor Rejected in Japan: Step-by-Step Alternatives for Foreigners

Updated November 2026 · GaijinHome

You found an apartment, filled in the forms, and waited. Then the agent called back: the guarantor company said no. Or the landlord refused to accept your guarantor at all. Nobody explained why, and you have no idea whether to try again or give up on the place.

This is one of the most common dead ends foreigners hit when renting in Japan, and it is very fixable. A guarantor rejection usually says more about how one particular screening system works than about you as a tenant. This guide explains what is really going on and gives you a concrete order of alternatives to try.

What Actually Happens When Your Guarantor Is Rejected

Most rentals in Japan today require a guarantor company (保証会社, hoshō gaisha) rather than, or in addition to, a personal guarantor (連帯保証人, rentai hoshōnin). When you apply, the real estate agent sends your application to the guarantor company the landlord or management company uses. That company screens you and decides whether it will cover your rent if you stop paying.

A rejection can come in a few different forms:

  • The guarantor company declines you outright. The agent says "審査に通りませんでした" (you did not pass screening), usually with no reason given.
  • The company approves you with conditions. For example, they want an additional personal guarantor, a higher initial fee, or a Japanese emergency contact.
  • The landlord refuses your personal guarantor. Your friend or relative offered to sign, but they live overseas, are not Japanese, are retired, or do not earn enough, so the landlord will not accept them.
  • Your employer refuses to act as guarantor. Some companies have a policy of never signing as guarantor for staff.

In almost every case, the agent will not be told the precise reason, and guarantor companies generally do not disclose their criteria. That is frustrating, but it also means you should not assume the rejection is final for every property.

Why Guarantor Rejections Happen to Foreigners

Understanding the structure helps you pick the right alternative instead of reapplying blindly.

Different guarantor companies use different data

Guarantor companies are often grouped into three rough types in the Japanese rental industry:

TypeWhat they typically checkWhy it matters for you
Credit-card affiliated (信販系)Your record with Japanese credit bureaus such as CICMissed phone installment payments or card debts can sink you; having no history at all can also be a weak point
Industry association members (協会系 / LICC系)Shared rent-arrears records among member companiesPast late rent with a member company can show up
Independent (独立系)Their own internal criteriaOften more flexible; may focus on income, job, and visa

The key insight: being rejected by one type does not tell you much about the others. Someone with a problem in credit-bureau records can often pass an independent company.

Visa period shorter than the lease

A standard lease runs two years. If your residence card shows six months or a year remaining, some screeners see a risk that you leave Japan mid-contract. This is very common for people on their first work visa or a student visa.

Weak or missing emergency contact

Many guarantor companies require an emergency contact (緊急連絡先) living in Japan who can speak Japanese. If you listed a family member abroad, or a friend who could not be reached when the company called to verify, the application can stall or fail.

Income, job length and employment type

A common rule of thumb in the industry is that rent should be roughly one-third of monthly income or less. New arrivals who just started a job, freelancers, and people on probation periods are viewed as higher risk, even if they have savings.

Phone and payment details

Screeners call your phone number. A foreign number, a prepaid SIM that cannot receive calls, or not answering a call from an unknown Japanese number can all hurt you.

Common Mistakes After a Guarantor Rejection

  1. Reapplying to the next apartment from the same management company. If the same management company uses the same guarantor company, you will likely get the same answer. Ask which guarantor company each property uses before you apply.
  2. Applying to many places at once. Multiple simultaneous applications to the same guarantor company can look bad. Apply one or two at a time and change variables between attempts.
  3. Not asking the agent what can be adjusted. Agents frequently know whether a higher deposit, an extra guarantor, or a different emergency contact would change the result. Many tenants never ask.
  4. Giving up on personal guarantors entirely. Some properties still accept a personal guarantor instead of a company, and some accept both as a combination when one alone is not enough.
  5. Ignoring your phone during screening. Missing the verification call is a silent and avoidable cause of rejection.

Step-by-Step Fix: What to Do After a Guarantor Rejection

  1. Ask the agent two specific questions. Say: "Which guarantor company did they use?" (どこの保証会社ですか？) and "Is there anything I can add to pass, such as another guarantor or a larger deposit?" You may not get a reason, but you will learn what can be changed.
  2. Fix the easy weak points first. Get a Japanese mobile number that can receive calls, line up a Japan-based emergency contact who agrees to answer an unknown number, and gather proof of income and savings. Then tell the agent you have updated these details.
  3. Request a property that uses a different type of guarantor company. If you were rejected by a credit-card affiliated company, ask the agent to filter for listings using an independent company. Some agents that specialize in foreign tenants work regularly with guarantor companies offering multilingual support.
  4. Offer a stronger application package. Options that sometimes change the outcome include paying several months' rent in advance, offering a higher deposit, or adding a personal guarantor on top of the guarantor company. Not every landlord accepts these, but asking costs nothing.
  5. Ask your employer about a corporate contract (法人契約). Even if your company will not sign as guarantor, it may be willing to be the contracting party, with rent deducted from salary. Corporate leases are often screened on the company's strength rather than yours.
  6. Look at UR housing. Public UR apartments do not require a guarantor, key money, or agent fees. They do have income or savings requirements and their own procedures. Read our UR housing application guide to see if you qualify.
  7. Use a no-guarantor housing type as a bridge. Share houses and some furnished apartment operators handle screening themselves and do not require a guarantor company. This lets you build a Japanese address, phone record and payment history, which strengthens your next standard application. [PR] For example, Oakhouse share houses can be rented without a guarantor.
  8. Reapply after your situation changes. A visa renewal with a longer period, passing probation, or six months of salary slips can turn a rejection into an approval. Note the date and try again when one of these changes.
Key tip: Treat each application as an experiment. Change one variable each time (guarantor company type, emergency contact, deposit amount, or housing type) so you learn what is actually blocking you instead of repeating the same rejection.

Required Documents for a Stronger Guarantor Application

Exact requirements vary by guarantor company, but having these ready avoids delays and shows you are organized:

  • Residence card (在留カード), front and back
  • Passport, including the page with your landing permission
  • Certificate of residence (住民票, jūminhyō) if requested, issued by your city or ward office
  • Certificate of employment (在職証明書) or employment contract showing salary and start date
  • Recent salary slips (給与明細), typically the last 2–3 months
  • Tax certificate (課税証明書) or income certificate (所得証明書) if you have been in Japan long enough to have one
  • Bank balance proof (残高証明書) or a passbook copy, useful if your income is new or irregular
  • Student ID and enrollment certificate (在学証明書) for students
  • Japanese mobile number that can receive calls
  • Emergency contact (緊急連絡先) details: name, address, phone and relationship, ideally someone living in Japan
  • Personal guarantor's ID and income proof, if you are adding one

City and Region Differences

Tokyo

Tokyo has the largest number of agents and guarantor companies used to foreign tenants, which means more chances to find a company that will approve you. The flip side is competition: popular listings may go to the first applicant who passes screening, so a slow or incomplete application loses out. Our no-guarantor apartments in Tokyo guide covers options in the capital.

Osaka and Kansai

Osaka and the wider Kansai area historically used different deposit customs, such as a non-refundable portion of the deposit (敷引き, shikibiki), although practices have shifted toward the national standard. Guarantor company use is just as widespread as in Tokyo. Some smaller, long-established landlords may be more open to a personal guarantor. See our Osaka renting guide for local details.

Regional cities

In cities such as Fukuoka, Nagoya and Sapporo, and in smaller towns, there are fewer agents with English support but sometimes more room to negotiate directly with a landlord or local agent. Employer-arranged housing and corporate contracts are especially common for people working in factories, schools and regional offices.

Frequently Asked Questions

Can I find out exactly why the guarantor company rejected me?

Usually not. Guarantor companies rarely disclose their reasons, even to the agent. What you can do is ask which company was used and what conditions might help, then adjust your application based on the most likely weak points such as visa length, emergency contact, or income documentation.

Does a guarantor rejection go on my record?

A rejection itself is generally not shared as a negative credit event in the way a missed payment is. However, a guarantor company may keep its own internal record of your application, so reapplying to the same company shortly afterwards is often pointless. Switching to a different company or company type is a better approach.

Can a foreign friend be my personal guarantor?

Sometimes. Landlords usually want a personal guarantor who lives in Japan, has stable income, and can be contacted easily. A foreign resident with permanent residency and a steady job may be accepted, while someone living overseas is rarely accepted. Ask the agent before involving your friend.

Is paying a larger deposit a guaranteed way to get approved?

No. Offering more upfront money can persuade some landlords, but if the property requires a guarantor company, the company's decision often cannot be overridden by a bigger deposit. It is worth offering, but have a backup plan such as a different property or a no-guarantor housing type. See our hidden costs guide to budget for this.

How long should I wait before reapplying?

There is no fixed rule. Rather than waiting a set time, reapply when something meaningful changes: you get a longer visa, pass probation, have several months of salary slips, or switch to a property using a different guarantor company. Changing the inputs matters more than the calendar.
