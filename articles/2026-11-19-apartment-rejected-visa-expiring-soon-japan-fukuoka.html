Apartment Rejected Because Your Visa Expires Soon in Fukuoka? What to Do Next

Updated November 2026 · GaijinHome

You found a place in Fukuoka you actually like. Maybe it is a compact 1K near Tenjin, or a slightly bigger room a few stops from Hakata. You filled in the application, handed over a copy of your residence card, and waited. Then the agent called back with a polite but firm answer: the application did not pass because your period of stay ends soon.

This is one of the most frustrating rejections a foreign renter can get, because it often has nothing to do with your income, your job, or how you behave as a tenant. You may have every intention of renewing your visa, and you may be fully eligible to do so. But on paper, your residence card says your right to stay in Japan ends in a few months, and a standard lease is usually two years long. That mismatch is what the screening process reacts to.

The good news is that this problem is usually fixable. This article explains what is happening behind the scenes, the mistakes that make it worse, and a step-by-step plan to get approved in Fukuoka or anywhere else in Japan.

What actually happens

When you apply for an apartment in Japan, your application is usually reviewed by at least two parties: the landlord (or the management company acting for them) and a rent guarantee company (保証会社, hoshō gaisha). For most apartments in Fukuoka today, using a guarantee company is mandatory, even if you have a Japanese friend who could act as a personal guarantor.

Both parties look at your residence card (在留カード, zairyū kādo). On the front, there is a field showing your period of stay and the expiry date. If that date is close, for example within six months or so, many screeners flag the application. Some will reject it outright. Others will put it on hold and ask for "proof that you can stay." A few will approve it with extra conditions, such as a larger deposit or a shorter contract.

What you usually hear from the agent is vague: "The guarantee company did not approve," or "The owner is worried about the visa." You rarely get a written reason. That vagueness is normal in Japan, and it does not necessarily mean you were judged personally. It usually means a rule or a checklist item was not satisfied.

If you want a broader overview of why foreign applications get turned down, see our page on apartment rejections in Japan. This article focuses specifically on the visa expiry problem.

Why it happens

Understanding the logic behind the rejection makes it much easier to fix. There are several structural reasons, and they often stack on top of each other.

The contract length does not match your period of stay

A typical residential lease in Japan is a standard lease (普通借家契約, futsū shakuya keiyaku) for two years, with the option to renew. If your residence card expires in four months, the landlord sees a tenant who, on paper, may have to leave the country long before the contract ends. Early departure means vacancy, cleaning, re-advertising, and lost income. From the landlord's point of view, that is a risk they would rather avoid.

The guarantee company's screening rules

Guarantee companies promise to pay the landlord if you stop paying rent. Their entire business is risk assessment. A tenant who might leave Japan suddenly is, from their perspective, a tenant who might leave with unpaid rent or unpaid restoration costs that are hard to recover from abroad. Many guarantee companies have internal rules about minimum remaining period of stay. These rules are not published, and they vary from company to company, so one company may reject you while another approves you on the same day.

Landlords are cautious by default

Many apartments in Fukuoka are owned by individual landlords, often older owners with one or two buildings. They tend to be conservative, and some have had a bad experience with a tenant who left the country without notice. Even a single story like that can make an owner cautious about every foreign applicant whose visa looks short.

Language and emergency contact concerns

A short visa period often combines with other worries: Will the tenant understand the contract? Who can be contacted in an emergency? If you are also missing a Japanese-speaking emergency contact, the visa issue becomes the final reason to say no. We cover that part in detail in our article on rejections due to no emergency contact.

Your renewal is invisible to them

Here is the core of the problem: the screener only sees the date printed on your card. They do not see that your employer has already confirmed your contract extension, or that you plan to apply for renewal next month. Unless you show them evidence, the expiry date is the only information they have.

Common mistakes

  • Applying without mentioning your renewal plans at all. If you hand over your residence card and say nothing, the screener will assume the worst. A short explanation, backed by documents, changes the conversation.
  • Waiting until the last few weeks before expiry to apply for renewal. In most cases you can apply for an extension of your period of stay up to about three months before it expires. Many people wait much longer, and then try to move apartments at the same time, which makes both processes more stressful.
  • Applying for the same apartment again through the same guarantee company. If one guarantee company rejected you, resubmitting the same application usually produces the same answer. You need either new evidence or a different guarantee company.
  • Hiding or downplaying the visa situation. Some applicants try to avoid the topic. The screener will see the card anyway. Being upfront and organised looks far better than appearing evasive.
  • Only looking at standard two-year leases. There are several housing types where a short remaining visa period matters much less. Ignoring them can leave you stuck in a loop of rejections.

Step-by-step fix

  1. Check exactly when you can apply for renewal. Look at the expiry date on your residence card. For most statuses of residence, you can submit an extension application at the regional immigration office from roughly three months before expiry. In Fukuoka, that is the Fukuoka Regional Immigration Services Bureau. If you are already inside that window, apply now rather than later. If you need help preparing the paperwork, our article on residence card renewal document mistakes walks through common problems.
  2. Once you apply, use the "application in progress" status. When you submit a renewal application, immigration normally places a stamp or sticker on the back of your residence card showing that an extension application is under review. If your card expires while the application is pending, you can generally continue to stay for a limited period while waiting for the decision. Ask for a copy of your application receipt and keep it with your card. Show both to the agent. This is often the single most persuasive piece of evidence you can provide.
  3. Get a letter from your employer or school. Ask HR for a short letter confirming that your employment continues beyond your current visa expiry, ideally stating your contract end date or that your contract is open-ended, and that the company will support your renewal. Students can ask for a certificate of enrolment showing the expected graduation date. A document in Japanese on company letterhead carries far more weight than an email in English.
  4. Tell the agent exactly what to say to the screener. Agents in Japan often relay information briefly. Give them a clear message they can pass on. For example: "My visa expires in March, but I have already applied for renewal. Here is the receipt and a letter from my employer confirming I will continue working in Fukuoka after that date." In Japanese, you can say: 「在留期間の更新申請中です。申請受付票と会社の在籍証明書があります。」 (Zairyū kikan no kōshin shinsei-chū desu. Shinsei uketsuke-hyō to kaisha no zaiseki shōmeisho ga arimasu.)
  5. Ask the agent to try a different guarantee company. Many agencies work with several guarantee companies. Some are more familiar with foreign residents and have screening rules that fit your situation better. Ask directly: 「外国人の審査に強い保証会社はありますか?」 (Is there a guarantee company that is more flexible with foreign applicants?) Our page on renting without a guarantor explains how these companies work.
  6. Offer something that lowers the landlord's risk. Depending on the property, you could offer to pay several months of rent in advance, accept a slightly larger deposit (敷金, shikikin), or agree to a fixed-term lease (定期借家契約, teiki shakuya keiyaku) that ends near a date you are confident about. Not every landlord will accept these offers, but they show that you understand the concern and are willing to meet it halfway.
  7. Consider properties that are designed for this situation. If you need a place quickly and screening keeps failing, look at options where a short remaining period of stay matters less: share houses, monthly apartments (マンスリーマンション), and furnished rentals run by large operators that regularly house foreign residents. These often have simpler screening, shorter minimum stays, and fewer upfront costs. For example, [PR] Leopalace21 offers furnished apartments with appliances across Japan, including Fukuoka, and does not charge an agency fee. You can stay there while your renewal is processed, then move into a long-term lease once your new card is issued.
  8. Reapply after your new card arrives. Once your renewal is approved and you receive a new residence card with a fresh expiry date, the original problem disappears. If you loved the first apartment and it is still available, ask the agent to resubmit with your new card. Many people who are rejected for this reason are approved without issue a few weeks later.
Important: Never stop paying attention to your own visa deadline because you are busy apartment hunting. Staying past your period of stay without a pending renewal application is a serious immigration problem, and no apartment is worth that risk. Handle the renewal first, then the apartment.

Required documents

Bring as many of these as apply to you. The more you can show, the easier it is for the agent to argue your case.

  • Residence card (在留カード, zairyū kādo), front and back
  • Passport
  • Receipt for your extension application (申請受付票, shinsei uketsuke-hyō), if you have already applied
  • Certificate of employment (在籍証明書, zaiseki shōmeisho) or employer letter confirming continued employment
  • Employment contract (雇用契約書, koyō keiyakusho) showing the contract period
  • Certificate of enrolment (在学証明書, zaigaku shōmeisho) for students
  • Recent pay slips (給与明細, kyūyo meisai) or tax certificate (課税証明書, kazei shōmeisho)
  • Certificate of residence (住民票, jūminhyō) from your ward office
  • Emergency contact details, ideally someone who speaks Japanese
  • Bank account details for rent payments

Also budget for the move-in costs themselves. Even when screening goes smoothly, the initial payment in Japan can be several times the monthly rent. See hidden costs of renting in Japan so nothing surprises you at contract time.

City / region differences

Fukuoka

Fukuoka is a relatively affordable city compared with Tokyo or Osaka. A one-room apartment in central areas like Tenjin, Akasaka, or around Hakata Station often falls somewhere in the range of about ¥45,000 to ¥75,000 per month, with cheaper options further out along the subway and Nishitetsu lines. Fukuoka also has a large student population and a growing number of foreign professionals and entrepreneurs, so some agents in central Fukuoka are used to working with foreign tenants. That said, many smaller local agencies still follow conservative screening practices, especially for older buildings owned by individuals.

If one agency in Fukuoka cannot help you, try another, particularly ones near the universities or in Hakata and Tenjin that advertise foreign-friendly support. The difference between agencies can be surprisingly large.

Tokyo and Osaka

In Tokyo, competition for apartments is stronger, so landlords can afford to be picky, and a short visa period is an easy reason to choose another applicant. On the other hand, Tokyo has the most foreign-friendly agencies and furnished rental operators. Osaka sits in between. For Osaka-specific advice, see our Osaka renting page.

Smaller cities

In smaller cities around Kyushu and elsewhere, the number of agencies used to foreign tenants is lower, and some landlords simply have little experience with residence cards. Bringing well-organised documents and a clear explanation matters even more there.

Frequently Asked Questions

How many months left on my visa is "enough" for most landlords?

There is no official rule, and every guarantee company and landlord sets its own standard. In practice, applicants with less than about six months remaining tend to face more questions. If you are in that range, prepare renewal evidence before you apply rather than waiting to be asked.

Can I sign a lease while my renewal application is still pending?

Often, yes. Many agents and guarantee companies will accept the application receipt and the "application in progress" stamp on your residence card, especially when combined with an employer letter. Some will still ask you to wait until the new card arrives. Ask the agent which documents their guarantee company accepts.

Will a fixed-term lease solve the problem?

It can help. A fixed-term lease (定期借家契約) has a set end date, so the landlord's risk is clearer. These leases are less common than standard leases, and the conditions can be stricter, so read the contract carefully, especially the rules on early termination and renewal.

I am on a working holiday visa. Is it impossible to rent a normal apartment?

Not impossible, but harder, because working holiday visas usually have a fixed period that cannot simply be renewed. Most working holiday makers have better success with share houses, monthly apartments, or furnished rentals with short minimum stays. These are designed for people who will not stay for two years.

Should I tell the agent that I might leave Japan in the future?

Be honest about your current plans, but focus on facts that matter for the lease: your job, your renewal status, and how long you intend to stay. If you are planning to stay long term, say so clearly and support it with documents. If you know you will leave within a year, a furnished rental or share house is usually a better fit than a two-year lease.
