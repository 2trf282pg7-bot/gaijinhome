Deposit Refund Denied in Japan? Fight Unfair Deductions and Get Your Money Back

Updated November 2026 · GaijinHome

You cleaned the apartment, handed back the keys, and waited for your deposit. Then the settlement statement arrived: wallpaper replacement, full flooring repair, professional cleaning, key exchange, and a final line showing you get almost nothing back, or even owe money. If you are about to leave Japan or have already left, it can feel impossible to argue. But many of these charges are negotiable, and some are simply not the tenant's responsibility. Here is how to tell the difference and what to do about it.

What Actually Happens at Move-Out

When you move out, the management company (管理会社) or a contractor inspects the unit, usually with you present (退去立会い, taikyo tachiai). They note damage and may ask you to sign an inspection sheet on the spot. A few weeks later you receive a settlement statement (敷金精算書) listing restoration costs (原状回復費用) deducted from your deposit (敷金). The balance, if any, is transferred to your bank account.

Problems arise when the statement charges you for things that are normal aging, when the costs look inflated, or when you signed the inspection sheet without understanding it. Foreign tenants are especially exposed because they often leave Japan soon after moving out and are less likely to dispute.

Why Deposit Refunds Get Denied or Reduced

Confusion between wear and tear and damage

Japan's Ministry of Land, Infrastructure, Transport and Tourism publishes restoration guidelines (原状回復をめぐるトラブルとガイドライン). The basic principle is that tenants are not responsible for ordinary wear and tear (通常損耗) and aging (経年劣化), such as sun-faded wallpaper, furniture marks on flooring, or the natural aging of fixtures. Tenants are responsible for damage caused by negligence or misuse, such as cigarette burns, holes in walls, pet scratches, or mold caused by never ventilating. The 2020 revision of the Civil Code also stated the wear-and-tear principle in the law itself. Many disputed charges fall on the wrong side of this line.

Full replacement charged instead of proportional cost

Even when you did cause damage, items like wallpaper lose value over time. The guidelines describe reducing the tenant's share based on how old the item is. Being charged the full cost of brand-new wallpaper for a room that was last papered many years ago is a common overcharge.

Special clauses in the contract

Many contracts include special terms (特約) that make the tenant pay for certain items, most commonly professional cleaning or key replacement. These can be valid if they were clearly explained and the amount was reasonable and specific. Vague clauses that shift all restoration costs to the tenant are more open to challenge.

Non-refundable deposit arrangements

In some regions, part of the deposit is deducted by agreement (敷引き) or treated as non-refundable. That portion is usually written into the contract and harder to recover. See our article on non-refundable deposits in Japan for how these work.

The tenant has already left the country

Some management companies assume foreign tenants will not follow up. They may delay, ignore emails, or apply generous charges. Leaving without a forwarding plan makes this easier for them.

Common Mistakes

  • Signing the inspection sheet without reading it. You can write that you do not agree with specific items, or ask to take it home first.
  • Taking no photos at move-in. Without "before" evidence, it is hard to prove damage was already there.
  • Not reading the special clauses (特約). Know what you agreed to pay before arguing.
  • Arguing by phone only. Phone conversations leave no record. Put disputes in writing.
  • Closing your Japanese bank account too early. If the refund has nowhere to go, it gets complicated.

Step-by-Step Fix

  1. Request an itemized statement. If you only received a total, ask for line-by-line costs with quantities and unit prices: 「原状回復費用の明細と見積書をいただけますか。」 ("Could you send me an itemized breakdown and quote for the restoration costs?").
  2. Compare each line with the contract and guidelines. Mark each item as one of: (a) agreed in a special clause, (b) damage you caused, or (c) wear and tear or aging. Category (c) is your main argument.
  3. Gather evidence. Collect move-in photos, any move-in checklist (入居時チェックリスト), move-out photos, and the year the wallpaper or flooring was last replaced if you know it.
  4. Send a written objection. Email or letter is fine. Be calm and specific, for example: "Item 3, wallpaper replacement in the living room, appears to be normal aging. Under the MLIT restoration guidelines, this is generally the landlord's responsibility. Please remove or reduce this charge." Ask for a reply by a specific date.
  5. Ask about depreciation for real damage. If you did cause damage, ask how the cost was adjusted for the age of the item. Request the age of the wallpaper, flooring or fixtures.
  6. Use free consultation services. Local consumer affairs centers (消費生活センター) handle rental deposit disputes and can advise or sometimes mediate; the national consumer hotline 188 connects you to one. The Japan Legal Support Center (法テラス) can also provide information.
  7. Consider small claims court. For amounts within the small claims limit (currently up to 600,000 yen), Japan's small claims procedure (少額訴訟) is designed to be quick, often concluding in a single hearing. It is in Japanese, so you may need help, but often the possibility alone encourages a settlement.
  8. Protect your next deposit. For your next home, take dated photos of every room at move-in and send them to the management company. If you want to avoid large deposits altogether, look at UR housing, which follows clear restoration rules, or options covered in our full renting guide.
Key tip: You do not have to sign the move-out inspection sheet on the spot. Write 「内容を確認してから署名します」 ("I will sign after checking the contents") and take photos of every room before you leave.

Required Documents

  • Lease contract (賃貸借契約書), including special clauses (特約)
  • Important matters explanation (重要事項説明書)
  • Move-in condition checklist (入居時チェックリスト), if you received one
  • Move-in and move-out photos with dates
  • Move-out inspection sheet (退去立会い確認書)
  • Deposit settlement statement (敷金精算書) and itemized quotes (見積書)
  • Receipt or contract showing the deposit you paid (預り金 / 敷金の領収書)
  • Written communication with the management company
  • Bank account details for the refund, including an account that will remain open
  • Residence card (在留カード) copy, if requested for identity confirmation

City and Region Differences

Tokyo has a local ordinance, often called the Tokyo Rules (東京ルール), that requires agents to explain restoration and wear-and-tear responsibilities to tenants at contract time for residential leases in Tokyo. If you rented in Tokyo and never received that explanation, mention it when disputing charges. Deposits in Tokyo are commonly around one to two months' rent, with key money often charged separately.

Osaka and Kansai are where 敷引き and 保証金 arrangements are most common. The agreed deduction is generally kept by the landlord, but charges beyond it still need to be justified. Read our Osaka guide for typical contract terms.

Other regions, such as Fukuoka and parts of Kyushu, may also use agreed deductions in older contracts. Regional consumer affairs centers are often less busy, which can make it easier to get detailed help.

If you have already left Japan: You can still dispute by email. Give the management company an overseas contact address and, if possible, keep a Japanese bank account open or nominate a trusted person in Japan to receive the refund. International transfers may carry fees, so ask who pays them.

Frequently Asked Questions

Is professional cleaning always my responsibility?

Not automatically. Under the general principle, normal cleaning at the end of a tenancy is often treated as the landlord's cost. However, many contracts include a special clause making the tenant pay a fixed cleaning fee. If the clause is clear and the amount is specific and reasonable, it is likely valid. If it is vague, you can question it.

Can I refuse to pay for wallpaper replacement?

If the wallpaper is simply faded or aged, you can argue it is ordinary wear and tear. If you caused damage, such as holes or heavy stains, you may have to pay part of the cost, but it should normally be reduced to reflect the wallpaper's age. Ask how depreciation was calculated.

How long should it take to get my deposit back?

It varies by contract and company, but settlements commonly arrive within about a month after move-out. If you have heard nothing after that, send a written request asking for the statement and the expected refund date.

The management company ignores my emails. What now?

Send a formal written request with a deadline, then contact a consumer affairs center for advice. Mentioning that you are seeking consultation, or considering small claims court, often prompts a response. Keep copies of everything you send.

Will a dispute hurt my chances of renting again?

Disputing restoration charges politely and in writing is a normal part of renting in Japan and is not generally reported to guarantor companies. What can affect future screening is unpaid rent or unpaid amounts that go to collection, so avoid simply refusing to pay legitimate charges. See our rejection page for what affects screening.
