Why Foreigners Struggle to Find Affordable Apartments in Tokyo — Real Solutions

Updated November 2026 · GaijinHome

You set a budget that sounded reasonable, opened the listing sites, and found what looked like plenty of options. Then reality hit: the cheap units were "already taken," agents stopped replying, and the few places that accepted you wanted five months of rent upfront. If you are trying to rent cheaply in Tokyo as a foreigner, the problem is usually not that affordable apartments do not exist. It is that the cheapest part of the market is also the part most closed to foreign applicants. Here is why, and how to get around it.

What Actually Happens When You Search on a Budget

Most budget searches in Tokyo follow the same frustrating pattern:

  • You find a ¥60,000–¥75,000 one-room (1K) apartment online, contact the agent, and are told it is no longer available — while the listing stays up.
  • An agent offers to show you "similar" units that turn out to cost ¥15,000–¥25,000 more per month.
  • You are told the owner does not accept foreigners (外国人不可), or that you need a Japanese guarantor.
  • When you finally get an approval, the initial cost estimate is four to six times the monthly rent, which blows your moving budget.

Some of the "already taken" listings are genuinely gone; some are bait (おとり物件) used to bring in customers. Either way, the outcome is the same: you burn weeks and still have no keys.

Why It Happens: The Structure Behind Tokyo's Budget Market

Cheap units are owned by the most risk-averse landlords

Low-rent apartments in Tokyo are often in older buildings owned by individual landlords, sometimes elderly, who manage only a few units. They worry about communication problems, noise, garbage-sorting disputes and tenants leaving the country without notice. A large management company might have a process for foreign tenants; a small private owner often just says no.

Guarantor company screening is tighter at the low end

Almost every rental now requires a guarantor company (保証会社). These companies check your income against the rent, your visa status and your history. Some are linked to credit card or consumer-finance groups and can check credit databases such as CIC or JICC; if you have no Japanese credit history, or late phone-installment payments, that can hurt. Applicants on lower incomes — students, new arrivals, part-time workers — are exactly the ones who get flagged, and they are also the ones searching cheap units. If you have been turned down already, see our guide to apartment rejections.

Your visa period and the two-year lease

Standard Tokyo leases run for two years. If your residence card shows only six months or one year remaining, some landlords and guarantor companies treat that as a risk, even if renewal is routine for your visa type.

Initial costs are the real barrier

Even when the monthly rent is affordable, the upfront bill — deposit, key money, agency fee, guarantor fee, first month's rent, insurance and lock change — is what stops many people. That is why "cheap rent" and "cheap to move in" are two different searches.

Common Mistakes on a Tight Budget

  1. Only searching English-language listing sites. English portals show a small, often more expensive slice of the market. Most cheap stock is listed only on Japanese portals or by local agents.
  2. Insisting on central Tokyo and a short commute. Looking only at Shibuya, Shinjuku or Minato on a ¥70,000 budget means competing for very few units, many of them tiny or very old.
  3. Ignoring the building age and walking time. Filters like "built within 10 years" and "within 5 minutes of the station" quietly remove most affordable options.
  4. Applying to many places at once with weak documents. Multiple rejections in a short time do not help, and a thin application wastes your best options.
  5. Comparing only the monthly rent. A ¥68,000 unit with two months' key money can cost more over two years than a ¥73,000 unit with none.

Step-by-Step Fix: How to Actually Rent Cheap in Tokyo

  1. Set two budgets, not one. Decide your maximum monthly rent (many guarantor companies want it to be roughly one-third of your monthly take-home pay or less) and, separately, your maximum move-in cost. Knowing both tells you whether to target low-rent units or low-initial-cost units.
  2. Widen your map along one train line. Pick the station you commute to, then look 20–40 minutes out along the same line. Prices often drop noticeably once you leave the central wards or cross into Saitama, Chiba or Kanagawa.
  3. Relax two filters. Allow buildings 25–40 years old and up to 10–15 minutes' walk from the station. These two changes alone open a large part of the budget market. Check whether older buildings meet the post-1981 earthquake standard (新耐震基準) if that matters to you.
  4. Use an agent who works with foreign tenants. Ask directly: "Do you have landlords who accept foreign tenants and guarantor companies that accept my visa type?" An agent who already knows which owners say yes will save you weeks.
  5. Prepare your file before you view. Have your documents (list below) scanned and ready. When you find a good unit, apply that day — cheap units in Tokyo often go within days.
  6. Target zero-zero and free-rent listings. Ask for 敷金・礼金ゼロ (no deposit, no key money) or フリーレント (free rent) listings to cut the move-in bill. Read our key money explainer before negotiating.
  7. Consider alternatives with lower barriers. UR public housing requires no guarantor and charges no key money or agency fee, though rents are not always the cheapest (see our UR guide). Share houses let you move in with a fraction of the usual upfront cost and are often the fastest way to get a Tokyo address while you look for a longer-term place.   8. Use a share house as a stepping stone. After several months in Japan, you will have pay slips, a registered address and possibly a Japanese bank account — all of which make a regular lease much easier to get.
Key tip: Ask the agent for the total initial cost estimate (初期費用の見積もり) before viewing. If a unit's move-in cost is beyond your second budget, skip the viewing and save your time for places you can actually afford.

Required Documents

  • Residence card (在留カード), front and back, with enough remaining validity
  • Passport
  • Certificate of employment (在職証明書) showing your salary and start date, or an offer letter (内定通知書)
  • Recent pay slips (給与明細), usually the last 2–3 months
  • Tax certificate (課税証明書) or withholding slip (源泉徴収票) if you have a previous year's income in Japan
  • Student certificate (在学証明書) and proof of funds or scholarship, for students
  • Bank statement or savings balance, useful if your income is low or new
  • Japanese phone number and an emergency contact (緊急連絡先) in Japan

Rents vary by building, size and station, so treat the ranges below as rough guides for a one-room (1R/1K) apartment, not guarantees.

Tokyo quirks that affect your budget

  • Renewal fees (更新料). Common in Tokyo, often around one month's rent every two years. Add this to your long-term cost.
  • Management and common fees (管理費・共益費). Listed separately from rent. A "¥65,000" unit with ¥8,000 in fees is really ¥73,000.
  • Bicycle parking fees. Many buildings charge a small monthly fee for bike parking, and you will need anti-theft registration (防犯登録) for any bicycle you own. If you plan to cycle to save on train costs, check parking first.
  • Peak season. January to March is the most competitive time. Searching from May to November usually means more negotiating room.

Frequently Asked Questions

What is a realistic rent for a foreigner on a budget in Tokyo?

For a basic one-room apartment, many budget renters end up somewhere around ¥55,000–¥80,000 per month, depending on the area, building age and distance from the station. Below that is possible but usually means older buildings, long walks or locations in outer Tokyo or neighboring prefectures.

Why do cheap listings disappear when I contact the agent?

Some units are genuinely rented quickly, because good budget units move fast. Others are bait listings kept online to attract inquiries. Ask the agent to confirm availability with the management company before you visit, and judge the agent by whether they show you real alternatives in your budget.

Can I rent a cheap apartment in Tokyo without a guarantor?

Most regular rentals use a guarantor company instead of a personal guarantor, so you usually do not need a Japanese friend to sign. If guarantor companies reject you, UR housing, share houses and monthly apartments are options. Our no-guarantor guide explains each route.

Is it cheaper to live outside the 23 wards?

Usually yes. Western Tokyo cities and commuter towns in Saitama, Chiba and Kanagawa often offer the same or larger space for considerably less rent. Factor in commuting time and whether your employer covers train passes, which many Japanese employers do.

How much money do I need to move into a Tokyo apartment?

For a standard lease, budget roughly four to six months of rent for the initial costs. Zero-deposit, zero-key-money and free-rent listings can bring that down considerably, and share houses typically require much less upfront.
