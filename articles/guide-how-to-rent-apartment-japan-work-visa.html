Apartment Rejected in Japan on a Work Visa? What You're Missing and What to Do

Updated November 2026 · GaijinHome

You have a job, a salary and a valid work visa. On paper you are exactly the kind of tenant landlords want. So a rejection feels illogical, and maybe insulting. You did everything right, and the answer was still no.

Here is the uncomfortable truth: in Japan, having a work visa is the starting point, not the deciding factor. Screening looks at how your visa, job and paperwork fit together over the length of a lease. Most work visa holders who get rejected are missing one specific piece of that picture. This article is about finding that piece.

What Actually Happens

Typical stories from work visa holders look like this:

  • You started your job a month ago, applied for an apartment, and the guarantor company declined you because you have no payslips yet.
  • Your residence card shows one year remaining, and the landlord would not accept a two-year lease.
  • You transferred to Japan from an overseas branch, your salary is paid partly abroad, and the screening company could not verify your income.
  • You are on a contract or dispatch (派遣, haken) role, and screening treated it as unstable employment.
  • You recently changed jobs and your current employer does not yet match the one on your residence card records.

In almost every case, nobody tells you which of these was the problem. You just hear "the screening did not pass" (審査が通りませんでした).

Why It Happens

Screening checks the next two years, not today

A standard Japanese lease runs two years. The guarantor company (保証会社) and landlord want confidence that rent will be paid for that whole period. Your visa type matters less than three questions: how long you can legally stay, how stable your income is, and how easy it would be to recover money if you left.

Period of stay is read literally

Work visas are granted for periods such as one, three or five years. If yours ends six months into a two-year lease, some screeners will treat the gap as a risk even though renewal is routine for people with ongoing employment. A newly arrived worker with a one-year first period of stay is particularly exposed to this.

Income has to be provable in Japan

Guarantor companies typically want Japanese-format proof: payslips, a certificate of employment, and for people who have been here over a year, a resident tax certificate (課税証明書). If you are new, have no Japanese tax history, or are paid from overseas, the screener may have nothing they recognize. A strong salary that cannot be documented in the expected format can still be rejected.

Employer recognition

Screening often considers the employer itself: its size, how long it has existed, and whether it is easy to verify. Large, well-known companies can make approval easier. A small start-up, a foreign company without a strong Japan presence, or a dispatch arrangement can make the screener cautious, even if your job is secure.

Credit history in Japan

Some guarantor companies are linked to credit card companies and check credit records. As a newcomer you usually have no Japanese credit history, which is neutral rather than negative, but late phone or card payments in Japan can count against you. Common Mistakes

  1. Applying before your first payslip without compensating. If you have no payslips yet, you need an offer letter or certificate of employment stating your salary, and ideally a higher deposit or prepayment to balance it.
  2. Choosing rent that is too high for your salary. Many screeners expect rent to stay around a third of monthly income or lower, though thresholds vary. Stretching your budget is one of the most common causes of rejection.
  3. Forgetting that your employer will be called. Screeners may phone your workplace to confirm employment. If reception does not recognize your name or HR is not expecting the call, verification can fail.
  4. Applying right after a job change. If you changed employers, you must notify immigration within the required period. Mismatched information between your application and your records looks bad, and screeners often prefer some tenure at the new job.
  5. Not asking about a corporate lease. Many work visa holders could have their company sign the lease and never ask.

Step-by-Step Fix

  1. Diagnose the weak point. Ask the agent whether the issue was income, employment length, visa period, credit, or the landlord's preference: 「どの点が審査で引っかかったか、分かる範囲で教えていただけますか？」. Even a hint narrows your fix.
  2. Ask HR about a corporate contract or housing support. Approach HR with a specific request: "Could the company sign as the lessee (法人契約), or provide a letter confirming my employment and expected visa renewal?" A corporate lease often bypasses individual screening altogether.
  3. Brief your workplace for the verification call. Tell your manager or HR that a guarantor company may call to confirm your employment, and give them your name in both English and katakana so reception can find you.
  4. Close the visa gap on paper. If your remaining period of stay is short, apply for renewal as early as allowed and show the application receipt. Alternatively, ask whether the landlord accepts a fixed-term lease (定期借家契約, teiki shakuya keiyaku) that ends before your period of stay does.
  5. Lower the rent or raise the guarantee. Drop your target rent until it fits the screener's comfort zone, or offer a larger deposit or several months' prepayment. Both directly reduce the risk the guarantor is assessing.
  6. Change the guarantor company or the route. Ask the agent to try a guarantor company that is more experienced with foreign applicants. If standard screening keeps failing, look at options that skip it: UR public housing, which has no guarantor requirement and income-based eligibility (see our UR housing guide), or guarantor-free apartments covered in our no-guarantor guide.
  7. Use a furnished bridge while you build history. Three months of payslips can change your screening outcome. A furnished apartment for that period is often cheaper than repeated failed applications. [PR] CrossOneRoom furnished Tokyo apartments is one option aimed at foreign residents.
Key tip: If you are newly arrived, the single most powerful document is a certificate of employment (在職証明書) on company letterhead that states your salary, start date and employment type. Ask HR for it in Japanese before you start apartment hunting.

Required Documents

  • Residence card (在留カード), front and back, showing status of residence and period of stay
  • Passport (パスポート)
  • Certificate of employment (在職証明書), stating salary, start date and contract type
  • Employment contract (雇用契約書) or offer letter, if you have not yet received payslips
  • Recent payslips (給与明細), usually two or three months
  • Resident tax certificate (住民税課税証明書) or withholding slip (源泉徴収票, gensen chōshūhyō), if you have Japanese tax history
  • Immigration renewal application receipt, if your period of stay ends soon
  • Certificate of residence (住民票) from your city office
  • Emergency contact (緊急連絡先) in Japan
  • Japanese bank account, and a credit card if the guarantor company requires one

City and Region Differences

Tokyo

Tokyo screening is the most standardized: almost every property uses a guarantor company, and agents are used to work visa holders at large companies. High rents mean the income-to-rent ratio is the most common failure point. Looking a few stations out on the same line, or in neighboring Kawasaki or Saitama, is often the easiest fix. Agents in central wards are also more likely to know which guarantor companies are flexible with foreign workers.

Osaka

Osaka has lower rents, so the income ratio is easier to satisfy, but traditional Kansai practices such as non-refundable deposit deductions (敷引き, shikibiki) can still appear in some contracts. Read the deposit terms carefully; our article on non-refundable deposits explains what to look for. Our Osaka guide covers the local market.

Nagoya, Fukuoka and industrial regions

In regions with many manufacturers or technical intern and skilled worker programs, employers frequently provide company housing or sign leases on behalf of staff. If you are in one of these areas, ask your employer first; going through the company is often far simpler than applying as an individual.

Frequently Asked Questions

Can I rent an apartment in Japan before receiving my first salary?

Often yes, but you need to replace payslips with other proof. A certificate of employment or employment contract stating your salary, plus savings or a willingness to prepay or pay a higher deposit, can be enough for many guarantor companies. A corporate lease removes the problem entirely.

Does the type of work visa matter for renting?

Screeners care less about the exact category than about your period of stay, employer and income. A longer remaining period of stay and a stable, verifiable employer usually help more than any particular visa name.

My visa expires in eight months. Can I still sign a two-year lease?

Many landlords will accept it, since a standard lease can be renewed or ended with notice. Others will not. Showing that your employment continues and that you will renew, or choosing a fixed-term lease that ends before your period of stay, are the usual ways to get past this. Our visa guide covers renewal basics.

Is a dispatch or contract job a problem for screening?

It can make screeners more cautious, especially with a short contract. Showing a longer track record, a contract renewal history, or a letter from the dispatch company can help. Lower rent and a larger deposit also offset the perceived risk.

What should I do right after a rejection?

Ask the agent which part failed, fix that one thing, and reapply with a different property or guarantor company rather than the same combination. Our guide to apartment rejection reasons lists the most common causes in more detail.
