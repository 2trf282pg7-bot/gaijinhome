Why Are Foreigners Confused About Key Money in Japan? What Is It Really?

Updated November 2026 · GaijinHome

You found an apartment you like, the agent sends over an estimate, and there it is: a line called 礼金 (reikin), often equal to one or two full months of rent. You ask what it is for, and the answer is something like "it is a thank-you to the landlord." You will not get it back. Nothing is provided in exchange. If that sounds strange, you are not alone — key money is one of the most confusing parts of renting in Japan for newcomers, and it is also one of the easiest costs to reduce once you understand how it works.

What Actually Happens: The Estimate That Doesn't Add Up

Most foreigners first meet key money on the initial cost estimate (初期費用の見積もり). A typical estimate for a Tokyo apartment at ¥100,000 per month might look roughly like this:

ItemJapaneseTypical amountRefundable?
Security deposit敷金 (shikikin)0–2 months' rentPartly, after cleaning/repair deductions
Key money礼金 (reikin)0–2 months' rentNo
Agency fee仲介手数料Up to 1 month + taxNo
Guarantor company fee保証会社 初回保証料Often 50–100% of one month's rentNo
First month's rent (and pro-rated days)前家賃・日割り家賃1–2 monthsNo
Fire insurance, lock change, etc.火災保険・鍵交換代Roughly ¥15,000–¥50,000 combinedNo

Add it up and you can easily be asked for four to six months of rent before you get the keys. The key money line is the one that confuses people most, because unlike the deposit, there is no condition under which it comes back. It is simply gone the moment you sign. Why It Happens: Where Key Money Comes From and Why It Survives

A custom, not a legal requirement

Key money is not required by any law. It is a market custom that is commonly traced back to the post-war housing shortage, when tenants competed for scarce rooms and landlords could ask for an extra payment. Today it functions as one-off income for the landlord, collected at the start of the lease. Because it is a custom, the amount is set by the landlord and can be zero.

It is negotiable price, not a fixed fee

This is the part most guides skip. Landlords and management companies treat key money as a lever. A popular, newly built apartment near a station might ask for two months. The same landlord with a unit sitting empty for three months may quietly drop it to zero. Key money is one of the main tools landlords use to adjust demand without lowering the monthly rent, because a lower monthly rent affects the building's valuation and future tenants, while a one-time discount does not.

Why foreigners feel it more

  • No equivalent back home. Most countries have a refundable deposit and maybe a holding fee. A non-refundable "gift" feels like a scam if no one explains it.
  • Fewer options means less leverage. If many landlords decline foreign applicants, you end up competing for a smaller pool of "foreigner-OK" (外国人可) listings, and those landlords know it.
  • Short visa periods. If you only expect to stay one or two years, paying a large non-refundable sum hurts much more per month of stay than it does for a local tenant staying a decade.
  • Language gaps in the estimate. Key money is often listed only as 礼金 with no explanation, next to other similar-looking fees, so it is hard to question line by line.

Key money vs. the deposit vs. renewal fee

Three different payments get mixed up all the time. The deposit (敷金) is security money held against unpaid rent and damage beyond normal wear; it is returned minus legitimate deductions. Key money (礼金) is never returned. The renewal fee (更新料) is a separate charge, common in the Tokyo area, often around one month's rent when a standard two-year lease is renewed. If your deposit was not returned, that is a different problem — see our guide on disputing a non-refunded deposit.

Common Mistakes Foreigners Make With Key Money

  1. Assuming it is refundable. People pay it thinking it works like a deposit, then plan their move-out budget expecting money back. It will not come back under any circumstances.
  2. Not asking for a reduction at all. Many renters accept the listed amount because they assume it is fixed. In practice, asking costs nothing and often works, especially in the off-season.
  3. Negotiating the wrong way. Demanding that the landlord "remove illegal fees" or comparing Japan unfavorably with your home country tends to end the conversation. Polite, specific requests through the agent work far better.
  4. Chasing zero key money without reading the rest. Some "no key money" listings make up for it with a higher rent, a mandatory cleaning fee, a short-term cancellation penalty (短期解約違約金), or an expensive required support package. Always compare the total cost over your planned stay.
  5. Negotiating after applying. Once your application has passed screening and the contract is drafted, the landlord has little reason to change terms. Raise key money before or together with the application.

Step-by-Step Fix: How to Reduce or Avoid Key Money

  1. Calculate your real cost per month of stay. Take all non-refundable initial costs (key money, agency fee, guarantor fee, lock change) and divide by the number of months you realistically expect to live there. This tells you which listing is truly cheaper, and gives you a clear number to negotiate around.
  2. Filter for 礼金なし or ゼロゼロ listings. On Japanese portals you can filter for no key money (礼金なし) and for "zero-zero" properties (敷金・礼金ゼロ), meaning no deposit and no key money. Ask your agent to search these specifically if you cannot read the sites.
  3. Time your search. Demand peaks from roughly January to March, when students and new employees move. From late spring through autumn, landlords are more flexible. If your move date is flexible, use that.
  4. Ask the agent, not the landlord, and ask specifically. A polite request works best: "I really like this apartment and would like to apply. Would the landlord consider reducing the key money from two months to one?" In Japanese: 「礼金を1ヶ月に下げていただくことは可能でしょうか？」 Asking for a partial reduction is more likely to succeed than asking for zero.
  5. Offer something in return. Landlords respond to lower risk and less vacancy. You can offer to sign quickly, start the lease earlier (so they lose fewer empty days), or show strong documents such as a certificate of employment. Units that have been listed for a long time are the best targets.
  6. Ask about free rent as an alternative. If the landlord will not touch key money, ask for free rent (フリーレント), typically a fraction of a month to one month. It achieves a similar saving and some landlords prefer it.
  7. Consider housing types that do not charge key money. UR public rental apartments do not charge key money, agency fees or renewal fees, and do not require a guarantor — see our UR housing application guide. Share houses and monthly apartments usually skip key money too. [PR] For example, Oakhouse share houses are set up for foreign residents and do not require a guarantor.
  8. Get the final figures in writing. Before you pay anything, ask for an updated estimate showing the agreed key money. Check that the same figure appears in the contract (賃貸借契約書) and the important matters explanation (重要事項説明書).
Key tip: Negotiate one thing at a time. Asking for lower key money, lower rent and free rent all at once looks like you are not serious. Pick the item that saves you the most over your expected stay and ask for that first.

Required Documents

Key money is negotiated alongside your application, and a strong file makes landlords more willing to compromise. Have these ready:

  • Residence card (在留カード), front and back
  • Passport (photo page and current visa/landing stamp)
  • Certificate of employment (在職証明書) or offer letter, or a student certificate (在学証明書)
  • Proof of income: recent pay slips (給与明細), or tax certificate (課税証明書) / withholding slip (源泉徴収票) if you have worked in Japan for a year or more
  • Certificate of residence (住民票) if you are already registered at a Japanese address
  • Emergency contact details — ideally someone in Japan who can communicate in Japanese
  • Bank account or credit card for automatic rent payments, if the guarantor company requires one
  • Personal seal (印鑑) if requested, though many contracts now accept a signature

City and Region Differences

Tokyo and the Kanto area

Key money is most deeply rooted in the Tokyo area. One month is very common, two months appear on newer or in-demand buildings, and zero is common on older buildings, units far from stations, and properties that have been vacant a while. Renewal fees are also common in Tokyo, so budget for both. Central wards such as Minato, Shibuya and Meguro tend to see key money held firm on popular units, while outer wards and suburbs in Saitama, Chiba and Kanagawa offer more zero-key-money options.

Osaka and the Kansai area

Kansai has historically had a different system. Instead of a deposit plus key money, some contracts use a larger security payment (保証金) with a fixed portion deducted at move-out, known as 敷引き (shikibiki). The effect is similar to key money — part of what you pay does not come back — but it is labeled differently, so foreigners sometimes think there is "no key money" and then lose part of their deposit. Many Osaka listings now follow a Tokyo-style deposit and key money format, often with lower amounts. Read every line carefully. Our Osaka renting guide explains the local terms.

Other cities

In regional cities and smaller towns, key money is generally lower or absent, because supply is higher and competition among landlords is stronger. Nagoya, Fukuoka and Sapporo all have plenty of low or zero key money options. Customs vary by area and by management company, so always check the actual estimate rather than assuming a regional rule.

Frequently Asked Questions

Is key money legal in Japan?

Yes. Key money is a long-standing market custom, and Japanese courts have generally accepted related upfront charges as valid when they are clearly stated in the contract and not excessive. What matters is that the amount is disclosed before you sign. If it was not shown in your estimate or contract, raise it with the agent before paying.

Will I get my key money back if I move out early?

No. Key money is not refunded whether you stay one month or ten years. That is why it is worth calculating the cost per month of your expected stay before signing. If you might leave early, also check for a short-term cancellation penalty, which is a separate charge.

Is a "no key money" apartment always cheaper?

Not always. Some landlords waive key money but set a higher monthly rent, add a mandatory cleaning fee, or require an early cancellation penalty. Compare the total cost over your planned stay, including deposit deductions and renewal fees, before deciding.

Can I negotiate key money as a foreigner?

Yes. Negotiation is done through your real estate agent and is normal for Japanese tenants too. A polite request for a partial reduction, combined with a quick decision and solid documents, has a reasonable chance of success, especially outside the January–March peak season and on units that have been vacant for a while.

What is the difference between key money and shikibiki?

Key money is paid upfront and never returned. Shikibiki, mostly found in the Kansai area, is a fixed amount deducted from your deposit or security payment when you move out. Both result in money you do not get back, but they appear in different places in the contract, so check both the initial cost estimate and the move-out terms.
