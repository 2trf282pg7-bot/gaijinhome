Apartment Rejected Because of Your Pet in Osaka? What to Do as a Foreigner

Updated November 2026 · GaijinHome

You found a place in Osaka that looked perfect. The agent was friendly, the rent fit your budget, and then you mentioned your cat or dog. Suddenly the answer changed: "Sorry, this landlord does not accept pets," or worse, a vague "the owner decided not to proceed" a few days after you applied. If you are a foreigner, you may also be wondering whether the real problem was your pet, your nationality, or both.

This is one of the most frustrating rental problems in Japan, because it stacks two filters on top of each other. Many landlords are already cautious with foreign tenants, and the pool of pet-allowed apartments is small to begin with. The good news is that pet owners do get approved in Osaka every month. It just takes a different search strategy and a much better application package than a standard rental.

What actually happens

Pet rejections in Japan usually happen at one of three points, and knowing which one you hit tells you what to fix.

  • At the search stage. The listing never allowed pets in the first place. Most listings say ペット不可 (no pets) or simply say nothing, which in practice means no. Agents may show you these anyway and only check later.
  • At the landlord check. The listing says ペット相談可 (pets negotiable). This is not a yes. It means the agent must ask the owner, and the owner decides case by case based on the animal's species, size, number, and the tenant's profile.
  • At the guarantor company screening. The landlord said yes to the pet, but the rent guarantee company (保証会社) rejected the overall application. The pet is rarely the official reason here, but extra risk from a pet can tip a borderline foreign applicant into a rejection.

Agents often won't tell you which stage failed. They may give you a polite non-answer. Ask directly: "Was it the owner who refused the pet, or did the guarantor screening fail?" The answer changes everything about your next step.

Why it happens

Understanding the landlord's logic helps you write an application that answers their fears before they raise them.

Restoration costs and odor

Japanese rentals are returned under 原状回復 (restoration to original condition) rules. Scratched flooring, chewed tatami, stained wallpaper, and lingering odor can mean replacing whole sections of an apartment. Landlords who have been burned once by a pet tenant often switch the entire building to no pets permanently.

Neighbors and building rules

In a 分譲マンション (condominium unit rented out by an individual owner), the building's management association rules apply. Even if the owner personally likes animals, the building bylaws may ban pets or limit them to small dogs and cats. Barking complaints in thin-walled older アパート buildings are another common reason owners say no.

Foreign-tenant risk stacking

Landlords worry about foreign tenants leaving Japan suddenly, ending a contract early, or being hard to contact. Add a pet and they picture a worst case: the tenant leaves the country, the apartment needs expensive repairs, and there is nobody to bill. This is why your residence period, job stability, and emergency contact matter even more when you have an animal.

Language and communication

Owners want to be sure you understand the pet rules in the contract: where the animal can be walked, whether it can be on the balcony, what to do about noise. If the agent can't explain these to you in English, some owners will simply refuse rather than risk a misunderstanding.

Lease length vs. visa length

A standard lease is two years. If your residence card expires in eight months, the owner may assume you might leave mid-contract, and with a pet the potential damage from a sudden exit is higher.

Common mistakes

  1. Applying to "pets negotiable" listings with no details. Writing just "I have a cat" forces the owner to imagine the worst. Owners approve specific animals, not vague ones.
  2. Hiding the pet. Moving an animal into a no-pets apartment is a contract violation. If discovered, you can face a demand to remove the pet, full restoration charges, and possibly contract termination. It is not worth the risk, especially on a visa where housing stability matters.
  3. Underestimating the larger animal problem. Many pet-allowed buildings accept only small dogs or cats. A medium or large dog, or more than two animals, cuts your options sharply. Searching broadly instead of filtering by size wastes weeks.
  4. Not budgeting for the extra deposit. Pet-allowed apartments commonly ask for an extra month of 敷金 (security deposit), sometimes more, and the pet-related cleaning cost is often non-refundable. Showing up unable to pay this signals risk.
  5. Relying on a single agency. Agencies only see their own listings plus what they choose to search. If one agent says "there's nothing for pets in this area," that is one agent's opinion.

Step-by-step fix

  1. Find out exactly why you were rejected. Ask the agent in simple terms: "Did the owner refuse the pet, or was it the guarantee company?" If it was the guarantor, your problem is your profile, not your pet. Read our breakdown of why foreigners get rejected and the no-guarantor options before applying again.

  2. Search only true pet-allowed listings. Tell agencies you want ペット可 (pets allowed) or ペット相談可 (pets negotiable) properties, and specify your animal type and size up front. On Japanese portals, the filter is usually labeled "ペット相談". Newer buildings marketed as pet-friendly (ペット共生型) sometimes have foot-washing areas or pet doors and are more likely to say yes without long negotiation.

  3. Build a one-page pet profile. Prepare this in Japanese if possible (a translation app is fine for a draft, but have a Japanese speaker check it). Include:

    • Species, breed, age, weight, and whether neutered or spayed
    • A clear photo
    • Vaccination dates (for dogs, the annual rabies vaccination is required in Japan)
    • Training notes: litter-trained, crate-trained, does not bark when alone
    • How long you have lived with the pet without damage, with a reference from a previous landlord if you have one

    Hand this to the agent and ask them to send it to the owner with your application. It turns "a foreigner with a dog" into "a responsible tenant with a calm, vaccinated 5 kg dog."

  4. Offer concrete protection for the owner. Phrases that work well, said politely through the agent:

    • "I can pay an additional month of deposit for the pet."
    • "I will pay professional cleaning and deodorizing at move-out."
    • "I am happy to sign a pet agreement (ペット飼育に関する覚書) with the building rules."

    You don't need to offer everything at once. Start with the extra deposit; it's the most common expectation.

  5. Strengthen your foreign-tenant profile. Bring an employment certificate (在職証明書), recent payslips, and your residence card. If your visa expires within a year, bring proof that renewal is in process or a letter from your employer stating your contract continues. Our visa guide explains which documents landlords look for. Have a reachable emergency contact ready, ideally someone living in Japan.

  6. Use a guarantee company that accepts foreigners. Ask the agent which guarantor company the property uses. Some are known to be stricter with foreign applicants. If the first property's guarantor rejected you, ask whether the next property can use a different company.

  7. Widen the search radius slightly. In Osaka, pet-allowed stock is thin in the most central, high-demand zones. Moving one or two stations out along the same train line often doubles your options with little change in commute time.

  8. Line up a bridge plan if your move date is fixed. If you need to move before you find a pet-friendly place, arrange a temporary pet hotel or a trusted friend to care for your animal, and do not move the pet into a no-pets apartment. If you will be negotiating with agencies by phone and want to explain your pet's details yourself, a few targeted Japanese lessons can help; [PR] italki lets you book a tutor for a short session focused on rental conversations.

  9. Read the pet clauses before you sign. Confirm the allowed number and size of animals, whether the extra deposit is refundable, who pays for wallpaper or floor damage, and whether you must notify the owner if you get another pet later. Our hidden costs breakdown lists move-in fees to check line by line.

Important: If you are bringing a dog or cat into Japan from overseas, the import process through Japan's Animal Quarantine Service can take many months, including microchipping, rabies vaccinations, and a blood test with a waiting period for animals coming from many countries. Start that process long before you start your apartment search, and keep all paperwork, because landlords may also ask to see vaccination records.

Required documents

  • Residence card (在留カード), front and back copy
  • Passport
  • Employment certificate (在職証明書) or job offer letter (内定通知書)
  • Recent payslips (給与明細) or proof of income (課税証明書 if you have lived in Japan for a full tax year)
  • Emergency contact details (緊急連絡先), ideally a person in Japan
  • Pet profile sheet with photo, breed, weight, and age
  • Vaccination certificate (for dogs, the rabies vaccination certificate, 狂犬病予防注射済証)
  • Dog registration record from your city office (犬の登録), if already registered in Japan
  • Animal import/quarantine certificate, if the pet came from overseas
  • Reference from a previous landlord, if available

City / region differences

Osaka. Central wards such as Kita-ku and Chuo-ku have many newer apartment buildings, and some newer towers allow small pets, but rents for pet-allowed units there sit higher than comparable no-pet units. Wards a little further out, such as Yodogawa-ku, Higashinari-ku, or areas along the Midosuji and Tanimachi lines outside the center, tend to offer more ペット相談可 options at more realistic prices. Older wooden buildings are less likely to accept dogs because of noise. For a broader picture of renting in the city as a foreigner, see our Osaka page and the article on early termination penalties in Osaka, which matters if you might need to move out before the lease ends.

Kobe and the Hanshin area. Suburban areas between Osaka and Kobe often have more space and a higher share of family-type housing that accepts pets, which can be worth considering if you have a larger dog and can handle a longer commute.

Kyoto. Strict building rules and a high proportion of older housing in central Kyoto make pet-allowed rentals harder to find than in Osaka. Expect a longer search.

Tokyo comparison. Tokyo has more pet-allowed listings in absolute numbers, but competition and costs are higher. If you are deciding between cities, Osaka can be easier for pet owners on a moderate budget, provided you are flexible about the neighborhood.

Typical extra costs. As a rough range, pet-allowed apartments in the Kansai area may add one extra month of deposit and sometimes a small monthly pet fee, often in the range of a few thousand yen. Some charge a fixed non-refundable cleaning fee instead. Always confirm the exact amounts in writing.

Frequently Asked Questions

Can a landlord in Japan refuse me just because I have a pet?

Yes. Owners can choose whether to allow pets in their property, and "no pets" is a normal, legitimate contract term in Japan. Your best approach is to target pet-allowed listings and make your application as reassuring as possible, rather than trying to change a no-pets owner's mind.

Does "ペット相談可" mean my pet will be accepted?

No. It means the owner is willing to consider it. Approval depends on the animal's type, size, and number, and on your overall profile. A detailed pet profile and an offer of extra deposit significantly improve your chances.

Is it easier to get approved with a cat or a small dog?

In general, small dogs and cats are the most commonly accepted. Some buildings accept small dogs but not cats because of scratching damage, while others are the opposite. Ask the agent to confirm the exact rule for each property before you apply.

Will I get the extra pet deposit back when I move out?

It depends on the contract. Some deposits are partly refundable after deducting cleaning and repair costs; other contracts state a fixed amount that is kept regardless. Read the move-out and restoration clauses carefully, take dated photos on move-in day, and see our tips on deposit disputes if you disagree with deductions later.

Can I keep a pet in a share house in Osaka?

Most share houses do not allow pets because of shared spaces and other residents' allergies. A few operators have pet-friendly properties, but they are rare and fill quickly. If you have a pet, a private apartment is usually the more realistic option.
