Apartment Rejected Because You Applied From Overseas? What to Do Before You Move to Tokyo

Updated November 2026 · GaijinHome

You have your job offer or school acceptance, your flight is booked, and you want a Tokyo apartment ready the day you land. So you contact an agent from abroad, pick a place from photos, send your passport scan, and wait. Then the reply comes back: the application could not be accepted. Sometimes the agent says the landlord "does not take overseas applicants." Sometimes they simply stop replying.

This is one of the most common frustrations for people moving to Japan, and it is not a judgment on you. The standard Japanese rental process is built around people who are already physically in the country, with a residence card, a phone number, and an address. When you apply from outside Japan, you are missing several pieces the system expects, all at once. This article explains what is really going on and gives you a practical plan so you have somewhere safe to sleep on day one and a realistic path to a long-term lease soon after.

What actually happens

When you apply for a regular apartment in Tokyo, the real estate agent forwards your application to the management company (管理会社, kanri gaisha) and, in most cases, a rent guarantee company (保証会社, hoshō gaisha). The guarantee company runs a screening and decides whether it will cover you if you fail to pay rent. In practice, its decision is usually the one that matters.

For an overseas applicant, the process often breaks down at one of these points:

  • Before screening even starts. Many agents will not submit an application at all without a residence card (在留カード, zairyū kādo) number. They may tell you to "contact us again after you arrive."
  • At the guarantee company. The screener cannot call a Japanese mobile number, cannot verify a Japanese address, and cannot confirm income from a Japanese employer. The file is marked incomplete and rejected.
  • At the landlord. Some owners simply prefer not to sign a lease with someone they cannot meet, who has not seen the property in person, and who might cancel if their visa is delayed.
  • At contract time. Even if you are pre-approved, the lease usually cannot be finalized until you can provide the residence card, pay the move-in costs, and sometimes sign documents in person.

Rejections are rarely explained in detail. Guarantee companies generally do not tell agents exactly why they declined, so the message you get is often vague. That is normal, and it does not mean you will be rejected again once you are in Japan.

Why it happens

It helps to understand the structural reasons, because each one points to a specific fix.

The guarantee company needs things you do not have yet

Guarantee companies usually verify identity and contactability with a residence card, a Japanese phone number, and often a phone call to you and to your workplace. If you are abroad, the phone call may be impossible or happen in Japanese at a time you cannot answer. Without a residence card, there is no official record that you are allowed to live in Japan for the length of the lease.

Your visa status is not final until you land

If you are moving on a new visa, you typically receive a Certificate of Eligibility (在留資格認定証明書, zairyū shikaku nintei shōmeisho), then a visa stamp at an embassy, and finally your residence card on arrival at certain major airports. From a landlord's point of view, until you have that card, there is a risk your entry is delayed or denied. They do not want to hold an empty apartment for a tenant who may not come.

Landlords avoid risk they cannot measure

Japanese landlords tend to be cautious. A tenant who is overseas, who has not viewed the unit, and who has no local work history is hard to assess. Rather than take that risk, many owners only accept applicants who can come to the office. This is a business preference, not a legal requirement, which is why some properties do accept overseas applications and others never will.

Income cannot be verified from abroad

Screeners look for Japanese payslips, a tax withholding slip, or a resident tax certificate. An overseas applicant usually has none of these. A job offer letter helps, but it is often treated as weaker evidence. If you are in that situation after arriving, our article on apartments rejected when you only have a job offer letter walks through how to strengthen it.

No emergency contact in Japan

Many applications ask for an emergency contact (緊急連絡先, kinkyū renrakusaki) who lives in Japan and can speak Japanese. Newcomers rarely have one. We cover workarounds in detail in apartment rejected with no emergency contact in Tokyo.

Move-in payments are hard to make from abroad

Move-in costs in Tokyo commonly add up to several months of rent: deposit, key money, agent fee, guarantee fee, first month's rent, insurance, and key exchange. These are usually paid by bank transfer to a Japanese account. International transfers can be slow and expensive, and some agents simply do not want to handle them. See the hidden costs of renting in Japan for a full breakdown.

Common mistakes

  1. Trying to lock in a long-term lease before you arrive. Spending weeks chasing a standard two-year lease from overseas often ends in rejection and wasted time. A short-term bridge is usually the faster route.
  2. Sending a passport scan and nothing else. A bare passport tells the screener almost nothing. If you do apply, send a full package: visa or Certificate of Eligibility, offer or enrollment letter, proof of savings, and a short cover note in simple English or Japanese.
  3. Applying through general listing sites that are not set up for foreigners. Many listings on big Japanese portals are managed by agents who do not handle overseas or English-speaking clients. Your inquiry may simply be ignored.
  4. Paying money before checking who you are paying. Be careful with any "agent" who asks for a large payment to hold an apartment without a clear contract, company name, and registered real estate license number (宅建業免許番号, takkengyō menkyo bangō). Rental scams that target people moving from abroad do exist.
  5. Booking a hotel for months and then panicking. Hotels add up quickly. Plan a bridge stay of a few weeks to a couple of months, and start the long-term search as soon as you have your residence card.

Step-by-step fix

  1. Accept a two-stage plan. Stage one: a furnished place you can book from abroad for your first one to three months. Stage two: a regular lease once you have a residence card, phone number, and address. This removes the pressure to win a standard lease remotely.
  2. Book a bridge stay that accepts overseas reservations. Share houses, serviced apartments, and monthly apartments (マンスリーマンション, mansurī manshon) are designed for people without local documents. Many take payment by credit card and do not require a guarantor. Rent is higher per month than a regular lease, but you avoid key money and most move-in fees. One option many newcomers use is [PR] Oakhouse share houses and furnished apartments, which has English support and is used to people booking from abroad. Our no-guarantor housing page lists other types to compare.
  3. If you still want to try a regular lease from abroad, ask the right question first. Before sending any documents, ask the agent directly: "Do you accept applications from people currently overseas, before they have a residence card?" In Japanese: 「海外在住で、在留カード取得前でも申込できますか？」 (kaigai zaijū de, zairyū kādo shutoku mae demo mōshikomi dekimasu ka?). A clear "no" saves you weeks.
  4. Ask about remote explanation and contract. The legally required explanation of important matters (重要事項説明, jūyō jikō setsumei) can be done online for rentals at many agencies, often called IT重説. Ask whether they offer it in English or with an interpreter. If they do, they are more likely to be set up for overseas clients.
  5. Build a strong overseas application package. Include your passport, Certificate of Eligibility or visa, offer letter with salary and start date (ideally on letterhead, signed, with a company contact in Japan), proof of savings covering several months of rent, and an email address and messaging app you check daily. If your employer will act as a contact or sign as a corporate tenant, say so clearly.
  6. Ask your employer or school for help. Some Tokyo employers lease apartments in the company's name (法人契約, hōjin keiyaku) or have relationships with relocation agents. Universities often have dorms or partner housing. This is often the single most effective fix, so ask early: "Does the company support housing for new hires from overseas?"
  7. Once you land, move fast. Get your residence card at the airport if issued there, register your address at the ward office (区役所, kuyakusho) within the required period, get a Japanese phone number, and open a bank account. With these in hand, you are now a normal domestic applicant. See our full guide to renting in Japan for the standard process.
  8. Reapply with an agent that works with foreigners. Ask for properties that use foreigner-friendly guarantee companies, and say up front that you have just arrived. Be honest about your start date and income. If you are rejected again, read what to do after an apartment rejection before trying the next property.
Never send a deposit or "holding fee" to a personal bank account or an unverified overseas account. A legitimate agent will provide a company name, office address, license number, and a written breakdown of costs before you pay anything.

Required documents

Exact requirements vary by agent and guarantee company, but these are the documents most likely to be requested. Prepare digital copies before you leave home.

  • Passport (パスポート, pasupōto), photo page and visa page
  • Certificate of Eligibility (在留資格認定証明書) if you have one
  • Residence card (在留カード, zairyū kādo), required for most regular leases, obtained on arrival
  • Employment contract or offer letter (雇用契約書 / 内定通知書), showing salary, position, and start date
  • Certificate of enrollment (在学証明書) or acceptance letter, for students
  • Proof of savings, such as recent bank statements in English
  • Japanese phone number (携帯電話番号), after arrival
  • Certificate of residence (住民票, jūminhyō), after registering your address at the ward office
  • Emergency contact (緊急連絡先) details, or the name of a service your agent accepts instead
  • Credit card for bridge-stay bookings and, if accepted, move-in payments

City / region differences

Tokyo has the largest supply of options that accept overseas bookings: share houses, serviced apartments, monthly apartments, and agents with English-speaking staff. Competition for regular apartments in central wards like Shibuya, Minato, and Shinjuku is high, so landlords there can afford to be picky, and overseas applicants are often pushed down the list. Areas further from the center, or along less famous train lines, tend to be more flexible. Bridge-stay rents in Tokyo are usually higher than a regular lease for the same size, often in the range of roughly ¥80,000 to ¥180,000 per month depending on type and area, with share house rooms at the lower end.

Yokohama and Kawasaki are good alternatives if you will commute into Tokyo. Rents are generally a little lower, and some agents are more willing to work with newcomers.

Osaka follows a similar pattern but at lower prices, and has its own set of foreigner-friendly agents. If you are moving there instead, see our Osaka housing page.

Smaller cities often have fewer English-friendly agents and fewer furnished bridge options. If you are moving to a regional city, asking your employer or school for housing support becomes even more important.

Whatever the city, the key rule is the same: the system becomes much more open to you once you have a residence card, a phone number, and a registered address. If you are still sorting out your status of residence, our visa guide explains the steps.

Frequently Asked Questions

Is it illegal for a landlord to reject me because I am overseas?

Generally, no. Landlords and guarantee companies can set their own screening criteria, and requiring the applicant to be in Japan with a residence card is common. It is frustrating, but the practical solution is to choose properties and providers that are set up for overseas applicants rather than to argue with those that are not.

Can I sign a regular two-year lease before I arrive in Japan?

Sometimes, but it is not the norm. Some agents that specialize in foreigners, and some employer-arranged corporate leases, can complete contracts remotely. Most standard leases still require a residence card, so plan for a bridge stay unless your agent clearly confirms otherwise in writing.

How long should my bridge stay be?

Many people book one to three months. That gives you time to get your residence card, register your address, open a bank account, get a phone number, view apartments in person, and complete screening. Booking a little longer than you think you need reduces stress if screening takes time.

Will being rejected from abroad hurt my chances later?

Usually not in any lasting way. The rejection was most likely based on missing documents, not on a negative record about you. Once you reapply from inside Japan with a complete package, you are evaluated as a new applicant.

Can my company in Japan help if I am rejected?

Often, yes. Ask HR whether they can sign as a corporate tenant, provide a Japanese-language employment certificate, act as your emergency contact, or introduce a relocation agent. Even a short letter confirming your start date and salary can make a guarantee company more comfortable.
