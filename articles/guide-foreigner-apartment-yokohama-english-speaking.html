Apartment Rejected in Yokohama as a Foreigner? English-Speaking Fixes That Work

Updated November 2026 · GaijinHome

Yokohama looks like it should be easy for foreign renters. It is a port city with a long international history, a large foreign community, and plenty of companies that bring in overseas staff. So it can be a shock when your application comes back rejected, or when an agent tells you the landlord "does not accept foreign tenants."

The reality is that Yokohama's rental market behaves a lot like Tokyo's on paper, but with more small, locally owned buildings and fewer agencies that routinely work in English. That combination creates specific failure points. Once you understand them, you can work around them.

What Actually Happens

Most Yokohama rejections land at one of three stages:

  • Before you even apply. You find a listing on a portal, ask an agent near the station, and are told the owner will not consider a foreign tenant. This is common with older buildings owned by individual landlords.
  • At guarantor screening. You submit the application, and a few days later the agent calls to say the guarantor company (保証会社) did not approve you. Usually no detailed reason is given.
  • At the landlord's final review. The guarantor company approved you, but the landlord still declined, sometimes after asking about your Japanese ability or how long you plan to stay.

A frequent Yokohama-specific situation: you work in Tokyo, choose Yokohama for lower rent or more space, and apply through a local agency that rarely handles commuters on short-term contracts. The agent is unfamiliar with your employer, and that alone makes them nervous.

Why It Happens

Many small, individual landlords

Away from the big redevelopment areas like Minato Mirai, a lot of Yokohama's rental stock is in small apartment buildings (アパート, apāto) owned by individuals. These owners often decide personally who rents, and some have had a bad experience, or heard about one, involving garbage rules, noise, or a tenant who left the country without notice. They react by avoiding foreign tenants entirely.

Guarantor company screening criteria

Guarantor companies look at income relative to rent, employment stability, visa type and remaining period of stay, and whether they can reach you or your emergency contact. A commonly cited rule of thumb is that monthly rent should be no more than roughly a third of monthly income, though each company sets its own threshold. Short employment history, a period of stay that ends soon after the lease starts, or a missing Japanese emergency contact are typical reasons for refusal.

Lease length versus visa length

Standard leases in Japan run two years (普通借家契約, futsū shakuya keiyaku). If your residence card shows only one year left, some landlords and guarantor companies will hesitate, even though renewals are routine. They are not assessing your likelihood of renewal; they are just seeing a mismatch.

Language and emergency contact

Local agencies in residential Yokohama neighborhoods may not have English-speaking staff. They worry about explaining the contract, handling complaints, and contacting you in an emergency. A Japanese-speaking emergency contact (緊急連絡先) solves a large part of this concern.

Common Mistakes

  1. Applying only through small station-front agencies. These agencies know the local stock well but may rarely work with foreign tenants. Combine them with at least one agency that advertises foreign-resident support.
  2. Choosing rent at the edge of your budget. If rent pushes past what a guarantor company considers comfortable relative to your salary, a small step down in rent can turn a rejection into an approval.
  3. Listing an emergency contact abroad. A parent overseas is not useful to a Yokohama guarantor company. Ask a coworker, manager or friend in Japan instead.
  4. Not explaining your Tokyo commute. If you work in Tokyo, say so clearly and show your employer's details. An agent who does not understand why you are in Yokohama may assume your situation is unstable.
  5. Reapplying to the same building right away. If a landlord has declined, resubmitting rarely changes the outcome. Move on to properties that are already marked as foreigner-friendly.

Step-by-Step Fix

  1. Find out which stage rejected you. Ask your agent directly: "Was it the guarantor company or the landlord that declined?" (「保証会社の審査ですか、それともオーナーさんの判断ですか？」). The fix is completely different for each.
  2. If the guarantor company refused you, change the guarantor company. Agents usually work with several. Ask: "Is there a guarantor company you use that is more familiar with foreign applicants?" Some accept English documents or are more flexible with visa periods. Our guide on what to do when a guarantor refuses you goes deeper.
  3. If the landlord refused you, filter for foreigner-friendly listings. Ask the agent to search only for properties marked 外国人可 or 外国人相談, and to call the management company before arranging a viewing.
  4. Strengthen your application package. Provide a certificate of employment, recent payslips, and if you have been in Japan over a year, your resident tax certificate. If your employer has an HR or relocation contact, ask whether they can write a short letter confirming your position and expected visa renewal.
  5. Offer something that lowers the landlord's risk. Options include paying a higher deposit, prepaying several months of rent, or having your company sign the lease as a corporate contract (法人契約, hōjin keiyaku) if it is willing. Corporate contracts are often approved much more easily.
  6. Use a bridge option if time is short. UR public housing has large estates around Yokohama and does not require a guarantor (see our UR guide). A furnished apartment or monthly mansion can also hold you over while you reapply.
Key tip: In Yokohama, a 10,000 yen drop in monthly rent often matters more than the neighborhood. If guarantor screening is your problem, search one or two stations further out on the same line before you change anything else.

Required Documents

  • Residence card (在留カード), front and back
  • Passport (パスポート)
  • Certificate of employment (在職証明書), showing position, start date and salary
  • Recent payslips (給与明細), typically two or three months
  • Resident tax certificate (住民税課税証明書) or tax payment certificate (納税証明書), if available
  • Certificate of residence (住民票, jūminhyō) from your current city office, if requested
  • Emergency contact (緊急連絡先) in Japan, with name, relationship and phone number
  • Japanese bank account for rent debit, or credit card accepted by the guarantor company
  • For a corporate contract: company registration details provided by your employer

Yokohama Neighborhoods, Rents and Local Quirks

Central Yokohama and Minato Mirai

Areas around Yokohama Station, Minato Mirai, Sakuragicho and Kannai have newer buildings and more management companies used to corporate and foreign tenants. Rents are the highest in the city. As a rough guide, a 1K or 1LDK here can run from around 80,000 yen to well over 150,000 yen depending on size and building. Approval is often easier in large managed buildings than in small private ones.

Yamate, Motomachi and the Bluff

The historic Yamate area has a long association with foreign residents and international schools. Housing tends to be more expensive and family-oriented, and some properties are marketed toward expatriates. It is worth asking agents specifically about this area if you have an expat housing allowance.

Kohoku, Tsuzuki and the Tokyu lines

Kohoku New Town, Center-Kita, Center-Minami, Hiyoshi and Tsunashima are popular with families and Tokyo commuters. There is a mix of newer condos and large estates, including UR properties. Rents vary widely but can offer more space for the money than central Tokyo.

Tsurumi, Kanagawa-ku and Naka-ku's older districts

Tsurumi has a diverse community, including many residents with roots in Latin America and Okinawa, and some local agencies there are comparatively used to foreign tenants. Older areas in Naka-ku and around Chinatown also have a long multicultural history. Rents can be more modest than central areas.

Local quirks

Yokohama is hilly, and "walking distance to the station" on a listing may include steep slopes; always check the route. Garbage sorting rules are set by the city and are detailed; showing an agent that you are aware of them is a small but real trust signal. Frequently Asked Questions

Are there English-speaking real estate agents in Yokohama?

Yes, though fewer than in Tokyo. They tend to be concentrated around Yokohama Station, Minato Mirai and areas with international schools. Many Tokyo-based agencies that serve foreign residents also handle Yokohama properties, so it is worth asking them too.

Is it easier to rent in Yokohama than in Tokyo as a foreigner?

Not necessarily easier, but different. Rents are generally lower, which helps with guarantor screening, but there are more small private landlords who decide case by case. Working with an agent who already knows which owners accept foreigners matters more here.

Can my company sign the lease for me?

Many companies will sign a corporate lease (法人契約) for employees, especially if they offer a housing allowance. Landlords often prefer this because the company is responsible for rent. Ask your HR department whether this is available before you start searching.

What if I have no Japanese emergency contact?

Some guarantor companies offer an option or a paid service in place of an emergency contact, and some are more flexible for foreign applicants. Ask your agent which ones they use. Otherwise, a coworker or manager is usually the easiest person to ask. See our no-guarantor guide for more options.

How much should I budget to move into a Yokohama apartment?

Move-in costs commonly add up to several months of rent once deposit, key money, agent fee, guarantor company fee, first month's rent and insurance are included. Key money is less universal than it used to be; our article on key money explains what it is and when you can negotiate it.
