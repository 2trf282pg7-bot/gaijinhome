Apartment Contract Cancelled Before Move-In in Kobe? What to Do and How to Get Your Money Back

Updated November 2026 · GaijinHome

You found a place in Kobe. Maybe it was a 1K near Sannomiya, a quiet unit in Nada, or something close to your new office on Port Island. You filled in the application, paid an initial amount, and started planning your move. Then, days or even hours before you were supposed to get the keys, the agent calls: the contract is off. Or the reverse happened — your job fell through, your visa timing changed, and now you need to cancel, but the agency says you will lose everything you paid.

Both situations are stressful, especially when the explanation comes in fast Japanese or a vague English email. The good news is that the outcome depends heavily on one question: had the lease actually been signed and concluded, or were you still at the application stage? Once you know that, you can work out what you are owed and how to push back. This article walks you through it step by step.

What actually happens

Renting in Japan usually moves through several stages, and foreigners are often not told clearly which stage they are in:

  1. Application (申込, moushikomi) — you submit a form, ID, and income details. Some agencies ask for an application deposit (申込金, moushikomikin), often a few tens of thousands of yen or one month's rent.
  2. Screening (審査, shinsa) — the landlord and, in most cases, a rent guarantee company review you. This can take from a couple of days to over a week.
  3. Important matters explanation (重要事項説明, juuyou jikou setsumei) — a licensed real estate agent must explain the key terms before the contract. This is often done in Japanese, sometimes online.
  4. Contract signing and payment of move-in costs — deposit, key money, agency fee, guarantee company fee, first month's rent, insurance, and key exchange.
  5. Key handover and move-in.

Cancellations "before move-in" can happen at any point between steps 1 and 5. The common versions are:

  • The landlord or guarantee company pulls out after you thought you were approved — for example, after a second check on your visa, employer, or emergency contact.
  • The agency says the unit was given to another applicant who applied earlier or offered better terms.
  • You need to cancel because your job offer changed, your Certificate of Eligibility was delayed, or you found a better place.
  • The agency tells you that you owe a penalty or that your application deposit, key money, or full move-in costs are non-refundable.

Why it happens

Understanding the structural reasons makes it easier to negotiate calmly instead of feeling personally targeted.

Guarantee company screening is not one single decision

Most landlords now require tenants to use a rent guarantee company (保証会社, hoshou gaisha). Some agents tell applicants "you passed" when only the landlord has agreed in principle, while the guarantee company is still checking. If the guarantee company later declines — because your residence period is short, your employment is new, or they could not reach your emergency contact — the landlord usually withdraws. If guarantor issues are the root problem, our no-guarantor options page explains alternatives.

Landlords are risk-averse about the remaining residence period

A standard Japanese lease is often two years. If your residence card shows only a few months left, or your visa status is "pending," a landlord may get cold feet once they look more closely. This frequently surfaces at the final document check, when the agency asks for a clearer copy of your residence card.

Language and communication gaps

Many smaller Kobe landlords, especially in older buildings, worry about explaining garbage rules, building rules, or emergencies in English. If the agency mentioned at the last minute that you need a Japanese-speaking emergency contact and you could not provide one, that can be the real reason behind a sudden cancellation.

The application is not a contract — but money changes the picture

Until the lease is concluded, either side can usually walk away. An application deposit paid at that stage is generally meant to show serious intent, not to be a fee, and in Japan agents are generally expected to return it if no contract is formed. After the contract is signed, however, cancelling is usually treated as an early termination of a lease that already exists, and the contract's notice-period clause applies even though you never moved in.

Key point: Before arguing about money, confirm in writing whether a lease contract was actually concluded (signed by you, and accepted by the landlord). Your rights before and after that moment are very different.

Common mistakes

  1. Paying move-in costs before receiving the important matters explanation. Some agencies ask for full payment early "to secure the room." If the deal collapses, recovering money becomes harder, and you may not even have written terms showing what each payment was for.
  2. Accepting a phone explanation only. If you are told "the money is non-refundable" verbally, ask for the reason by email. Agencies are often more careful about what they put in writing.
  3. Signing documents you could not read. A cancellation clause, short-term penalty clause, or cleaning fee clause may be buried in the Japanese contract. Ask for a translation or at least a written summary of the cancellation terms before signing.
  4. Not asking for an itemized breakdown. Move-in costs are a bundle: deposit, key money, agency fee, guarantee fee, insurance, key exchange, first rent. Each item has a different refund logic. Treating them as one lump sum makes it easy for the agency to keep everything. Our hidden costs breakdown explains what each item usually covers.
  5. Waiting too long. If you need to cancel, tell the agency as early as possible. Rent clauses usually count from the date your written notice is received, so every day of delay can cost you money.

Step-by-step fix

  1. Find out exactly which stage you were at. Send a short email: "Could you please confirm in writing whether the lease contract was concluded, and on what date?" In Japanese you can add: 「賃貸借契約は成立していますか。成立日を書面で教えてください。」 Keep every email, LINE message, and receipt.
  2. Request an itemized statement of everything you paid. Ask for a breakdown showing each payment — application deposit, deposit (敷金, shikikin), key money (礼金, reikin), agency fee (仲介手数料, chuukai tesuuryou), guarantee company fee, insurance, key exchange, and prorated rent. You want to know which items are being kept and why.
  3. If no contract was concluded, ask for a full refund of the application deposit. A polite but firm message works best: "As no lease contract was concluded, I request the full refund of my application deposit to the following account." Include your bank details. If the agency refuses, ask them to state the reason and the specific document you signed that allows them to keep it.
  4. If the landlord or guarantee company cancelled after you signed, ask for the reason and a full refund. If you did nothing wrong and the other side withdrew after the contract was concluded, you have strong grounds to ask for all move-in costs back. Ask the agency to confirm in writing who cancelled and why. If an approval was clearly communicated to you and you incurred costs (for example, you cancelled another apartment or booked movers), mention that too.
  5. If you are the one cancelling after signing, read the termination clause carefully. Look for the notice period (often one month, sometimes two) and any short-term cancellation penalty. In many cases you will owe rent for the notice period, and key money and the guarantee company fee are often not refunded. The deposit, however, should generally come back minus legitimate deductions — and since you never lived there, there should be little or nothing to deduct for damage or cleaning. Our article on early termination penalties in Kansai covers this calculation in more detail.
  6. Negotiate the agency fee. If the contract never really took effect because of the other side, it is reasonable to ask for the agency fee back. If you cancelled, you can still ask for a partial refund, especially if the agency did not complete the key handover. Be polite: "Since I have not moved in and the keys were not handed over, could you consider refunding part of the agency fee?"
  7. Escalate if needed. If the agency stops responding or refuses without explanation, you can contact the consumer hotline by dialing 188 in Japan, which connects you to your local consumer affairs center (消費生活センター, shouhi seikatsu sentaa). Kobe City and Hyogo Prefecture both run consumer consultation services. Some centers can arrange interpretation or accept a Japanese-speaking friend calling with you. For larger amounts, a lawyer or a legal aid service can advise you.
  8. Find a replacement quickly, ideally one with fewer hurdles. If your move date is fixed, you may not have time to repeat a full screening process. Furnished options with simpler contracts can bridge the gap while you look for a long-term place. Required documents

Whether you are asking for a refund or reapplying elsewhere, have these ready:

  • Residence card (在留カード, zairyuu kaado), front and back, clear copy
  • Passport, photo page and visa or landing stamp
  • Copy of the application form (申込書, moushikomisho) you submitted
  • Important matters explanation document (重要事項説明書, juuyou jikou setsumeisho), if you received one
  • Lease contract (賃貸借契約書, chintaishaku keiyakusho), if signed
  • All receipts and bank transfer records for payments to the agency
  • Emails, LINE messages, or notes of calls showing approval and cancellation
  • Employment contract or job offer letter (雇用契約書 / 内定通知書)
  • Proof of income, such as recent pay slips or a certificate of employment (在職証明書, zaishoku shoumeisho)
  • Japanese bank account details (for refunds) or an alternative account the agency accepts

City / region differences

Kobe and the wider Kansai area

Kobe follows many Kansai rental customs that can confuse newcomers. Historically, parts of Kansai used a "hoshoukin" (保証金) system with a large deposit and a fixed non-refundable deduction known as "shikibiki" (敷引き). Many newer contracts in Kobe now use the more common shikikin and reikin structure, but older buildings may still have shikibiki clauses. If your contract includes one, the deducted portion may be kept even if you cancel before move-in, so check this line specifically. Our Osaka and Kansai renting page explains these terms with examples.

Rents in Kobe are generally lower than in Tokyo. A 1K or 1R near Sannomiya, Motomachi, or Rokkomichi often falls somewhere around ¥50,000–75,000 per month, with older or more distant units cheaper. This means notice-period rent penalties are usually smaller in absolute terms than in central Tokyo, but they can still feel significant on top of moving costs.

Kobe also has a large international community, especially around Kitano, Higashinada, and the Rokko Island area, so some agencies there are used to foreign tenants and may offer English support. It is worth asking whether an agency has handled foreign applicants before you pay any application deposit.

Tokyo, Osaka, and other large cities

In Tokyo, competition for good units is intense, so "the room went to another applicant" is a more common reason for a sudden cancellation. In Osaka, Kansai-style deposit terms are similar to Kobe. In smaller cities, there may be fewer guarantee companies willing to approve foreigners, which makes last-minute guarantee rejections more likely. Wherever you are, the stage-based logic above still applies: no concluded contract usually means no penalty, while a signed contract brings the termination clause into play.

Frequently Asked Questions

Can the landlord cancel my apartment after approving me?

Before a lease contract is concluded, a landlord can usually still decline, even if an agent told you informally that you were approved. After the contract is signed and concluded, the landlord generally cannot simply cancel without a valid reason. If this happens, ask for the reason in writing and request a full refund of everything you paid.

Will I get my application deposit back if I cancel?

If no lease contract was concluded, the application deposit is generally expected to be returned. If an agency refuses, ask them to show the signed document that says otherwise, and contact the consumer hotline (188) if they still refuse.

I signed the contract but never moved in. Do I still have to pay rent?

Often, yes, for the notice period written in the contract, usually one or two months from when your written cancellation is received. Key money and guarantee fees are commonly not refunded, but your deposit should generally be returned with little or no deduction because you never used the unit.

The agency only explained the cancellation by phone in Japanese. What should I do?

Reply by email asking them to confirm the cancellation, the reason, and what will happen to each payment. Written records protect you if you need help later from a consumer center or lawyer.

How can I avoid this happening again?

Ask the agency to confirm when both the landlord and the guarantee company have approved you, avoid paying full move-in costs before the important matters explanation, and prepare a clear residence card copy, income proof, and emergency contact in advance. Furnished, no-brokerage-fee options can also reduce the number of approvals you need.
