Phone Contract Rejected in Japan as a Foreigner? Why It Happens and What to Do Next

Updated November 2026 · GaijinHome

You walked into a carrier shop with your residence card, waited an hour, filled in a stack of forms in a language you barely read, and then the staff member came back with an apologetic bow and a short answer: the contract cannot be approved. Maybe they did not explain why. Maybe they said something vague about "screening" (審査, shinsa) and left it at that.

This is one of the most frustrating early experiences for foreigners in Japan, because so much else depends on having a Japanese phone number. Banks want one for verification codes. Real estate agents want one on your rental application. Employers, city offices, delivery services, and even some apps ask for it. When the phone contract fails, it can feel like the whole setup process is stuck.

The good news: a rejected phone contract is almost never a permanent block. In most cases the rejection is tied to one specific part of the application, usually the device installment plan, your payment method, or a mismatch in your documents. Once you know which part failed, there is nearly always another route to a working number, often within the same week.

What actually happens

A Japanese mobile contract is really two or three separate things bundled together, and each one can be approved or refused on its own:

  • The line itself (回線契約, kaisen keiyaku): the phone number and monthly plan. This requires identity verification under Japan's mobile phone anti-fraud rules, so the carrier must confirm who you are and where you live.
  • The device purchase: if you buy a phone on a 24-month or 48-month installment plan (分割払い, bunkatsu barai), that is effectively a consumer credit agreement. It goes through a separate credit screening, often checked against a credit bureau.
  • The payment method: the carrier needs a way to charge you every month, usually a credit card or direct debit from a Japanese bank account.

Shop staff often describe any failure as "the screening did not pass," even when only one piece failed. That is why the answer can feel random. Someone you know with the same visa got approved last month, and you did not, because they bought their phone outright and you tried to finance yours.

Typical outcomes after a rejection include:

  • The line is approved, but the installment plan is refused, so you are asked to pay the full device price upfront.
  • The whole application is refused at the counter because a document did not match.
  • An online application stalls at identity verification and is cancelled after a few days.
  • The application is refused because the payment card you offered cannot be used for monthly billing.

Why it happens

Carriers rarely give detailed reasons, but the structural causes are well understood. Most rejections come down to one of these.

1. Your remaining period of stay is short

Carriers look at the expiry date on your residence card (在留カード, zairyu card). An installment plan usually runs 24 months or longer. If your status of residence expires before the plan would be paid off, many carriers will either refuse the installment, shorten it to match your visa, or ask you to pay the device in full. Some carriers also restrict new lines when only a few months remain on your card. This is the same logic landlords use when your lease is longer than your visa, and it is not personal. If you have recently renewed, make sure the card you show has the new expiry date. The visa guide explains how status of residence affects contracts more generally.

2. No credit history in Japan

For the installment plan, the carrier checks your record with a Japanese credit bureau. A brand-new arrival has no record at all. A thin file is not the same as a bad file, but some screenings treat "no history" cautiously, especially combined with a short visa or a new job. Your excellent credit score back home does not transfer.

3. Past unpaid bills or a previous line left open

Japanese carriers share information about unpaid mobile bills with each other. If you previously had a phone in Japan, left the country, and a final bill or remaining device installments went unpaid, that can block new contracts at other carriers too, not just the original one. This catches people who came back to Japan after a few years away and forgot about a cancelled line.

4. Document mismatch

Identity checks are strict and literal. Common problems:

  • The address on the back of your residence card has not been updated after you moved.
  • Your name on the application is spelled differently from the card (middle names, order of names, missing characters).
  • Your credit card is in a slightly different name format from your residence card.
  • You are using a temporary address, such as a hotel or a friend's place, that is not registered at city hall.

5. Payment method problems

Many carriers and sub-brands do not accept foreign-issued credit cards for monthly billing, or accept them only for some plans. Prepaid cards and many debit cards are commonly refused. If you do not yet have a Japanese bank account, you may be stuck in the classic loop: the bank wants a phone number, and the phone carrier wants a bank account or Japanese card.

6. Language and risk avoidance at the counter

Some shops are cautious with customers they cannot explain the contract to clearly. Staff are responsible for confirming that you understood the terms, and if communication is difficult, a busy store may simply decline rather than risk a complaint later. This is less a formal rule than a practical habit, and it varies a lot from shop to shop.

Important: Do not apply at several carriers in a row on the same day after a credit refusal. Each installment application can leave a record, and multiple rapid applications can make your next screening look worse. Fix the cause first, then apply again.

Common mistakes

  1. Trying to finance a flagship phone in your first month. The single most common reason for rejection is the device installment, not the line. A new arrival with no Japanese credit history and a one-year visa asking for a 48-month plan is the hardest case to approve.
  2. Applying before updating your address. If you moved, register the new address at your city or ward office first so it is printed on the back of your residence card. A mismatch is an instant refusal in many shops.
  3. Assuming your home credit card will work. Ask about payment methods before you sit down for an hour of paperwork. Have a backup plan if your foreign card is refused.
  4. Leaving an old line unpaid when you left Japan before. If you had a Japanese phone in the past, settle anything outstanding before applying again.
  5. Going only to the big three carrier shops. Sub-brands and budget carriers (MVNOs) often have simpler screening and online applications, especially if you bring your own phone.

Step-by-step fix

  1. Ask which part failed. Politely ask the staff: "Was the line refused, or only the installment?" In Japanese: 「回線がだめですか、それとも分割払いだけですか？」 (Kaisen ga dame desu ka, soretomo bunkatsu barai dake desu ka?). This one question tells you which path to take next.
  2. If only the installment failed, separate the phone from the contract. Buy an unlocked phone outright (new or used, from an electronics store or online), or keep using your phone from home if it is unlocked and supports Japanese bands. Then apply for a SIM-only plan. SIM-only contracts skip device credit screening entirely, and they are usually much cheaper per month.
  3. Fix your documents. Update your address at the ward office, check that your name is written exactly as on your residence card, and make sure your card has not expired or is not about to. If you renewed your visa, bring the new card.
  4. Sort out payment. Opening a Japanese bank account solves most payment issues, because direct debit is widely accepted. If the bank is refusing you because you do not have a phone number yet, see why bank accounts get rejected and what to do. Japan Post Bank and some online banks are common first accounts. Once you have a Japanese card or account, reapply.
  5. Get a temporary number or data connection while you wait. A data eSIM or prepaid SIM keeps you online for maps, messaging apps, and email while you sort out the main contract. Note that many data-only products do not include a regular Japanese voice number, so they help you get by but may not satisfy a bank or landlord who wants a phone number.   6. Try a sub-brand or budget carrier with your own unlocked phone. These usually have online applications where you upload photos of your residence card. Choose a plan with a voice number if you need it for banking or rentals, not data-only.
  7. Bring a Japanese-speaking friend or use a shop with English support. Large stores in central city areas are more likely to have staff used to foreign customers. Ask before you start: 「英語で説明できる方はいますか？」 (Is there anyone who can explain in English?).
  8. If you have an old unpaid account, clear it first. Contact the old carrier, pay the outstanding amount, and keep the receipt. Then wait a little before applying elsewhere, because records may take some time to update.

A working phone number also matters for your housing search. Rental applications and guarantor companies almost always ask for one, and a missing number can delay or derail an application. Required documents

  • Residence card (在留カード, zairyu card) with your current address on the back and an expiry date well in the future
  • Passport (some shops ask for it as a backup or to check your name spelling)
  • Payment method: Japanese bank cash card and bank book (通帳, tsucho) or account details, or a credit card in your own name that the carrier accepts
  • Certificate of residence (住民票, juminhyo) if your residence card address is handwritten or recently changed and the shop asks for extra proof
  • Your unlocked phone, if applying for a SIM-only plan, with the IMEI ready in case it is needed
  • Your personal seal (印鑑, inkan) is rarely required for phones, but bring it if you have one, since some bank account setups at the same time may ask for it

City / region differences

Tokyo has the most options by far. Big electronics stores near major stations often have counters for several carriers in one place, and some staff are used to foreign customers. Competition also means more SIM-only promotions. The downside is long waits on weekends, so go on a weekday if you can.

Osaka is similar in central areas such as Umeda and Namba, with many carrier shops and large electronics stores. English support exists but is less consistent than central Tokyo. If you are also renting there, see the Osaka renting page.

Yokohama, Nagoya, Kyoto, Kobe, and Fukuoka all have carrier shops around main stations, and Fukuoka and Kyoto see many international students, so some shops near universities handle foreign applicants often. Outside the city centres, expect fewer English-speaking staff.

Rural areas may have only one or two carrier shops, and staff may have little experience with residence cards and foreign payment methods. In those cases, an online application for a sub-brand or budget carrier, with your own unlocked phone, is often the fastest route. The rules for identity checks and credit screening are the same nationwide; what changes is how comfortable the shop is with handling your case.

Frequently Asked Questions

Can I get a phone contract in Japan on a short-term visa?

Standard postpaid contracts generally require a residence card, which short-term visitors do not receive. Tourists and short-stay visitors usually rely on prepaid SIMs, data eSIMs, or pocket WiFi rentals instead. If you have a residence card but only a few months left on it, a SIM-only plan with your own phone is far more likely to be approved than a device installment.

Why was my installment refused but my friend's was approved?

Installment screening depends on your credit record, income situation, remaining visa period, and the amount being financed. Two people with the same nationality and visa type can get different results. Buying the device outright and choosing a SIM-only plan avoids this screening altogether.

Will a phone contract rejection affect my apartment application?

A refused phone contract itself is not generally something landlords can see. However, unpaid phone bills or unpaid device installments can show up in credit records, and guarantor companies may check those. More practically, not having a Japanese phone number can slow down a rental application. See renting without a guarantor if you are dealing with both problems at once.

Can I use my phone from home in Japan?

Often yes, if it is unlocked and supports the frequency bands used by your chosen carrier. Check the carrier's compatibility list before signing up. Using your own phone also removes the device installment from the application, which is the most common reason for rejection.

How long should I wait before reapplying?

If the cause was a document problem, you can reapply as soon as it is fixed. If it was a credit-related refusal, avoid rapid repeat applications. Fix the cause, switch to a SIM-only plan if possible, and apply once with a carrier whose screening fits your situation.
