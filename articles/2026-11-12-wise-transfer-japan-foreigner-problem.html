Wise Transfer Stuck or Refused in Japan? What to Do When Your Money Will Not Move

Updated November 2026 · GaijinHome

You set up a Wise transfer to pay rent, send savings home, or move your salary from a Japanese bank to an account overseas. Then nothing happens. The status sits on "verifying" for days, an email asks for documents you thought you already sent, the transfer gets cancelled and refunded, or your Japanese bank calls you asking questions in rapid Japanese about where the money came from. For a foreign resident living paycheck to paycheck between two countries, a stuck transfer is not a minor annoyance. It can mean a late rent payment, a missed loan installment back home, or a deposit that does not arrive before a move-in date.

The good news is that most Wise problems in Japan come from a small number of predictable causes. Japan has stricter rules on international money transfers than many countries, and both Wise and Japanese banks are required to follow them. Once you understand which check your transfer is failing, the fix is usually a document upload, a phone call, or splitting the payment differently. This article walks through what is happening and exactly what to do.

What actually happens

Foreign residents in Japan usually run into one of these situations when using Wise:

  • Account verification never finishes. You signed up, uploaded your residence card, and Wise keeps asking for more, or says your address could not be confirmed.
  • Wise asks for your My Number. You try to send money out of Japan and the app requires your Individual Number (マイナンバー). If you do not have the card or the notification document handy, the transfer cannot proceed.
  • The transfer is too large. You try to send a big amount, such as several months of savings or a lump sum for a deposit, and Wise blocks it or tells you to follow a separate process for large amounts.
  • Your Japanese bank holds the money. You paid Wise by bank transfer from your Japanese account, or money is arriving into your Japanese account from abroad, and the bank pauses it until you explain the purpose.
  • The transfer is cancelled and refunded. Often because the name on the paying bank account does not match your Wise account name, or because the payment reference was missing.
  • Your balance hits a limit. Wise accounts for Japan residents have holding limits, so money you planned to "park" in your Wise balance may be bounced or need to be sent out.

None of these mean you did anything wrong. They are compliance checkpoints, and each one has a specific way through.

Why it happens

Japan treats cross-border money movement carefully, and several structural reasons combine to make foreigners hit friction more often than Japanese nationals.

Japanese remittance rules are strict

In Japan, services like Wise operate as registered funds transfer service providers rather than banks. That category comes with rules on identity verification, record keeping, and how much can be sent in one transaction. Wise publishes separate help pages for people living in Japan explaining holding limits and a separate process for sending large amounts. A transfer that would be routine from the UK or US may need extra steps when it starts in Japan.

My Number is tied to overseas transfers

Japanese financial institutions are generally required to collect your My Number for international remittances so they can be reported to the tax authorities. Wise follows the same expectation for Japan residents sending money abroad. Many foreigners never picked up their physical My Number card, lost the paper notification, or moved and never updated it, so this step stops them cold.

Address verification depends on your residence card

Japanese identity checks lean heavily on the address printed on the back of your residence card (在留カード). If you moved and the new address was not written on the card at your ward office, or if the address you typed into Wise is formatted differently (building name, room number, romanization), automated checks may fail.

Banks are cautious about unusual patterns

Japanese banks watch for fraud and money laundering, and accounts held by foreign residents can attract extra attention, especially newer accounts, accounts close to the residence card expiry date, or accounts suddenly receiving or sending larger amounts. A bank may call you, send a letter, or temporarily restrict the account until you confirm the purpose. If this escalates, see our article on what to do when a bank account is frozen in Japan.

Language and contact details

Bank calls and letters are usually in Japanese. If you miss a confirmation call because the number was unknown, or cannot understand a letter, the hold simply stays in place. Wise also sends codes by SMS, so if your phone number changed or you rely on a foreign number with poor reception in Japan, verification can stall.

Common mistakes

  1. Paying from someone else's bank account. Wise generally expects the money to come from an account in your own name. Using a partner's, friend's, or company account often triggers a cancellation and refund, which can take days.
  2. Leaving out the payment reference. When you pay Wise by domestic bank transfer, you may be asked to include a reference code. Japanese bank apps sometimes cut or convert this field into katakana. If the reference is missing or garbled, Wise may not be able to match your payment.
  3. Sending one huge transfer at the last minute. Trying to send a deposit, key money, and first month's rent all at once, a day before your deadline, leaves no buffer if a large transfer review kicks in.
  4. Using an outdated address or residence card. An expired card, or an old address in your Wise profile, is one of the most common reasons for failed verification.
  5. Ignoring the bank's phone call. Many people let unknown Japanese numbers go to voicemail. If that call was the bank asking about your transfer, the money stays frozen until you call back.

Step-by-step fix

  1. Read the exact status in the Wise app. Open the transfer and note whether it says it is waiting for your money, verifying you, requesting documents, or cancelled. Each status points to a different problem. Check your email, including spam, for messages from Wise that list what they need.
  2. Update your residence card and address first. If you moved, visit your ward or city office and have the new address recorded on your residence card. Then update your address in Wise so it matches the card exactly, including building name and room number. If your card is near expiry, renew it at immigration before trying again.
  3. Prepare your My Number. Find your My Number card or the notification document. If you lost it, ask at your ward office about reissue or obtaining a resident record (住民票) that includes your My Number. Upload it in Wise when prompted.
  4. Make sure you can receive SMS codes. Wise and many Japanese banks rely on SMS for login and verification. If you do not have a stable Japanese number, a data plan with a local number avoids repeated lockouts. [PR] One option is JAPAN&GLOBAL eSIM, which can be set up without visiting a store.
  5. Pay only from an account in your own name. If a transfer was refunded because of a name mismatch, set up a new transfer and pay from your own Japanese account. Check how your name appears in the bank's records, since romanized and katakana names can differ, and make sure your Wise profile name matches your legal name on the residence card.
  6. Include the reference correctly. When paying Wise by bank transfer, enter the reference exactly as shown. If your bank app only allows katakana or truncates the field, contact Wise support before sending and ask how to handle it.
  7. Split or plan large transfers. If you need to send a large sum, check Wise's help page for people living in Japan about sending large amounts. Follow that process, prepare documents showing where the money came from (salary slips, a sale contract, a savings statement), and start several days ahead of any deadline.
  8. Call your bank back and explain the purpose clearly. If your Japanese bank paused a payment, call the number on the letter or the branch. Simple phrases help: "kaigai sōkin no kakunin desu" (I am calling to confirm an overseas transfer) and "kyūryō desu" (it is my salary) or "yachin no shiharai desu" (it is a rent payment). If your Japanese is limited, ask whether they have an English line or bring a Japanese-speaking friend to the branch.
  9. If rent is due, tell your landlord early. Contact your management company or guarantor company before the due date, explain that the payment is delayed due to a transfer check, and give an expected date. Being proactive matters a lot in Japan, and many companies are more flexible when you communicate first.
  10. Escalate with Wise support if stuck. If a transfer has been under review for longer than the app estimates, contact Wise through the app's help section, share the transfer ID, and attach any documents they asked for in one message so the case is not split.
If a stuck transfer was meant to pay an apartment deposit or move-in costs, do not assume the landlord will wait. Ask the agent in writing how long they can hold the room, and send whatever amount you can through your Japanese bank directly. Move-in costs in Japan often add up to several months of rent, so read our breakdown of hidden apartment costs in Japan before you plan the transfer.

Required documents

Have these ready before contacting Wise or your bank. Keep clear photos on your phone as well.

  • Residence card (在留カード), front and back, with your current address
  • My Number card (マイナンバーカード) or notification document (通知カード), or a resident record (住民票) showing your My Number
  • Passport
  • Bank cash card or passbook (通帳) for the Japanese account you pay from
  • Proof of source of funds for large transfers: salary slips (給与明細), a tax withholding certificate (源泉徴収票), or bank statements
  • Proof of the transfer purpose if asked: lease contract (賃貸契約書), invoice, or tuition notice
  • A phone number that can receive SMS codes

City / region differences

Wise itself works the same across Japan, but the surrounding steps do not.

Tokyo: Ward offices in central areas often have English support or interpretation services, which makes updating your residence card address or getting a resident record with My Number easier. Major banks in Tokyo are also more likely to have staff used to foreign customers asking about overseas transfers.

Osaka, Nagoya, Yokohama, Kobe: Large cities usually have international help desks at city offices, but bank branches outside the central business districts may have less English support. Calling ahead or visiting a main branch can save time. If you are setting up life in Osaka, our Osaka renting page covers local housing basics.

Kyoto and Fukuoka: Both have active international communities and support centers, but office hours are limited and appointments may be needed. Plan document errands for weekdays.

Rural areas: Regional banks and Japan Post Bank branches in smaller towns may have little experience with Wise or overseas transfers. Expect more questions and slower responses, and give yourself extra lead time for anything with a deadline.

Frequently Asked Questions

Why does Wise ask for my My Number when I send money from Japan?

Japanese rules generally require financial services to collect the My Number of people sending money overseas for tax reporting. Wise applies this to Japan residents. Without it, you usually cannot send money out of Japan through Wise, so having your card or a resident record with your number is essential.

My Wise transfer was cancelled and refunded. Is my account in trouble?

Usually not. The most common reasons are paying from an account not in your name, a missing reference, or an incomplete verification. Fix the specific issue, then start a new transfer. Refunds back to a Japanese bank account can take a few business days.

Can I send a large amount, like a full deposit or my savings, in one Wise transfer?

There are limits for people living in Japan, and Wise has a separate process and guidance for large amounts. You may need to split the payment or provide proof of where the money came from. Start early and check the Wise help pages for Japan residents before you send.

My Japanese bank called about my transfer. What should I say?

Call back as soon as you can and explain simply what the money is for, such as salary, rent, or savings. They are confirming the purpose, not accusing you. If you cannot handle the call in Japanese, ask for English support or visit the branch with your residence card and passbook.

Will a delayed transfer affect my apartment contract?

A single delay that you communicate early is rarely a serious problem. Contact your management company or guarantor company before the due date, explain the reason, and confirm when payment will arrive. Repeated unexplained late payments are what cause trouble with guarantor companies.
