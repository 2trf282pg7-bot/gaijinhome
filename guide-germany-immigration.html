Moving from Japan to Germany: What Expats Actually Face

📅 Updated May 2026 ⏱ 12 min read Visa + Bureaucracy + Housing

Why Germany Is Different from Japan

Japan and Germany both have reputations for precision, punctuality, and thorough paperwork — but the similarities end there. German bureaucracy runs on an entirely different logic than Japan’s. The Bürgeramt (resident registration office), the Ausländerbehörde (foreigner authority), and the Einwohnermeldeamt (population registration office) each have their own rules, appointment systems, and documentation standards. Missing one document can mean a weeks-long delay, and most offices do not accept walk-ins.

This guide is written specifically for Japanese nationals planning to relocate to Germany — whether for work, study, partnership, or permanent settlement. It covers the visa types that actually apply to you, the registration process that catches almost everyone off guard, and the practical housing and financial steps you’ll need to clear before you feel settled.

⚠ Key Reality Check

Germany does not have a working holiday visa agreement with Japan. The options available to Japanese nationals are primarily job seeker visas, skilled worker visas, student visas, and family reunification visas. Tourist visa stays (visa-free for Japanese) are capped at 90 days within any 180-day period.

Visa Types: What Actually Applies to Japanese Nationals

Germany offers several visa pathways for non-EU nationals. Here is what is realistically available to someone coming from Japan:

  • Skilled Worker Visa (Fachkräftezuwanderungsgesetz): Requires a German employer and recognition of your qualifications. The most straightforward route if you have a job offer. Credential recognition (Anerkennung) can take 3–6 months.
  • Job Seeker Visa: Allows you to stay in Germany for up to 6 months while looking for work. Requires proof of qualifications, sufficient funds (~€5,000–€8,000), and health insurance.
  • Student Visa: For enrollment in a German university or language school. Requires an admission letter, proof of financing (blocked account of ~€11,208/year), and health insurance.
  • Family Reunification Visa: If you have a German spouse or registered partner. Language requirements (A1 German) apply in most cases.
  • EU Blue Card: For university graduates with a job offer meeting the minimum salary threshold (€45,300 for most sectors in 2026). Leads to permanent residency faster than a standard skilled worker visa.

The Anmeldung: Germany’s Most Underestimated Bureaucratic Step

Within two weeks of moving into a German apartment, you are legally required to register your address at the local Einwohnermeldeamt or Bürgeramt. This is called the Anmeldung. Without it, you cannot open a bank account, receive official mail, register a car, or in many cases even sign an employment contract.

1

Secure a Wohnungsgeberbestätigung

Your landlord must provide a signed confirmation form stating that you are living at the address. Without this, the Bürgeramt will not register you. Ask for it explicitly before your appointment.

2

Book an Anmeldung Appointment

In large cities like Berlin, Munich, and Hamburg, appointment slots fill up weeks in advance. Book online as soon as you have a confirmed address. Bring your passport, the landlord confirmation form, and the completed Anmeldeformular.

3

Receive Your Meldebescheinigung

After registration, you’ll receive a Meldebescheinigung (certificate of registration). This document is essential — you’ll need copies for everything from opening a bank account to registering with a Krankenkasse (health insurer). Make multiple photocopies.

4

Register with the Ausländerbehörde

Non-EU nationals must also register with the Ausländerbehörde (foreigner authority) to receive their Aufenthaltstitel (residence permit). Bring your passport, Meldebescheinigung, biometric photos, proof of health insurance, proof of income, and your employment or enrollment documents.

Visa & Administrative Procedures: Where Expats Lose Time

For expats navigating the German registration and visa process, germany-solved.vercel.app provides step-by-step guides tailored to non-EU nationals dealing with Anmeldung, Ausländerbehörde appointments, and residence permit applications — including specific pitfalls for Japanese applicants.

Common time-loss points for Japanese expats:

  • Credential recognition delays: Japanese university degrees are generally recognized, but the formal Anerkennung process can take several months. Start this before you arrive.
  • Apostille requirements: Official Japanese documents (birth certificates, marriage certificates) must be apostilled at Japan’s Ministry of Foreign Affairs before German authorities will accept them.
  • Language barrier at government offices: Staff at local Bürgeramt and Ausländerbehörde offices often speak only German. Bring a German-speaking friend or hire an interpreter for complex appointments.
  • Blocked account (Sperrkonto): For student visas and job seeker visas, a blocked account demonstrating sufficient funds is required. Services like Fintiba or Expatrio specialize in this for expats.

Finding an Apartment in Germany as a Non-EU National

The German rental market — particularly in Berlin, Munich, Frankfurt, and Hamburg — is highly competitive. As a non-EU national without a German credit history, landlords typically request: SCHUFA credit report, proof of income (minimum 3× net monthly rent), last 3 months of bank statements, Mietschuldenfreiheitsbescheinigung, and passport plus visa/residence permit.

Without SCHUFA history, consider offering a higher deposit (up to 3 months’ rent is legal in Germany) or bringing a guarantor. Furnished short-term apartments or co-living spaces are a practical option while building your administrative footprint during the first few months.

Health Insurance: A Legal Requirement, Not Optional

Germany has mandatory health insurance (Krankenversicherungspflicht). You must have coverage before you can complete your Ausländerbehörde registration. If you are employed below the income threshold (~€69,300 in 2026), you will be enrolled in statutory health insurance (GKV) automatically. Otherwise, private health insurance (PKV) applies.
