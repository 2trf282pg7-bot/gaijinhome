Real Estate Agents Refusing Foreigners in Tokyo? Finding English-Speaking Help That Works

You found a listing you like, walked into the agency or sent an inquiry, and got a polite "this property is not available for foreigners," or no reply at all. Then it happened again at the next agency. By the third or fourth time, it is easy to conclude that Tokyo simply does not want to rent to you.

That is not the full picture. Thousands of foreign residents sign leases in Tokyo every month. The difference is usually not luck. It is which agent they work with and how they approach that agent. This guide is about the agent side of the problem: why front-line agencies turn you away, and how to find someone who will actually work your case.

What Actually Happens

In Tokyo, the agency you contact is often not the agency that controls the property. Most listings are shared between agencies through industry databases. The agency you walk into (the "intermediary" or 仲介, chūkai) has to call the landlord's management company (管理会社, kanri gaisha) to ask whether a foreign applicant is acceptable.

So a typical refusal looks like this:

  • You ask about a property. The agent says they will "check." Ten minutes later they come back and say it is not possible.
  • The listing has a note such as 外国人不可 (foreigners not accepted) or 外国人相談 (foreigners: consult), and the agent stops there.
  • An agent who does not speak English simply declines to take you on because a lease explanation in English would take hours.
  • You send English emails to several agencies from a portal site and get no replies.

In each case, the agent is reacting to the cost of handling your application, not to a careful assessment of you.

Why It Happens

The agent gets paid only if a contract closes

Intermediary agents earn a commission, commonly up to around one month's rent, only when the lease is signed. If an agent thinks your application has a high chance of being rejected by the landlord or guarantor company, it is rational for them to spend their time on an easier customer. You are being triaged.

The legally required explanation is in Japanese

Before a lease is signed, a licensed real estate transaction agent (宅地建物取引士, takuchi tatemono torihikishi) must explain the important matters of the contract (重要事項説明, jūyō jikō setsumei). For a non-Japanese speaker, agents worry about whether you really understood it, and whether misunderstandings will turn into disputes later. Agencies without English-capable licensed staff often avoid the situation entirely.

Landlords and management companies set the real rules

Even a willing agent cannot override a landlord who has instructed the management company not to accept foreign tenants, or one who only accepts foreign tenants with a Japanese-speaking guarantor. The agent is just the messenger.

Guarantor company screening

Most Tokyo rentals now require a guarantor company (保証会社). Some of these companies are more familiar with foreign applicants and accept English documents or a non-Japanese emergency contact; others are not. Agents know which ones tend to approve foreigners, and an agent who mainly works with stricter companies will expect you to fail.

Common Mistakes

  1. Starting with a property instead of an agent. Falling for a listing and chasing it from agency to agency wastes time if the landlord behind it does not accept foreigners. Choose the agent first, then let them filter properties.
  2. Mass-emailing portal listings in English. Many small agencies cannot reply in English and will just ignore the message. A few targeted inquiries to agencies that advertise English support work far better.
  3. Arriving without documents. Showing up with only a passport signals that screening will be slow and uncertain. Agents prioritize customers who are ready to apply today.
  4. Not disclosing your budget and visa upfront. Agents need to know your rent ceiling, period of stay and employer to know which landlords to call. Vague answers make you look like a risky lead.
  5. Taking a single "no" as the final word. One agency's refusal means that agency could not or would not do it, not that no one will.

Step-by-Step Fix

  1. Shortlist agencies that explicitly support foreign residents. Look for agencies that publish English pages, list staff languages, and mention working with foreign tenants. Neighborhoods with large international populations tend to have more of them. Aim for three to five, not twenty.
  2. Send a complete introduction in one message. Include your nationality, visa type and period of stay, employer or school, monthly income, move-in date, budget, preferred areas and whether you have a Japanese emergency contact. Attach photos of your residence card and certificate of employment. This lets the agent decide quickly that you are worth their time.
  3. Ask the agent the right questions. "Which guarantor companies do you usually use for foreign tenants?" "Can the important-matters explanation be supported in English, or can I bring someone to interpret?" "Can you pre-filter properties that accept foreigners before we view?" Good answers to these tell you whether this agent can actually help.
  4. Ask for 外国人可 or 外国人相談 properties only. Requesting properties already flagged as foreigner-friendly saves both of you rejected calls. In Japanese: 「外国人可の物件だけで探していただけますか？」.
  5. Bring a Japanese speaker for the contract if needed. A coworker, HR contact or friend who can sit in on the important-matters explanation removes the agent's biggest worry. Some companies' HR departments will help, so ask.
  6. Consider routes that skip the intermediary. Public UR housing has no guarantor requirement and no agent commission, and it accepts foreign residents who meet its income or savings rules (see our UR housing guide). Furnished apartment operators that rent directly to foreigners are another option: [PR] CrossOneRoom furnished Tokyo apartments is one example.
  7. Prepare for the guarantor step. Once you have an agent, the next hurdle is the guarantor company. Read what to do if a guarantor refuses you before you apply, so you can react quickly.
Key tip: Judge an agent by the first reply. If they answer your detailed introduction with specific properties and a named guarantor company, keep them. If they reply with generic links or ask for information you already sent, move on.

Required Documents

Bring these to your first meeting so the agent can start screening immediately:

  • Residence card (在留カード), front and back
  • Passport (パスポート)
  • Certificate of employment (在職証明書) or offer letter stating salary and start date
  • Recent payslips (給与明細), usually the last two or three months
  • Resident tax certificate (住民税課税証明書) if you have lived in Japan more than a year
  • Student ID (学生証) and proof of funds, for students
  • Emergency contact (緊急連絡先) in Japan, ideally a Japanese speaker
  • Japanese phone number and bank account, or credit card for the guarantor company
  • Personal seal (印鑑) if you have one; many agencies accept a signature from foreign tenants

Tokyo: Where to Look and What to Expect

Neighborhoods with more English-capable agencies

Agencies used to foreign clients cluster in areas where international residents and companies are concentrated: Minato (Roppongi, Azabu, Hiroo), Shibuya and Ebisu, Meguro, and parts of Shinjuku. Areas with large international communities like Takadanobaba, Shin-Okubo and the Ikebukuro area also have agencies that handle foreign tenants routinely, often with staff who speak Chinese, Korean, Vietnamese or Nepali in addition to English.

Rough rent ranges

Prices vary heavily by station, building age and walking distance. As a rough guide, a small one-room or 1K in central wards commonly runs from around 80,000 to 130,000 yen, while areas further out on the same train lines, such as parts of Nerima, Itabashi, Adachi or across the river in Saitama and Kawasaki, can be noticeably cheaper. Premium international-style apartments in Minato can be far higher. Budget for move-in costs as well; see hidden costs of renting in Japan.

How Tokyo compares with Osaka

Tokyo has more English-speaking agencies than anywhere else in Japan, but also more competition and higher rents, so agents can afford to be choosy. In Osaka, fewer agencies advertise English, but some foreign residents find landlords there more flexible once they get in front of the right agent. Frequently Asked Questions

Is it legal for a Tokyo agent to refuse foreigners?

Japan does not have a broad anti-discrimination law that clearly covers private rentals in the way some countries do, and refusals based on nationality still happen. Many local governments and industry groups discourage it, but in practice the faster solution is usually to switch to an agent and landlord that welcome foreign tenants rather than to argue with one that does not.

Do I pay extra to use an English-speaking agent?

Not necessarily. Agent commission is commonly up to around one month's rent plus tax regardless of language, and some agencies charge less. Some premium relocation services charge their own fees on top, so ask what is included before you commit.

Can I sign a lease in Tokyo before I arrive in Japan?

It is possible with some agencies and furnished operators, but many standard landlords want you to have a residence card first. If you are still abroad, a furnished apartment or monthly mansion for the first one or two months is often the practical route.

What does 外国人相談 mean on a listing?

It roughly means "foreigners: please consult." The landlord has not ruled out foreign tenants but wants to review each case, often looking at visa type, employer, income and Japanese ability. It is worth applying to these if your documents are strong.

My agent keeps getting rejections from landlords. Should I switch?

If several rejections come back for the same reason, such as income level or guarantor screening, the issue may be your application rather than the agent; fix that first. If the agent cannot tell you why, or is calling landlords without checking whether they accept foreigners, switching is reasonable. See our rejection recovery guide for the next steps.
