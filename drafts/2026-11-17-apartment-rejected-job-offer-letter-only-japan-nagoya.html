Apartment Rejected in Nagoya Because You Only Have a Job Offer Letter? What to Do Next

You signed a contract with a company in Nagoya, you have a shiny offer letter, maybe even your new visa, and you found an apartment you like near your future office. Then the agent calls back: the guarantor company did not approve you. No reason given, or a vague one like "income cannot be confirmed."

This is one of the most common walls newcomers hit in Japan, and it is especially frustrating because, on paper, you are doing everything right. You have a job. You have a salary figure. You simply have not been paid yet. The good news is that this is a solvable problem. It is usually not about you as a person — it is about how Japanese screening systems are built to read documents. This article explains what is going on and gives you a concrete plan to get approved, or to get a roof over your head while you wait.

What actually happens

When you apply for a typical apartment in Nagoya (or anywhere in Japan), the real estate agent passes your application to two parties: the landlord or the management company (管理会社, kanri gaisha), and a rent guarantee company (保証会社, hoshō gaisha). In most mainstream rentals today, the guarantee company makes the decision that matters.

The guarantee company wants to see proof that you can pay rent month after month. The standard proof is one of the following:

  • Recent payslips (給与明細, kyūyo meisai) from a Japanese employer
  • A tax withholding slip (源泉徴収票, gensen chōshū-hyō) from the previous year
  • A resident tax certificate (住民税課税証明書, jūminzei kazei shōmeisho)

If you just arrived, you have none of these. What you have is an offer letter, often in English, sometimes from an overseas HR department, sometimes without a company seal (社印, shain). To a screener who processes hundreds of applications in Japanese, that document may look incomplete or unverifiable. So the application gets marked as "income unconfirmed," and the easiest decision for them is "no."

In many cases, you will be told the rejection came from the guarantor and that the agent "cannot explain the details." That is normal. Guarantee companies rarely disclose their reasons.

Why it happens

Understanding the structure behind the rejection helps you fix the right thing instead of guessing.

The guarantee company is pricing risk, not judging you

A guarantee company pays the landlord if you stop paying rent, then tries to collect from you. Its business depends on filtering out applicants who might default. A person with no payment history in Japan, no Japanese credit record, and no salary deposits yet is "unknown risk." Unknown risk is often treated the same as high risk.

Landlords avoid anything that looks complicated

Many landlords in Nagoya are individuals who own one building. They want a tenant who will stay for years and never cause trouble. A foreign applicant who has not started work yet raises questions in their mind: Will this person pass probation? Will they leave Japan in six months? Can we communicate if something goes wrong? None of this is fair, but it is the reality you are working against.

Probation periods make it worse

Many Japanese employers have a trial period (試用期間, shiyō kikan), commonly a few months. Screeners know that people sometimes leave or are let go during this time. An offer letter that mentions a probation period can quietly lower your score.

Residence card dates versus lease length

Standard leases in Japan are two years. If your period of stay on your residence card (在留カード, zairyū kādo) is one year, the screener sees a mismatch. This is not a dealbreaker by itself — renewals are routine — but combined with "no payslips," it adds up.

Language and emergency contact gaps

Guarantee companies usually require an emergency contact (緊急連絡先, kinkyū renrakusaki) who lives in Japan and, ideally, speaks Japanese. Newcomers often have nobody to list. That missing field alone can sink an otherwise reasonable application. Common mistakes

  1. Submitting the English offer letter as-is. A long English PDF with no Japanese summary, no company seal, and no clear monthly salary figure is hard for a screener to accept. They are not going to translate it for you.
  2. Applying to the "perfect" apartment first. Newer buildings near Nagoya Station, Sakae, or Fushimi often use stricter guarantee companies. Applying to the hardest target first, getting rejected, and then repeating the same documents elsewhere wastes weeks.
  3. Leaving the employer out of the process. Many people try to handle housing completely alone. Your HR department can often issue a Japanese employment certificate (在職証明書, zaishoku shōmeisho) or a certificate of employment expectation (内定証明書 / 雇用予定証明書) that carries far more weight than an offer email.
  4. Not mentioning savings. If you have savings, a screener may accept a bank balance as backup proof. Many applicants never offer it because nobody asked.
  5. Booking only a short hotel stay. Applicants who give themselves one week to find a place end up panicking and accepting a bad deal, or they run out of time completely.

Step-by-step fix

  1. Ask your employer for a Japanese employment document. Email HR and request a 在職証明書 (if your start date has passed) or a 内定証明書 / 雇用予定証明書 (if you have not started yet). Ask that it include your full name as it appears on your residence card, start date, job title, employment type (正社員 for full-time permanent, 契約社員 for contract), and annual or monthly salary. A company seal makes it much stronger. You can say: "The guarantee company for my apartment needs a Japanese-language certificate showing my employment and salary. Could you issue one with the company seal?"

  2. Gather backup proof of funds. Print or download a recent bank statement from your home country showing a balance that covers several months of rent plus move-in costs. A screener may accept this alongside the employment certificate. If you already opened a Japanese bank account, even a small balance with your name on it helps show you are settling in.

  3. Ask the agent which guarantee company the property uses. Say: "どちらの保証会社を使っていますか？外国籍で入社前でも審査に通りやすい会社はありますか？" (Which guarantee company do you use? Is there one that is easier to pass for a foreign national who has not started work yet?) Some agents can switch you to a guarantor that is known to accept newcomers, or to a property that allows a different guarantor.

  4. Offer a stronger application package. Things that can tip a borderline decision:

    • Paying several months of rent in advance (前家賃, mae-yachin), if the landlord accepts it
    • Listing your employer, or a Japanese-speaking coworker or manager, as an emergency contact (ask them first)
    • Asking HR whether the company can act as a corporate tenant (法人契約, hōjin keiyaku) — the company signs the lease and you live there. Some employers in the Nagoya region do this for transferees and new hires.

  5. Reapply to a more flexible property. Instead of re-submitting the same weak documents to another strict building, target places where the screening is designed for people in your situation. Look for "外国人可" (foreigners welcome), properties that work with foreigner-focused guarantee companies, or providers that bundle furniture and simplified contracts. Our page on no-guarantor options covers the main routes.

  6. Use a furnished, simplified-contract apartment as a bridge. If you need somewhere now, consider a furnished apartment with a short or flexible contract. You live there for a few months, collect two or three Japanese payslips, and then apply for a regular apartment with documents that pass easily.   7. Reapply after your first payslip. Once you have one or two months of payslips, most of the "income unconfirmed" problem disappears. Keep every payslip, even digital ones, and save your residence card copy and bank statement in one folder so you can apply quickly when the right place appears.

Do not exaggerate your salary or invent an employment date to pass screening. Guarantee companies sometimes call the employer to confirm. A mismatch can get you blacklisted with that company, which makes every future application harder.

Required documents

Bring these to your next application. Having everything ready the first time makes you look organized and reliable — which is exactly what screeners want to see.

  • Residence card (在留カード, zairyū kādo), front and back copy
  • Passport, photo page and visa/landing stamp page
  • Employment certificate in Japanese (在職証明書) or certificate of expected employment (内定証明書 / 雇用予定証明書), with company seal if possible
  • Your original offer letter or employment contract (雇用契約書, koyō keiyakusho)
  • Bank statement showing savings (home country or Japan)
  • Japanese phone number (a working mobile number is almost always required)
  • Emergency contact name, address, and phone number in Japan
  • Resident registration certificate (住民票, jūminhyō), if you already registered at a ward office
  • Personal seal (印鑑, inkan) if requested — many agents now accept a signature, but ask

If you are still arranging your phone, sort it out before you apply. Applications with only an overseas number or a temporary SIM often stall at the first screening call.

City / region differences

Nagoya

Nagoya is a manufacturing and corporate city, and a large share of foreign residents come for jobs with major employers and their suppliers. That has an upside: many agents near Nagoya Station and in Sakae are used to new hires who arrive with an offer letter. The downside is that newer buildings in central wards like Naka-ku and Nakamura-ku can be strict. Areas a little farther out, such as Chikusa-ku, Showa-ku, or along the Higashiyama and Meijo subway lines, often have more flexible landlords and lower rent. A one-room or 1K apartment in central Nagoya generally runs somewhere around ¥45,000–¥75,000 per month, while outer wards can be cheaper. Move-in costs, including deposit, key money, guarantee fee, and agency fee, can easily reach four to six months of rent. See our breakdown of hidden move-in costs before you budget.

Tokyo and Osaka

In Tokyo, competition is fiercer, so landlords can afford to reject borderline applicants more often. However, Tokyo also has many more foreigner-focused agencies and furnished providers, so the bridge-apartment strategy is easier. Osaka sits in between: generally a bit more flexible than central Tokyo, with plenty of agencies used to international residents.

Smaller cities around Aichi

If your job is in Toyota, Kariya, Anjo, or another industrial city near Nagoya, your employer may already have company housing (社宅, shataku) or a partner real estate agent. Always ask HR before searching on your own — in these areas, a company introduction can open doors that a solo application cannot.

Frequently Asked Questions

Can I rent an apartment in Japan before my job starts?

Yes, it is possible, but it is harder. You need documents that convince the guarantee company your income is real. A Japanese-language certificate of expected employment from your employer, plus proof of savings, gives you the best chance. If that still fails, a furnished apartment or share house is a practical bridge until your first payslips arrive.

Will the guarantee company tell me why I was rejected?

Usually not. Guarantee companies rarely share reasons, and agents often do not know the details either. Instead of trying to get an explanation, focus on strengthening the weak points: income proof, emergency contact, and phone number. See our page on what to do after a rejection for more.

Does paying rent in advance help?

Sometimes. Some landlords accept several months of prepaid rent as extra security, and it can push a borderline case to approval. It does not work everywhere, because the guarantee company still runs its own check. Ask the agent before you offer, and never hand over cash without a written receipt.

How many payslips do I need before I can apply normally?

There is no single rule, but many applicants find that one to three months of Japanese payslips is enough to pass a standard screening. The longer your work history in Japan, the easier it gets.

Is a corporate lease (法人契約) a good idea?

If your employer offers it, yes. The company becomes the contract holder, so screening focuses on the company rather than you. The trade-offs are that you may have less choice of property and you may need to move out if you leave the job. Ask HR about the rules before you sign.
