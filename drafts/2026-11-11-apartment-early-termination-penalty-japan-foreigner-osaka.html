Charged an Early Termination Penalty on Your Osaka Apartment? What to Do Before You Pay

You found a new job in Tokyo, your visa situation changed, or the apartment simply is not working out. You tell the management company you want to leave, and the reply comes back: you owe one or two months' rent as a "penalty" for breaking the lease early. On top of that, you may be told you still owe rent for the notice period, a cleaning fee, and possibly part of the deposit is gone.

If you are a foreigner renting in Osaka (or anywhere in Kansai), this situation is very common, and it is confusing because the rules sit in a Japanese-language contract you probably signed quickly. The good news: in most cases the amount is fixed by your contract, it is predictable, and you can often reduce what you actually pay by timing your notice correctly and asking the right questions. This article walks you through exactly that.

What actually happens

Most Japanese residential leases are two-year contracts (普通借家契約, futsu shakuya keiyaku). Despite the two-year term, tenants can usually leave before the end of the term by giving notice. The penalty question depends on two separate clauses that are often mixed up:

  • The notice period (解約予告期間, kaiyaku yokoku kikan). Typically one month, sometimes two. You pay rent until the end of this period whether you live there or not. This is not a penalty; it is simply how leaving works in Japan.
  • The short-term cancellation penalty (短期解約違約金, tanki kaiyaku iyakukin). An extra charge if you leave within a set period, often within the first 6 months or the first 12 months. A common pattern is something like one month's rent if you leave within one year, and sometimes two months' rent if you leave within six months. The exact terms vary a lot by contract.

So when you hear "you have to pay two months," it may actually be one month of notice-period rent plus one month of penalty. Or it may be entirely notice-period rent, with no penalty at all. Separating the two is the first step to understanding what you really owe.

In Osaka there is a third element that surprises many people: shikibiki (敷引き), a Kansai custom where a fixed portion of your deposit (sometimes called 保証金, hoshokin) is kept by the landlord when you move out, regardless of how clean the room is. If you leave early, people often assume the shikibiki deduction is part of the penalty. It is usually a separate clause that applies whenever you move out.

Why it happens

Early termination penalties are not aimed at foreigners specifically, but several structural reasons make foreign tenants hit them more often.

Zero-deposit and free-rent campaigns

Many Osaka listings advertise "no deposit, no key money" (敷金礼金ゼロ) or one or two months of free rent (フリーレント, free rent). These deals are popular with foreigners because the move-in cost is low. The catch is that the landlord recovers the discount through a short-term penalty. If you leave within the first year, you may be asked to repay the free rent or pay a fixed penalty. This is the single most common reason for a surprise charge.

Landlord risk management

Every time a tenant leaves, the landlord pays for cleaning, advertising, and the agent's fee to find someone new. A short stay means those costs are spread over very few months of rent. Landlords who worry that foreign tenants are more likely to leave suddenly, for example when a contract job ends or someone returns home, sometimes add a stricter penalty clause as protection.

Visa length versus lease length

Your residence status may be valid for one year while your lease runs for two. If your visa is not renewed, or you change jobs and move cities, you end up breaking the lease through no real choice of your own. The contract does not care why you are leaving, so the penalty still applies unless you negotiate.

Language and the guarantor company

Many foreign tenants sign through a guarantor company (保証会社, hosho gaisha), and the lease plus the guarantee contract are written in Japanese. Important clauses about notice and penalties are often explained quickly, or not at all, at signing. Later, the management company may only communicate in Japanese, which makes it hard to question the amount.

Important: Before you send any notice or agree to pay anything, find your lease (賃貸借契約書) and the important matters explanation (重要事項説明書). The penalty, the notice period, and the shikibiki amount should all be written there. If a charge is not in your contract, ask the management company in writing which clause it is based on.

Common mistakes

  1. Telling the agent verbally and assuming notice is done. Many management companies only count notice from the day they receive the official cancellation form (解約通知書 or 解約届). A phone call or a chat message to the agent who found you the apartment may not count. That can add an extra month of rent.
  2. Not checking whether rent is prorated. Some contracts calculate the final month by day (日割り, hiwari), others by half-month or full month (月割り). If yours is full-month, moving out on the 2nd of the month costs the same as moving out on the 30th.
  3. Confusing shikibiki with a penalty. In Osaka, the deposit deduction is often fixed. Arguing that it is an "unfair penalty" usually goes nowhere if it was clearly in the contract. Focus your energy on charges that are not in the contract.
  4. Leaving the country before settlement. If you move out and fly home without settling, the guarantor company may pay the landlord and then chase you. This can affect future rentals in Japan and makes resolving disputes much harder from abroad.
  5. Paying a lump sum without an itemized statement. Always ask for the final settlement (精算書, seisansho) in writing, broken down into notice-period rent, penalty, cleaning, repairs, and deposit return.

Step-by-step fix

  1. Read the two key clauses. In your lease, look for 解約 (cancellation), 解約予告 (notice), and 違約金 (penalty). Note: how many months of notice, whether the penalty applies within 6 or 12 months, and how much it is. If you cannot read it, use a phone translation app on a photo of the page, or ask a Japanese-speaking friend to check just those lines.
  2. Do the date math before giving notice. If your penalty disappears after month 12 and you are currently in month 11, giving notice now with a one-month notice period might push your move-out date past the deadline. Ask the management company directly: "If my notice reaches you today, is my contract end date before or after the penalty period ends?" A few weeks of timing can save you a full month's rent.
  3. Submit notice the official way. Ask: "解約通知書の書式をください" (Please send me the cancellation notice form). Fill it in, send it by the method they specify (online form, fax, mail), and keep a copy or a screenshot with the date. If they accept email, send one anyway as a record.
  4. Ask for the penalty in writing, with the clause number. Say: "違約金の根拠となる契約書の条項を教えてください" (Please tell me which clause of the contract the penalty is based on). Management companies in Japan generally respond better to a calm written request than to arguing on the phone.
  5. Negotiate if you have a reason. Penalties are set in the contract, but landlords sometimes reduce or waive them, especially if you can help them avoid vacancy. Options to offer: finding a replacement tenant, leaving the room in excellent condition, or moving out at a time of year when rooms fill quickly (spring, around February to April, is the busiest season in Japan). If your move is caused by a job transfer, mention it; some landlords are flexible.
  6. Check whether a charge looks excessive. If the penalty is far larger than what is common, such as several months' rent for leaving after a year, or it does not appear in the contract at all, you can get free advice from Japan's consumer hotline by dialing 188. They can point you to a local consumer center in Osaka. Explain the situation simply; some centers can arrange interpretation, but ask a Japanese speaker to help if you can.
  7. Attend the move-out inspection (立ち会い, tachiai). Be present when the staff checks the room. Take photos of every wall, floor, and fixture before they arrive. Ordinary wear and tear (natural aging, sunlight fading) is generally the landlord's responsibility, not yours. See our breakdown in deposit disputes in Japan.
  8. Line up your next place before the deadline. If you are moving within Osaka or to another city and want to avoid another long lease and penalty clause, consider a share house or furnished room with flexible terms. [PR] Oakhouse share houses and apartments offer short contracts designed for foreign residents, which can bridge the gap while you sort out your next long-term rental.
  9. Settle before you leave Japan. Confirm the final amount, pay it or have it deducted from your deposit, and give a Japanese bank account or overseas account details for any refund. Keep the settlement statement.

Required documents

Have these ready when you contact the management company and at move-out:

  • Lease contract (賃貸借契約書, chintaishaku keiyakusho)
  • Important matters explanation (重要事項説明書, juyo jiko setsumeisho)
  • Guarantor company contract (保証委託契約書), if you used one
  • Cancellation notice form (解約通知書 or 解約届) with a copy for yourself
  • Residence card (在留カード, zairyu card) for identity confirmation
  • Bank account details for deposit refund (Japanese or overseas)
  • Your forwarding address in Japan or abroad
  • Photos of the room at move-in and move-out, if you have them
  • All keys, including any spare keys you were given (鍵)

Also remember to file a moving-out notice (転出届, tenshutsu todoke) at your ward office if you are leaving the city, and to cancel or transfer electricity, gas, water, and internet. Internet contracts often have their own cancellation fees, so check those too.

City / region differences

Osaka and Kansai (Kobe, Kyoto)

The shikibiki custom means a part of your deposit may not come back regardless of when you leave. Contracts here sometimes use the term 保証金 (hoshokin) instead of 敷金 (shikikin). Check both the shikibiki amount and the short-term penalty separately so you know your true total. Many newer Osaka listings, especially low-initial-cost ones aimed at foreigners and students, include a short-term penalty clause in exchange for zero deposit. If you are still choosing a new place in the city, see our Osaka renting overview.

Tokyo and Yokohama

Shikibiki is much less common. Instead, you usually pay a standard deposit (shikikin) and key money (reikin). Short-term penalties still exist, particularly with free-rent offers. Renewal fees (更新料) at the two-year mark are common, which sometimes makes people decide to leave early; see lease renewal problems.

Nagoya and Fukuoka

Practices vary by management company rather than strictly by region. Some older contracts in these areas also include deposit deductions similar to shikibiki. As always, the contract text is what matters.

Frequently Asked Questions

Is an early termination penalty legal in Japan?

Short-term cancellation penalties written in the contract are widely used and generally treated as valid when the amount is reasonable, such as around one or two months' rent. If a penalty looks extremely large or was never explained, Japan's consumer law may offer protection, and the consumer hotline (188) can advise you. This article is not legal advice, so for a serious dispute, consult a consumer center or legal professional.

Can I avoid the penalty if my visa was not renewed?

The contract usually does not make an exception for visa issues. However, many landlords will listen if you explain the situation politely and in writing, especially if you give as much notice as possible. Ask whether the penalty can be reduced or waived; the worst they can say is no.

Do I have to pay rent for the notice period if I already moved out?

Usually yes. Rent is owed until the contract end date set by your notice, even if the room is empty. Giving notice as early as possible is the most reliable way to reduce this cost.

What happens if I leave Japan without paying?

If you used a guarantor company, it will typically pay the landlord and then try to collect from you. An unpaid balance can create problems if you ever want to rent in Japan again, and it can also affect your relationship with the guarantor company. Settle before departure whenever possible.

Is shikibiki the same as an early termination penalty?

No. Shikibiki is a fixed deduction from your deposit that normally applies whenever you move out, regardless of timing. An early termination penalty applies only if you leave before a certain date. You may have to deal with both in Osaka, so read both clauses.
