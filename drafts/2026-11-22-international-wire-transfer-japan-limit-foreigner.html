International Wire Transfer Refused or Limited in Japan? What to Do When Your Bank Blocks Your Money

You walk into your bank branch, or log into online banking, to send money home: rent for a flat you still have overseas, a loan payment, help for family, or savings you want to move before you leave Japan. Then it stops. The app says overseas remittance is "not available for this account." The teller asks for documents you did not bring. Or the transfer goes through the form stage and then gets held for days with a phone call asking what the money is for.

This is one of the most common money frustrations for foreign residents in Japan, and it is rarely about you personally. Japanese banks run overseas transfers through a strict compliance process, and foreign customers trigger more of its checks than most Japanese customers do. The good news is that most refusals and limits can be fixed once you know which check you failed. This article walks through what is actually happening, why, and the exact steps to get your transfer approved.

What actually happens

Foreigners usually run into one of these situations when trying to send an international wire from a Japanese bank:

  • The option is simply missing. Your account works for domestic transfers, but overseas remittance does not appear in the app, or the branch says your account type does not support it. Many banks require a separate registration or application before an account can send money abroad.
  • The transfer is capped. Online banking may allow only a modest amount per day or per transaction, and anything larger must be done at a branch. Some banks set a lower ceiling for accounts that are new or for customers who have not completed extra verification.
  • You are asked for your My Number. Before the bank will process an overseas remittance, it asks you to submit your Individual Number (My Number, マイナンバー) and a declaration form. If you cannot provide it, the transfer stops.
  • The bank wants to know why. Staff ask about the purpose of the transfer, your relationship to the recipient, and where the money came from. Vague answers lead to a hold or a refusal.
  • The transfer is refused because of your status. If you arrived recently, your residence card is close to expiry, or your registered address or name does not match exactly, the bank may decline until the details are sorted out.
  • The money leaves but arrives short or late. The bank sends it, but intermediary banks deduct fees, or the receiving bank returns it because of a wrong SWIFT code or name mismatch.

The frustrating part is that staff often explain this only in Japanese, or give a short "we cannot do this" without telling you which requirement you missed. Knowing the structure behind it lets you ask the right question.

Why it happens

Japanese banks are not trying to trap your money. They operate under foreign exchange and anti-money-laundering rules, and they have become much more conservative over the past decade. Several structural reasons stack up for foreign residents:

Anti-money-laundering checks are strict

Japanese banks are required to confirm the identity of the sender, the purpose of the transfer, and in many cases the source of funds. Overseas remittances get the closest scrutiny. Customers who send money abroad frequently, send round numbers, send to countries the bank treats as higher risk, or send amounts that do not match their declared income or occupation are more likely to be stopped for questions.

My Number and the declaration form

For overseas remittances, banks generally require a declaration form (告知書, kokuchisho) along with your My Number. Larger transfers are reported to the tax office by the bank, which is why they need these details on file. If you never received your My Number notification card, lost it, or registered it at a different address, this becomes a blocker.

Residency status and the six-month line

Under Japan's foreign exchange framework, a foreign national who has been in Japan for a short time may still be treated as a non-resident rather than a resident for some banking purposes. Many banks restrict services, including overseas transfers, for customers who have been in Japan for less than six months. This is a common reason newly arrived workers and students find the option unavailable.

Residence card expiry and contract mismatch

Banks track the period of stay on your residence card. If it is close to expiring and you have not updated the bank with your renewed card, the bank may freeze international services or the whole account. For the same reason, people who announce they are leaving Japan often find the bank becomes cautious about large outbound transfers.

Language and documentation gaps

The remittance form asks for the recipient's full name, address, bank name, SWIFT/BIC code, account number or IBAN, and the purpose of the transfer. Small mismatches, such as a middle name missing or a different romanization of your name compared with the account, cause the bank to reject the form. If your account name is registered in katakana and your passport is in Latin letters, staff may need to confirm both match.

Important: If a bank asks you to explain a transfer, answer honestly and specifically. Inconsistent or evasive answers are what turn a routine check into an account review. In the worst case, unusual activity can lead to an account freeze, which is far harder to undo than a single refused transfer.

Common mistakes

  1. Trying to send a large sum online on the first attempt. Online limits are often lower than branch limits, and the first overseas transfer from an account almost always needs extra checks. Splitting it into many small transfers to stay under the limit can itself look suspicious.
  2. Not bringing My Number. Going to the branch without your My Number card or notification document means the form cannot be completed. You will have to come back.
  3. Writing "personal" or "other" as the purpose. Banks want a concrete reason: "living expenses for my parents," "repayment of my student loan in the UK," "transfer of my own savings to my account in Canada." A one-word answer invites a hold.
  4. Not updating the bank after renewing your residence card. Your bank still has your old expiry date on file. When it passes, services stop, even though your status is valid.
  5. Using an account meant for domestic use only. Some online-only or simplified accounts cannot send money abroad at all. Customers keep trying instead of asking whether the account type supports it.

Step-by-step fix

  1. Find out exactly which requirement you failed. Call the bank or visit the branch and ask directly. A useful phrase is: "Kaigai sōkin ga dekinai riyū o oshiete kudasai" (Please tell me why I cannot make an overseas remittance). Ask whether the issue is the account type, missing registration, My Number, your period of stay, or the amount.
  2. Register your account for overseas remittance. If the option is missing, ask whether you need to apply for overseas remittance service. This usually requires a branch visit with your residence card and possibly your seal (inkan) if the account was opened with one.
  3. Submit your My Number and declaration form. Bring your My Number card or notification document. If you never received it, your city or ward office can tell you how to get a certificate of residence (住民票, jūminhyō) that includes your My Number, which banks usually accept.
  4. Update your residence card details. If you renewed your status, bring the new card to the branch or upload it through the app if your bank allows. Ask staff to confirm the new expiry date is registered.
  5. Prepare a clear purpose and proof. For larger amounts, bring something that explains where the money came from and where it is going: recent payslips, a tax certificate, a loan statement, a tuition invoice, or a letter from a family member. You do not always need these, but having them ready shortens the process.
  6. Double-check the recipient details. Copy the recipient name exactly as it appears on the overseas account, along with the SWIFT/BIC code and IBAN or account number. Ask the receiving bank whether an intermediary bank is required, and whether they charge an incoming fee.
  7. Do the first large transfer at a branch. Even if online banking works later, the first sizable transfer is easier in person, where staff can complete the checks on the spot. After that, ask whether your online limit can be raised.
  8. If the bank still refuses, use a licensed money transfer service. Registered remittance companies operate in Japan and can be cheaper and faster than bank wires for everyday amounts. They also verify identity and My Number, so the same documents apply. If you run into problems there too, see our article on Wise transfers stuck or refused in Japan.
  9. If you are leaving Japan, plan ahead. Move larger amounts in the weeks before departure, not on your last day, and keep your account open until you confirm the money has arrived. Tell the bank your plans honestly so they do not treat the activity as unusual.

If your account has already been frozen after a transfer, the process is different. Read what to do when your bank account in Japan is frozen before you try again.

Required documents

  • Residence card (在留カード, zairyū kādo) with a valid period of stay
  • My Number card (マイナンバーカード) or notification document, or a certificate of residence (住民票, jūminhyō) showing your My Number
  • Passport, especially if your name on the account differs in spelling or order
  • Bank passbook (通帳, tsūchō) and cash card for the sending account
  • Seal (印鑑, inkan) if your account was opened with one
  • Recipient's full name, address, bank name, branch, SWIFT/BIC code and IBAN or account number
  • Proof of purpose or source of funds for larger amounts (payslips, tax certificate, loan or tuition statement)

City / region differences

The rules come from national law and each bank's own policy, so they are mostly the same anywhere in Japan. What changes from place to place is how easy it is to get help.

  • Tokyo and Yokohama: Major branches near business districts and international areas are more likely to have staff experienced with overseas remittances, and some banks run English-language support lines. Expect waiting times at popular branches, especially at the end of the month.
  • Osaka, Kobe and Kyoto: Large branches in central areas handle overseas transfers regularly. Smaller neighborhood branches may send you to a main branch for international services.
  • Nagoya and Fukuoka: Main branches handle international transfers well, but English support is thinner. Bringing a written note in Japanese with the purpose and recipient details helps.
  • Rural areas and regional banks: Some regional banks offer limited overseas remittance services or route them through partner banks, which can mean higher fees and longer processing times. Japan Post Bank or a licensed transfer service may be more practical.

Fees vary widely. A bank wire typically costs a few thousand yen from the sending bank, plus possible intermediary and receiving fees and the exchange rate margin. Frequently Asked Questions

Why does my bank say I cannot send money abroad when I just opened my account?

Many Japanese banks restrict overseas remittances for new accounts and for customers who have been in Japan for less than about six months. Ask the bank when the restriction ends and what you need to register. A licensed money transfer service may be an option in the meantime, though it will also check your identity and status.

Do I really need My Number to send money overseas?

In most cases, yes. Banks and remittance services generally require My Number and a declaration form for overseas transfers. If you do not have your card, a certificate of residence that includes your My Number from your city or ward office is usually accepted.

Is there a maximum amount I can send?

There is no single national cap for an ordinary personal transfer, but each bank sets its own limits, especially for online banking. Larger amounts often need a branch visit and proof of the source of funds, and very large transfers trigger additional reporting by the bank. Ask your bank for its specific per-transaction and daily limits.

My residence card is about to expire. Can I still send money?

Possibly not. Banks often pause international services when the period of stay on file is close to ending. Apply for renewal early, and once you receive your new card, show it to the bank so they update your record. Our visa guide explains the renewal process.

The money left my account but never arrived. What should I do?

Contact your bank and ask for a trace (sometimes called an investigation request) using the transfer reference number. Common causes are a wrong SWIFT code, a name mismatch, or an intermediary bank holding the payment. The bank may charge a fee for the investigation, and returned funds usually come back minus fees and at a new exchange rate.
