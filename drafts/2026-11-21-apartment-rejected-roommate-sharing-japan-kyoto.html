Apartment Rejected Because You Want to Share With a Roommate in Kyoto? What to Do Next

You found a two-bedroom place in Kyoto that would be affordable if you split the rent with a friend. You filled out the application, listed both names, and a few days later the agent called back with a short answer: the owner does not accept room sharing. Or worse, the agent told you before you even applied that "two unrelated people" is not possible for this building.

This is one of the most common and most confusing rejections foreigners face in Japan, because the apartment clearly has space for two people. The good news is that this rejection is usually about the type of occupancy the owner allows, not about you personally. Once you understand how Japanese listings categorize occupancy, you can search in a way that avoids this rejection almost entirely.

What actually happens

In Japan, every rental listing carries occupancy conditions set by the owner or management company. Many listings are marked 単身者限定 (tanshinsha gentei, single occupant only). Others say 二人入居可 (futari nyūkyo ka, two occupants allowed). A smaller group says ルームシェア可 (rūmu shea ka, room share allowed).

The key detail most foreigners miss is that "two occupants allowed" and "room share allowed" are not the same thing. "Two occupants allowed" is often written with couples or family members in mind, such as a married couple or a parent and child. "Room share" means two or more unrelated adults splitting a unit, usually friends, coworkers, or classmates. Many owners who happily accept a married couple will still refuse two friends.

When you apply as roommates for a listing that does not explicitly allow room sharing, a few things can happen:

  • The agent refuses to submit the application at all.
  • The application is submitted, but the owner declines without detailed reasons.
  • The guarantor company approves one person but the owner still rejects the arrangement.
  • You are told one person can sign, but the second person cannot be listed as living there.

That last option is a trap. Moving someone in who is not registered on the contract can be treated as a breach of the lease. More on that below.

Why it happens

Owners in Japan are generally cautious, and room sharing touches several of their biggest worries at once. Knowing these reasons helps you answer them directly.

Who pays if one person leaves

The biggest concern is what happens when one roommate moves out. Couples tend to stay or leave together. Friends often split up when one person changes jobs, graduates, or returns home. The remaining tenant may not be able to cover the full rent alone, and the owner then faces late payments or an early termination. In Kyoto, where a large share of renters are students and short-term residents, owners have seen this pattern many times.

Guarantor company screening becomes more complicated

Most rentals now require a guarantor company (保証会社, hoshō gaisha). With a couple, the company screens the main contract holder and treats the partner as a household member. With roommates, the company may want to screen both people's income, visa status, and contact details, and some companies simply do not offer a product for unrelated co-tenants. If the guarantor company will not cover the arrangement, the owner will not either.

Residence period and contract length do not line up

A standard Japanese lease is usually two years. If one roommate has a visa that expires in eight months and the other has three years left, the owner sees a built-in risk that the household will break up mid-contract. This is the same issue covered in our article on apartment rejections when your visa is expiring soon, but doubled.

Noise, guests, and "turnover" of residents

Owners worry that shared units turn into informal share houses, with friends of friends staying over, people swapping in and out, and more noise for neighbors. Traditional wooden buildings and older apartments in Kyoto have thin walls, so owners are especially sensitive to this.

Language and emergency contact concerns

If neither roommate speaks Japanese and neither has a contact in Japan, the owner may worry about communication when something goes wrong. Two foreign tenants without a Japanese-speaking emergency contact is a harder sell than one.

Common mistakes

  1. Applying to "two occupants allowed" listings as friends without asking first. Many of these listings mean couples or family. Always confirm that room sharing between unrelated adults is acceptable before you spend time on viewing and paperwork.
  2. Agreeing to an unregistered second occupant. If an agent hints that one of you can sign and the other can "just live there," do not do it. An unregistered resident can be grounds for the owner to demand they leave or to terminate the lease, and you lose your deposit protections in a dispute. See our article on subletting and occupancy violations for how seriously this is treated.
  3. Not deciding who the main contract holder is. Owners want one clear person responsible for rent. If you show up undecided, it signals that the arrangement is not well planned.
  4. Submitting weak documents for the second person. Even if only one person signs, the owner may review the other person's residence card and income. A roommate with no proof of income can sink the whole application.
  5. Searching only on Japanese portals with default filters. Default searches rarely filter for room sharing, so you end up applying to many places that were never going to accept you.

Step-by-step fix

  1. Search specifically for ルームシェア可. When you browse listings or talk to an agent, use the exact phrase "ルームシェア可" (room share allowed). If your Japanese is limited, write it down and show it. Ask the agent: "ルームシェア可の物件だけ探してください" (Please only look for room-share-allowed properties). This alone removes most rejections.
  2. Confirm the occupancy type before viewing. For any listing that only says "二人入居可," ask: "友人同士のルームシェアでも大丈夫ですか?" (Is it OK for two friends sharing?). Get the answer before you pay any application fees or visit.
  3. Choose one main contract holder. Pick the person with the most stable job, the longest remaining residence period, and the highest income. That person becomes the 契約者 (keiyakusha, contract holder). The other is listed as a 同居人 (dōkyonin, co-resident).
  4. Prepare a full set of documents for both people. Bring residence cards, proof of income or enrollment, and contact details for both. Showing that each person could contribute to rent reassures the owner. If one of you has only part-time income, read our article on rejections due to part-time income for how to present it.
  5. Offer a clear answer to "what if one of you leaves?" Tell the agent the main contract holder can cover the full rent alone if needed, or that you plan to give notice together. If the main holder's income is roughly three times the full monthly rent or more, mention it.
  6. Line up an emergency contact. Owners and guarantor companies often want a contact in Japan, ideally one who speaks Japanese. A coworker, school office, or friend can work. If you have no one, see our article on rejections with no emergency contact.
  7. Ask about a guarantor company that handles room sharing. Ask the agent: "ルームシェアに対応している保証会社はありますか?" (Is there a guarantor company that supports room sharing?). Some agencies have partners that accept it; others do not.
  8. Consider a share house instead of a standard lease. If you keep getting refused, a share house operator is built for exactly this situation: each person signs their own contract, there is usually no Japanese guarantor needed, and one person leaving does not affect the other's lease. You and your friend can apply for rooms in the same house. [PR] Oakhouse runs share houses and apartments for foreign residents and lists properties in the Kansai area as well as Tokyo.
  9. If you are a couple, say so clearly. Unmarried couples are sometimes accepted under "二人入居可" even when friends are not. Be honest about your relationship; do not claim to be married if you are not, because a later discovery can create trust problems with the owner.
Important: Never move a second person into an apartment that was rented for one occupant without the owner's written approval. If the owner finds out, you can be asked to remove the person or face termination of the lease, and both of you could lose your home at short notice.

Required documents

Exact requirements vary by agency and guarantor company, but for a room-share application in Kyoto you should be ready with the following for both people:

  • Residence card (在留カード, zairyū kādo), front and back copy
  • Passport copy
  • Certificate of residence (住民票, jūminhyō), if you already have an address in Japan
  • Proof of income: recent pay slips (給与明細, kyūyo meisai), employment certificate (在職証明書, zaishoku shōmeisho), or tax certificate (課税証明書, kazei shōmeisho)
  • For students: certificate of enrollment (在学証明書, zaigaku shōmeisho) and proof of scholarship or sponsor support
  • Japanese phone number for each person
  • Emergency contact name, address, and phone number in Japan
  • Bank account details for the main contract holder (for rent withdrawal)
  • Personal seal (印鑑, inkan) if requested, or confirmation that a signature is acceptable

City / region differences

Kyoto

Kyoto's rental market leans heavily toward small single-person units (1K and 1R), especially around universities in areas such as Sakyo-ku and Kita-ku. Larger 2DK or 2LDK units exist, but many are aimed at families, and owners of those units often prefer couples or families over roommates. Older buildings and wooden houses are common, and noise sensitivity is high. As a rough guide, a 2DK or 2LDK in central Kyoto often ranges from about ¥70,000 to ¥130,000 per month depending on location, age, and size, so splitting rent can make a big difference. Local agencies near universities are more used to student and foreign tenants, and some will know which owners accept room sharing. Our Kyoto English-speaking landlord article has more on finding these agencies.

Osaka and Kobe

Osaka generally has more supply of larger units at lower rents than Kyoto, and some agencies there are more flexible with foreign tenants. If your work or school allows, searching in Osaka can widen your options. See our Osaka page for more.

Tokyo

Tokyo has the largest number of room-share-allowed listings in absolute terms, but competition is intense and screening can be strict. Share houses are widely available, which is why many roommates in Tokyo end up choosing them instead of a joint lease.

Frequently Asked Questions

Can we both sign the lease as joint tenants?

Sometimes, but it is not the usual setup. Most owners prefer one contract holder with the other listed as a co-resident. Some agencies can arrange joint contracts for room-share-allowed properties, so ask directly, but be prepared for one person to take legal responsibility for rent.

Is "二人入居可" enough for two friends?

Not always. That label often means the owner accepts two people, but they may only be thinking of couples or relatives. Always ask whether friends sharing is acceptable before applying.

What if my roommate leaves before the contract ends?

If you are the contract holder, you remain responsible for the full rent. You can look for a replacement roommate, but you usually need the owner's approval to register a new co-resident. If you cannot afford the rent alone, you may need to give notice and pay any early termination fee in your contract.

Will being foreigners make a room-share rejection more likely?

It can add to the owner's concerns, mainly around visa length, language, and emergency contacts. Strong documents, a Japanese-speaking emergency contact, and a clear main contract holder reduce those concerns significantly.

Is a share house a better option for two friends?

Often, yes. In a share house each person has their own contract, so one person leaving does not affect the other. Approval is usually easier for foreigners, and move-in costs are typically lower than a standard lease. The trade-off is less privacy and shared common areas.
