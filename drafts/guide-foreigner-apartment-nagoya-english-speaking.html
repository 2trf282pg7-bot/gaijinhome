Apartment Rejection Problems in Nagoya for Foreigners: What Actually Works

Nagoya should be easier than Tokyo. Rents are lower, there is less competition, and the city has a large working foreign population tied to manufacturing, universities and schools. Yet many foreigners hit the same wall here: agents who say "this owner doesn't accept foreigners," applications that disappear for a week and come back declined, and very few English-speaking agencies to turn to.

If that is where you are right now, this guide is for you. It focuses on how renting in Nagoya and the wider Aichi area actually works, why rejections happen here specifically, and the steps that get people approved.

What Actually Happens in Nagoya

The typical pattern for foreign applicants in Nagoya looks like this:

  • You find listings on a big portal, contact the agent, and are told the unit is "no longer available" or "the owner prefers Japanese tenants."
  • You apply for a unit, the agent sends your details to the owner and a guarantor company, and after several days you hear "審査に通りませんでした" (you did not pass screening).
  • You are asked for a Japanese-speaking emergency contact or a guarantor and you have neither.
  • Your employer is in Toyota City, Kariya or another industrial town, and agents there have little experience with foreign applicants who are not on a company-arranged lease.

The overall rental market in Nagoya is less tight than Tokyo, which works in your favor. The problem is usually not a lack of units but a lack of agents and owners who are set up for foreign tenants.

Why It Happens

A higher share of individual, older landlords

Large parts of Nagoya's rental stock are owned by individuals rather than large real estate companies. Individual owners tend to be more cautious and more likely to reject any applicant they see as "different" — not necessarily out of hostility, but because they worry about communication, noise and garbage rules, and what happens if a tenant leaves Japan suddenly.

Fewer English-speaking agencies

Tokyo has many agencies that specialize in foreign clients. Nagoya has some, but far fewer. Most local agents will try to help, but if they cannot explain the contract to you in English, they may quietly steer you away from units to avoid trouble.

Company leases set expectations

Many foreign residents in Aichi arrive through companies that arrange housing for them via a corporate lease (法人契約) or dormitory. Local agents are therefore used to foreigners coming through employers. A foreigner applying individually, especially on a short contract, can look unusual to them.

Standard screening factors still apply

  • Remaining visa period shorter than the standard two-year lease.
  • Short employment history in Japan, or a job still in probation.
  • No Japanese phone number in your own name.
  • No Japanese-speaking emergency contact.

Common Mistakes in Nagoya

  1. Only searching near Nagoya Station or Sakae. These areas get the most competition and the most selective owners. Equally convenient areas one or two subway stops away are often easier.
  2. Using a small local agent without asking if they handle foreign tenants. Ask directly at the start. It saves weeks.
  3. Forgetting the car question. Outside central Nagoya, many listings assume you have a car. If parking is mandatory or bundled in, your total cost rises and the agent may assume you cannot afford it.
  4. Not asking your employer for help first. In Aichi, employers commonly have a relationship with a local agent or a corporate lease option. Many foreign employees don't ask.
  5. Sending a bare application. Without an employment certificate and visa explanation, a cautious owner just says no.

Step-by-Step Fix

  1. Start with your employer or school. Ask HR or the international office: "Do you have a partner real estate agent, or can the company sign a corporate lease?" Universities in Nagoya often have housing support for international students and staff. This single question resolves many cases.
  2. Filter for foreigner-friendly listings. On Japanese portals, search with conditions like 外国人可 (foreigners OK) or 外国人相談 (foreigners negotiable), and 保証会社利用可 (guarantor company accepted). This removes most owners who would reject you anyway.
  3. Bring a Japanese-speaking helper to the first agent visit. A coworker, classmate or volunteer interpreter can make the agent much more comfortable. The Nagoya International Center (名古屋国際センター) offers multilingual consultation and can point you to support services.
  4. Prepare a short self-introduction for the owner. Write three or four lines in Japanese (a translation app is fine): your job, how long you plan to stay, that you understand garbage sorting rules, and that you are a non-smoker if true. Ask the agent to attach it to the application. Cautious individual owners respond well to this.
  5. Address the visa period upfront. If your visa expires before the lease, include an employer letter stating your contract continues and that the company will support your renewal.
  6. Ask about a different guarantor company if rejected. Say: "他の保証会社で審査をお願いできますか？" (Could you try screening with another guarantor company?) Many agents can submit to an alternative company that is more open to foreign residents.
  7. Use an alternative while you keep searching. Monthly apartments (see monthly mansions for foreigners) and UR public housing (see how to apply for UR) both exist in and around Nagoya and generally do not need a guarantor. UR has a sizable presence in the Aichi area, including large estates in the eastern suburbs.
Key tip: In Nagoya, the agent matters more than the listing. Once you find one agent who is willing to work with foreign applicants, stick with them and let them search for you. They know which local owners say yes.

Required Documents

  • Residence card (在留カード), front and back
  • Passport
  • Certificate of employment (在職証明書) or school enrollment certificate (在学証明書)
  • Last 3 months' payslips (給与明細) or offer letter if newly arrived
  • Tax certificate (課税証明書) from your city office, if you were registered in Japan on January 1 of the previous year
  • Residence certificate (住民票), if requested
  • Employer letter confirming contract continuation (for visa period gaps)
  • Japanese mobile number in your name
  • Emergency contact details (Japanese-speaking if possible)
  • Short self-introduction note in Japanese (optional but helpful)
  • Driver's license and car registration details, if the unit includes a parking space

Nagoya Neighborhoods, Rents and Local Quirks

Where foreigners tend to find it easier

  • Chikusa, Imaike and Motoyama (Higashiyama Line): university area with many students and younger renters. Owners here are more used to international students and staff.
  • Yagoto and Showa-ku: residential, near several universities, good for families and long stays.
  • Kanayama and Atsuta: major transfer hub with good access to the south and to Chubu Centrair airport. Often better value than Meieki.
  • Higashi-ku (Takaoka, Shindeki): quiet and central, walking distance or a short ride to Sakae.
  • Osu and Kamimaezu: lively shopping district with a long multicultural history; mixed older and newer buildings.

Rough rent ranges

Rents vary by building age and distance from the station, but as a rough guide, a 1K or 1R studio in central Nagoya often falls somewhere around ¥45,000–75,000 per month, and a 1LDK around ¥70,000–120,000. Newer buildings near Meieki and Sakae sit at the top of these ranges or above. Suburban and industrial towns are usually cheaper but may need a car. Treat these as estimates and check current listings.

Local quirks to know

  • Parking: often charged separately, commonly several thousand to over ¥10,000 per month in the city. Some suburban listings require a parking contract.
  • Renewal fees: in many parts of Aichi, renewal fees (更新料) are less common than in Tokyo, but don't assume. Check your contract.
  • Garbage rules: Nagoya is known for detailed sorting rules. Mentioning that you understand them, and following them, matters to owners.
  • Initial costs: generally lower than Tokyo, but the guarantor fee, fire insurance, and key exchange still apply. See our hidden costs guide.

Compared with Tokyo, Nagoya has less choice of English-speaking agents but less competition for each unit. Compared with Osaka, Nagoya is more car-oriented and has fewer foreigner-specialist agents. Our full renting guide covers the national basics.

Frequently Asked Questions

Are there English-speaking real estate agents in Nagoya?

Yes, but there are far fewer than in Tokyo. Some agencies near Nagoya Station and Sakae advertise English support, and university housing offices can often recommend agents. Many foreigners succeed with a regular Japanese agent plus a Japanese-speaking friend for the first visit.

Is it legal for a landlord to refuse foreigners in Nagoya?

Japan does not have a comprehensive anti-discrimination law that clearly bans this in private rentals, so "no foreigners" listings still exist in practice. Arguing rarely helps. Filtering for foreigner-friendly listings and using an experienced agent is far more effective.

I work in Toyota City. Should I live in Nagoya or nearer work?

It depends on your commute and whether you drive. Living near work usually means cheaper rent and a car-based life, while Nagoya offers better transport and more foreigner-friendly agents. Ask your employer first, because many companies in the area offer housing support or a corporate lease.

Can I rent in Nagoya without a guarantor?

Yes. Most leases use a guarantor company instead of a personal guarantor, and UR housing and monthly apartments do not require one. See our no-guarantor guide for the full list of options.

How long does screening take in Nagoya?

Typically a few days to about a week after you submit the application. If the owner is an individual who reviews applications personally, it can take longer. If you have heard nothing after a week, ask the agent politely for an update.
