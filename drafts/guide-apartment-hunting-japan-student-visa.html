Student Visa Apartment Application Rejected in Japan? What to Do Next

You found a place near campus, filled out the application, and a few days later the agent called with bad news: the screening did not pass. No clear reason, just a polite apology. If you are on a Student status of residence (留学, ryūgaku), this is one of the most common walls you will hit in Japan, and it often lands right when your dormitory contract is ending or your language school term is about to start. The good news is that student rejections usually come from a small number of predictable causes, and most of them can be fixed.

What Actually Happens When a Student Gets Rejected

An apartment application in Japan typically goes through two checks: the landlord or management company, and a guarantor company (保証会社, hoshō-gaisha) that promises to cover the rent if you do not pay. As a student, you are usually flagged at one of these points:

  • The guarantor company declines because you have little or no income in Japan.
  • The landlord declines because your period of stay ends before the standard two-year lease does.
  • The application stalls because you have no Japanese emergency contact or no domestic phone number yet.
  • The listing was quietly restricted to working adults (社会人限定) or non-students, and nobody told you before you applied.

Agents rarely tell you which check failed. They may not know themselves, because guarantor companies usually do not share their reasons. That is why it helps to diagnose the likely cause yourself.

Why Student Applications Get Rejected

No income, or income that does not count

Guarantor companies look at whether the applicant can realistically pay the rent. A student with part-time work limited to around 28 hours a week, as standard student work permission allows, often earns less than many screening rules prefer for even a modest apartment. Scholarships and savings help, but some guarantor companies give them little weight unless you document them clearly.

Your remaining period of stay is short

Standard residential leases in Japan run for two years. If your residence card shows only a year left, or less, landlords worry you will leave suddenly or overstay. Language school students, whose permission is often granted in shorter blocks, feel this most.

Parents abroad do not count as guarantors in many systems

Your parents may be willing to back you, but many guarantor companies cannot verify or pursue someone overseas. A parent's signature from abroad is therefore often treated as an emergency contact at best, not as financial security.

Landlord fears about student tenants

Some owners have had bad experiences with any student, Japanese or foreign: noise, parties, unpaid utility bills, or tenants who vanish at graduation. Foreign students carry the added worry about communication. This is why some buildings are marked as unsuitable for students, regardless of nationality.

Missing local basics

If you arrived recently, you may not yet have a Japanese phone number, a bank account, or a registered address. Each gap makes the guarantor company's identity and contact checks harder, and incomplete applications are easy to reject.

Key tip: Your school's international office is your most underused asset. Many universities and some language schools have partnerships with agencies, act as or arrange guarantors, or run institutional guarantee schemes for international students. Ask them before you apply anywhere else.

Common Mistakes Students Make

  1. Applying for apartments sized for working adults. A rent that is fine for your budget on paper can look unaffordable relative to your documented income. Many students get approved simply by lowering the rent band they apply in.
  2. Not showing the money you actually have. If your parents send you a monthly allowance or you have a scholarship, the guarantor company needs to see it on paper. Saying "my parents will pay" without documents rarely helps.
  3. Applying to several places at once without telling anyone. Multiple simultaneous applications through different agents can create confusion, and if the same guarantor company sees repeated failed screenings, it does not help.
  4. Waiting until the last two weeks. Screening, contract paperwork, and key handover often take one to three weeks after approval. Students who start late end up desperate and accept bad deals.
  5. Ignoring alternatives. Student dormitories, share houses, and student-focused apartments are often easier and cheaper overall than a standard lease, especially for your first year.

Step-by-Step Fix: What to Do After a Rejection

  1. Ask the agent one specific question. Try: "Hoshō-gaisha no shinsa desu ka, ōnā-san no handan desu ka?" (保証会社の審査ですか、オーナーさんの判断ですか？ "Was it the guarantor company's screening or the owner's decision?"). Agents can often tell you this even if they cannot give the detailed reason. It tells you which problem to solve.
  2. If it was the guarantor company, strengthen the money side. Gather a bank balance certificate (残高証明書) from your Japanese bank or home bank, your scholarship award letter, and if possible a letter from your parents confirming monthly support with their bank statement. Ask the agent whether a different guarantor company is available for the same property; some landlords accept more than one.
  3. Offer to prepay. Some landlords and guarantor companies are more flexible if you offer to pay several months of rent in advance. This is negotiable, not standard, and you should only do it with a reputable agency and a written agreement showing the prepayment.
  4. If it was the landlord, change the property type. Ask specifically for listings marked 学生可 (students OK) or 学生歓迎 (students welcome), and for buildings already housing students from nearby universities. Properties close to campuses are often owned by landlords who rely on student demand.
  5. Go through your school. Ask the international student office whether they provide an institutional guarantee, a recommended agent list, or a comprehensive renters' insurance scheme for international students (some schools participate in such programs). An introduction from the school changes how an agent sees you.
  6. Use a no-guarantor housing option for now. Share houses typically skip the guarantor company and accept shorter stays. [PR] Oakhouse share houses are one example that does not require a guarantor. Our share house guide explains how they compare. Living in one for six months gives you a Japanese address, phone, and bank history, which makes the next application much stronger.
  7. Consider UR housing if you qualify. UR public rentals do not use a guarantor company or charge key money, but they have income or savings requirements; some students qualify with sufficient savings or a supporter. See our UR application guide for the details.
  8. Reapply with a cleaner file. When you apply again, submit everything at once: income, savings, school documents, emergency contact. A complete file at a slightly lower rent band succeeds far more often than a thin file at the top of your budget.

Required Documents for Student Applicants

  • Residence card (在留カード) showing Student status (留学) and expiry date
  • Passport
  • Certificate of enrollment (在学証明書, zaigaku shōmeisho) from your university or language school
  • Student ID card (学生証)
  • Scholarship award letter or certificate (奨学金証明書), if applicable
  • Bank balance certificate (残高証明書) for your own account, or your parents' account with a support letter
  • Payslips from part-time work (給与明細) and proof of work permission (資格外活動許可) stamped on your residence card
  • Emergency contact in Japan (緊急連絡先): a classmate, professor, school staff member, or friend, with their consent
  • Japanese phone number and, if available, certificate of residence (住民票)

City and Region Differences for Students

Tokyo: The densest student-housing market, with many agencies near major campuses and many share houses. Rents are highest, so the income gap is biggest. Students often find more success in areas along commuter lines a little further out rather than right next to central campuses.

Osaka and Kobe: Generally cheaper than Tokyo, and many neighborhoods have long-standing international communities. Small studios can be considerably cheaper than equivalents in central Tokyo, which helps the income-to-rent ratio. See our Osaka guide.

Kyoto: A huge student city with many old apartments built specifically for students, some managed by landlords who have rented to students for decades. Student co-ops at large universities often run their own housing introduction services, and many of these landlords accept international students without the same friction.

Fukuoka, Sendai, and other regional cities: Lower rents, many universities, and often more flexible landlords. Your school's housing list may cover most of the market, so start there.

If you are thinking about changing from a student visa to a work visa soon, keep that in mind: once you have a work status and an employer, your options open up considerably. Our visa guide covers how status of residence affects renting.

Frequently Asked Questions

Can I rent an apartment in Japan on a language school student visa?

Yes, but it is harder than for university students because language school periods of stay are often shorter and schools rarely offer guarantees. Many language school students start in a dormitory or share house arranged with the school, then move into a regular apartment once they have a longer period of stay or enroll in a university or vocational school.

Can my parents abroad be my guarantor?

Most guarantor companies cannot accept an overseas parent as the financial guarantor, though some landlords may accept them as an emergency contact or as supporting evidence of funds. Their bank statement and a signed support letter can still help your screening, so prepare them even if they cannot formally guarantee you.

Will working part-time help me pass the screening?

It helps, especially if you can show several months of steady payslips. Student work permission usually limits hours to around 28 per week during term, so part-time income alone may not cover a higher rent. Combining payslips with a scholarship letter or savings proof makes the strongest case.

How much should I budget for moving in?

Upfront costs for a standard apartment often add up to several months' rent once you include the deposit, key money if any, brokerage fee, guarantor company fee, insurance, and the first month's rent. Share houses and UR housing are usually much cheaper to enter. Our article on key money explains one of the biggest variables.

Should I tell the agent I am a student up front?

Yes. The agent will find out at application anyway, and telling them early means they can filter for properties marked students OK and choose a guarantor company that works with students. Hiding it only leads to more rejections and wasted application fees.
