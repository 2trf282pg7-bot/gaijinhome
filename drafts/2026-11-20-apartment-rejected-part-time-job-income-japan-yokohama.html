Apartment Rejected Because of Part-Time Income in Yokohama? What to Do Next

You found an apartment in Yokohama that fits your budget. Maybe it is a small 1K near Tsurumi, a room a few minutes from Kannai Station, or something along the Sotetsu line where rents are a little gentler. You submitted the application, waited a few days, and then the agent called back: the screening did not pass. When you asked why, the answer was vague, but the hint was clear. Your income is part-time.

This rejection is extremely common, and it often feels unfair. You may pay your bills on time, you may have savings, and the rent may be well within what you actually earn. But Japanese rental screening was built around a specific picture of the "safe" tenant: a full-time employee with a fixed monthly salary and a long employment history. Part-time work, shift work, and arubaito (アルバイト) income do not fit that picture neatly, and foreign renters with part-time jobs get hit twice, because they also have to clear the extra checks that come with being a non-Japanese applicant.

The good news is that a part-time income rejection is one of the more fixable problems in Japanese renting. This article explains what is happening behind the scenes, the mistakes that make it worse, and a practical plan to get approved in Yokohama or anywhere else in Kanagawa.

What actually happens

When you apply for an apartment, your application usually goes to two places: the landlord or management company, and a rent guarantee company (家賃保証会社). Most rentals in the Yokohama area now require a guarantee company, especially when the applicant is foreign or has no Japanese family guarantor. The guarantee company promises to pay the landlord if you stop paying rent, so it is the one that looks hardest at your income.

On the application form, you write your employer, your employment type, how long you have worked there, and your annual income. When you tick the box for part-time (パート or アルバイト) instead of full-time employee (正社員), several things happen:

  • The screener compares your stated annual income with the monthly rent. A rough rule many agents use is that rent should be around one third of monthly income or less, and part-time applicants are often held to that line more strictly.
  • They look at stability. Hourly work that can be cut at short notice is treated as riskier than a monthly salary, even if your actual earnings are similar.
  • If you are on a student or dependent visa, they may check whether your work hours are limited by your permission to engage in activity other than that permitted (資格外活動許可), which generally caps part-time work at 28 hours per week during term time. That cap limits how much income they believe you can realistically earn.
  • They consider your period of stay, your emergency contact, and whether you can communicate in Japanese if a problem comes up.

If the overall picture looks risky, the guarantee company declines, and the landlord usually follows. The agent then tells you it "did not pass," often without detail, because guarantee companies rarely explain their reasons.

Why it happens

Understanding the structure helps you fix the right thing instead of guessing.

Guarantee companies score stability, not just amount

A guarantee company is taking on the risk of paying your rent for months if something goes wrong. Its screening favors predictable income. A full-time contract with a fixed salary scores well. Part-time income, especially when it is new, varies month to month, or comes from several small jobs, scores lower. Some guarantee companies are more flexible with part-time workers than others, which is why the same application can fail at one company and pass at another.

Landlords are risk-averse by default

Many landlords in Yokohama are individuals who own one or two buildings. A single unpaid month matters to them. When they see "foreign applicant" and "part-time" together, some of them simply prefer to wait for a different tenant. This is not always about you personally. It is about how they perceive the chance of a problem they cannot easily solve.

Visa type limits the income they believe in

If you hold a student or dependent visa, screeners know your work hours are restricted. Even if you currently earn well, they may assume the cap limits your income. If your stay depends on someone else, such as a spouse or a school, they may want to see that person or institution involved in the application.

Period of stay and contract length

Standard leases in Japan are usually two years. If your residence card shows a shorter remaining period, and your income is part-time, the combination makes the application look weaker than either factor alone.

Language and emergency contact

Guarantee companies need to reach someone if rent is late or there is an emergency. If you have no Japanese-speaking emergency contact, that adds another small risk point on top of the income question. Our article on rejections due to no emergency contact covers this issue in detail.

Common mistakes

  1. Applying for rent that is too high for the stated income. If your annual income on the form is low relative to the rent, no amount of explaining will help. Many part-time applicants apply for the room they want rather than the room the screening will approve.
  2. Writing a vague or understated income. Some people write only their income from one job, or guess low. If you have more than one part-time job, regular freelance income, or support from family, the screener needs to see the full picture, with documents.
  3. Not mentioning savings. Savings are often not asked about directly, but a bank balance certificate can shift a borderline application. Many applicants never offer it.
  4. Applying through only one agent and one guarantee company. If an agent always uses the same guarantee company, you may be failing the same screening over and over. Asking which guarantee company was used, and whether another one is possible, can change the result.
  5. Reapplying immediately with the same information. Submitting the same application to another property with the same guarantee company usually produces the same outcome. Change something first: the rent level, the documents, the guarantor setup, or the property type.

Step-by-step fix

  1. Ask the agent what failed and which guarantee company screened you. You can say: 「どの保証会社で審査しましたか？収入面が理由でしょうか？」 ("Which guarantee company did the screening? Was it because of income?"). Even a partial answer tells you whether to focus on income, visa period, or contact details.
  2. Recalculate your target rent. Take your realistic total monthly income from all sources, and aim for rent of about one third of that or lower, including management fees (管理費). If you earn around 150,000 yen a month, that points to roughly 50,000 yen or less, which in Yokohama usually means older buildings, smaller rooms, or areas a bit further from the main stations.
  3. Gather proof of all your income. Bring recent payslips from every job, a certificate of employment if your employer will issue one, and if you have been in Japan long enough, a tax certificate from your city office showing your previous year's income. If you have freelance or remote income from abroad, bring bank statements showing regular deposits.
  4. Show savings. Ask your bank for a balance certificate (残高証明書). A solid balance that covers many months of rent tells the screener you can pay even if your shifts are cut. Some landlords will also consider prepaying several months of rent upfront; ask the agent if this is possible for the property.
  5. Add a stronger supporting party if you can. If you are a student, ask whether your school offers housing support or can act as a contact. If you are on a dependent visa, applying with your spouse as the main contract holder is often easier. A Japanese friend or colleague willing to be an emergency contact also helps.   6. Ask for a different guarantee company. Say: 「別の保証会社で審査をお願いできますか？」 ("Could you try the screening with a different guarantee company?"). Some agents work with several companies, and some of those are more used to foreign and part-time applicants.
  7. Use foreigner-friendly agents and properties. Agents who regularly work with international tenants know which landlords accept part-time income. They can save you weeks of rejections. Our rejected application page lists general strategies that apply here too.
  8. Consider furnished rentals or share houses as a bridge. Furnished apartments and share houses often have simpler screening and lower upfront costs.   9. Budget for the full move-in cost. Never inflate your income or invent an employer on the application. Guarantee companies may call your workplace or ask for documents, and a false statement can lead to an immediate rejection or problems with the contract later. It is always better to apply for a cheaper room with honest numbers.

Required documents

Bring as many of these as apply to you. A complete, organized file makes a part-time application look much more reliable.

  • Residence card (在留カード), front and back copies
  • Passport
  • Recent payslips from all jobs (給与明細), ideally the last three months
  • Certificate of employment (在職証明書), if your employer can issue one
  • Tax certificate showing last year's income (課税証明書 or 所得証明書), if you lived in Japan last year
  • Withholding tax slip (源泉徴収票), if you received one from your employer
  • Bank balance certificate (残高証明書) or recent bank statements
  • Student ID and certificate of enrollment (在学証明書), if you are a student
  • Permission to work part-time (資格外活動許可), if your visa requires it
  • Emergency contact details, ideally someone who speaks Japanese
  • Japanese phone number and, if possible, a Japanese bank account for rent payments

City / region differences

Yokohama is large and varied, and your chances with part-time income depend a lot on where you look.

  • Minato Mirai, Kannai, and the area around Yokohama Station: Newer buildings and higher rents. Managed by larger companies with stricter screening. Part-time applicants often struggle here unless the rent is well below their means.
  • Shin-Yokohama and Kohoku: Popular with commuters and students. A mix of new and older buildings. Older, smaller units can be realistic for part-time workers.
  • Tsurumi, Kanagawa Ward, and Naka Ward outside the center: Areas with a longer history of foreign residents, and some landlords who are more used to international tenants. Older apartments here are often more affordable.
  • Totsuka, Hodogaya, Izumi, and lines like Sotetsu: Further from the center, but rents tend to be lower, which makes the income-to-rent ratio easier to meet.

Compared with central Tokyo, Yokohama rents are usually somewhat lower for similar space, which helps part-time renters. Compared with Osaka, the screening culture is similar, but rents are often a bit higher. If you are flexible about city, our Osaka renting page may be worth a look. Wherever you search, the same principles apply: honest income, the right rent level, supporting documents, and an agent who knows which landlords accept part-time tenants.

Frequently Asked Questions

Can I rent an apartment in Yokohama with only part-time income?

Yes. Many foreigners do. The key is choosing rent that fits your income, showing proof of all income sources and savings, and working with an agent and guarantee company that accept part-time applicants. Older or smaller units outside the central areas are usually the most realistic starting point.

How much do I need to earn to rent a 1K in Yokohama?

There is no fixed rule, but many agents look for rent of about one third of monthly income or less. A 1K in outer Yokohama might range from roughly 45,000 to 70,000 yen per month depending on age and location, so the income needed varies. Savings and a strong supporting party can help when you are close to the line.

Will showing my savings really help?

It often helps, especially on borderline applications. A bank balance certificate shows you can cover rent even if your hours change. It does not guarantee approval, because every guarantee company has its own criteria, but it gives the screener a reason to say yes.

I am a student working 28 hours a week. Is that enough?

It can be, if the rent is modest. Screeners know about the work-hour limit, so they may also look at your scholarship, family support, or savings. Including proof of these, along with your certificate of enrollment, usually strengthens the application.

Should I try a share house instead?

A share house or furnished apartment can be a good bridge if regular apartments keep rejecting you. Screening is usually simpler and the move-in cost lower. After some months of stable income and on-time payments in Japan, applying for a regular apartment becomes easier.
