Bank Account Rejected in Japan as a Foreigner? Real Reasons and What to Do Next

You queued at the bank, filled in a form you could barely read, handed over your residence card, and then heard the words nobody wants to hear: "Sorry, we can't open an account for you today." Sometimes you get a vague reason. Often you get none at all. If you've just been turned away, you are far from alone. Bank account refusals are one of the most common frustrations for newcomers in Japan, and they hit at the worst possible moment, because your salary, rent, phone contract and utilities all depend on having one.

The good news: most rejections are not personal and not permanent. They usually come down to a small number of predictable issues that you can fix, or work around, within days or weeks. This article explains what's really going on behind the counter and gives you a clear plan to get an account open.

What actually happens

Bank refusals in Japan rarely look like a formal "denied" letter. In practice, you'll usually experience one of these:

  • Refused at the counter on the spot. The staff check your residence card and tell you the bank can't proceed, sometimes citing "less than six months in Japan" or "no Japanese phone number."
  • Asked to come back later. You're told to return once you have a specific document, such as proof of employment, a hanko (personal seal), or an updated address on your card.
  • Application accepted, then quietly declined. Particularly with online banks, you submit everything through an app and later receive an email saying the bank "cannot open an account based on a comprehensive assessment," with no further detail.
  • Account opened with limits. You get an account, but without a debit card, online banking, or international transfers until later.

Banks are generally not required to tell you why they refused, and front-line staff often don't know the full internal criteria themselves. That's why it helps to understand the structural reasons, so you can work out which one probably applies to you.

Why it happens

The "six-month rule" and non-resident status

Under Japan's foreign exchange framework, people who have been in Japan for less than six months are often treated as non-residents for banking purposes, unless they work for a Japanese employer or meet certain other conditions. Many banks apply their own internal version of this rule and simply decline anyone who arrived recently and can't show local employment. This is the single most common reason newcomers, students, and working holiday visitors are turned away. Policies vary by bank and even by branch, so one refusal doesn't mean every bank will say the same.

Anti-money-laundering checks

Japanese banks have tightened identity and fraud checks over the past several years, partly due to accounts being sold or misused after their owners leave Japan. As a result, banks look closely at how long you'll be in the country, why you need the account, and whether your details line up across documents. A short remaining period of stay is a red flag for them, because an account left behind can be exploited.

Remaining period of stay

If your residence card shows only a few months left before expiry, some banks will decline or ask you to come back after renewing. They want reasonable confidence that you'll be in Japan, and reachable, for the life of the account.

Address and phone number problems

Your address on the back of your residence card must match what you write on the application, character for character in many cases. If you've moved and haven't updated it at the ward office, that mismatch alone can cause a rejection. Banks also send a cash card or confirmation by registered post, so a temporary address like a hotel, Airbnb, or a friend's place often won't work. Many banks also require a Japanese mobile number for verification and security codes.

Language and communication

Some branches are simply uncomfortable opening accounts when they can't confirm you understand the terms, particularly rules about not selling or lending your account. This isn't a legal requirement to speak Japanese, but a branch may decline if staff feel the conversation isn't getting through.

Purpose of the account

Banks ask why you're opening the account. Answers like "to receive salary," "to pay rent," or "for daily living expenses" are normal. Vague answers, or mentioning business, investment, or receiving money from abroad without context, may trigger extra scrutiny.

Common mistakes

  1. Applying before registering your address. Going to the bank before you've done residence registration at the ward office means your card has no address, or the wrong one. Banks almost always need this first.
  2. Using a temporary or hotel address. Even if it's technically where you sleep now, banks need somewhere stable to post your cash card.
  3. Going without a Japanese phone number. A foreign number frequently stops the process. Getting a local number first saves a second trip.
  4. Not bringing proof of employment or enrolment. If you've been in Japan less than six months, a letter from your employer or school can be the difference between yes and no.
  5. Giving up after one branch. Policies differ between banks and sometimes between branches of the same bank. One "no" is not a final answer for the whole country.

Step-by-step fix

  1. Find out which reason applies. Politely ask: "What do I need to bring next time to open an account?" (Tsugi wa nani o motte kureba ii desu ka?). Staff are often more willing to tell you what's missing than to explain why you were refused.
  2. Fix your residence registration. If your card address is blank or outdated, visit your city or ward office and update it. Ask for a copy of your residence certificate (juminhyo) at the same time; it costs a few hundred yen and is useful for banks, phone carriers, and landlords.
  3. Get a Japanese phone number. If you don't have one yet, a local SIM or eSIM with a Japanese number is often the quickest route. Some data-only plans don't include a voice number, so check before buying. If you need connectivity while you sort out a full contract, [PR] a Japan eSIM can keep you online in the meantime.
  4. Collect proof of why you're in Japan. Ask your employer HR for a certificate of employment (zaishoku shomeisho) or an offer letter, or ask your school for a certificate of enrolment (zaigaku shomeisho). If you're within your first six months, this document is often what banks need to treat you as a resident.
  5. Ask your employer or school which bank they use. Many companies and universities have a relationship with a nearby branch that regularly opens accounts for their foreign staff or students. A short introduction from HR or the international office can make the process routine.
  6. Try Japan Post Bank (Yucho). Japan Post Bank, available at post offices nationwide, is widely regarded as one of the more accessible options for newcomers. It still has requirements, and some branches apply a six-month condition, but it's often worth trying next.
  7. Consider online or app-based banks once you qualify. Several Japanese online banks accept foreign residents through a smartphone application with ID verification. They usually still need a valid residence card, a registered address, and a Japanese phone number, and some have their own residency or visa-period requirements.
  8. Prepare a simple purpose statement. When asked why you need the account, say clearly: "To receive my salary and pay rent and bills" (Kyuryo no uketori to yachin, kokyo ryokin no shiharai no tame desu).
  9. Bridge the gap while you wait. If you can't open a Japanese account yet, use a foreign card or a multi-currency account from home for everyday spending, and ask your employer whether they can pay you temporarily by another method. Our article on renting an apartment in Japan without a bank account covers how to handle rent during this period.
  10. Reapply after six months or after renewal. If the issue is time in Japan or a short remaining visa period, the simplest fix is often to wait until you pass the six-month mark or until your new card is issued, then return with updated documents.
Never buy, borrow, or rent someone else's bank account, and never sell or hand over your own when you leave Japan. Transferring a bank account to another person is illegal in Japan and can lead to serious consequences, including problems with future visa applications. When you leave, close your account properly at the bank.

Required documents

Exact requirements vary by bank, but bringing everything below will cover most situations:

  • Residence card (在留カード, zairyu kado) with your current address registered
  • Passport
  • Japanese mobile phone number
  • Certificate of residence (住民票, juminhyo), issued recently
  • Certificate of employment (在職証明書, zaishoku shomeisho) or certificate of enrolment (在学証明書, zaigaku shomeisho)
  • Health insurance card or My Number-related documents, if you have them (マイナンバー, My Number)
  • Personal seal (印鑑, inkan / hanko), if the bank requires one; many now accept a signature
  • Employer or school contact details, including a phone number

City / region differences

Tokyo: Branches in areas with many international residents and corporate offices are often more used to foreign customers, and some have English-speaking staff or English forms. At the same time, busy central branches can be stricter and quicker to say no. If one branch refuses, a branch near your home or office may handle things differently.

Osaka and Kansai: Large cities like Osaka, Kyoto, and Kobe have plenty of experience with students and workers from abroad, especially near universities. Your school's international office is often the best route. If you're also moving here, our Osaka housing page explains the local rental steps you'll face at the same time.

Regional cities and rural areas: Local and regional banks may have less experience with foreign applicants, and English support can be limited. On the other hand, smaller branches sometimes take more time to help once they know your employer. Japan Post Bank is often the most practical option outside major cities because post offices are everywhere.

Frequently Asked Questions

Can I open a bank account in Japan within my first six months?

Often yes, but it depends on your situation. If you work for a Japanese company or are enrolled at a Japanese school, many banks will open an account if you bring proof of employment or enrolment. People on short stays, or without local employment, are more likely to be refused until they pass six months.

Why did the bank refuse me without giving a reason?

Japanese banks generally aren't obliged to explain their decisions, and they make "comprehensive assessments" using internal criteria. Instead of asking why, ask what documents you should bring next time. That question usually gets a more useful answer.

Do I need a hanko to open a bank account?

Not always. Many banks now accept a signature, especially for foreign residents, but some branches still ask for a personal seal. If you're unsure, an inexpensive hanko made with your name in katakana can be ordered quickly at a stamp shop and avoids a second trip.

Can I open an account with a short visa period remaining?

It can be harder. If your residence card expires within a few months, some banks will ask you to come back after renewal. If you've already applied for an extension, bring the stamp or notice showing that your renewal application is in progress, and explain your situation.

Will a bank rejection affect my credit or my apartment application?

A counter refusal is not the same as a credit failure, and it isn't usually recorded as a credit event. However, without a bank account you may struggle to set up rent payments, which can affect your apartment application. If that's happening, read our article on apartment rejections and what to do.
