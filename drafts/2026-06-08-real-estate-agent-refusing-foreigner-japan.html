Why Are Real Estate Agents Refusing to Work With You in Japan?

You walked into a real estate office, or sent an inquiry through a listing site, and the answer was some version of "sorry, we can't help." Maybe they said the apartment was already taken, even though it is still online the next day. Maybe they stopped replying after you mentioned your nationality. Maybe a staff member simply looked uncomfortable and handed you a flyer. It feels personal, and it is frustrating, but the refusal usually has less to do with you than with how rental brokerage works in Japan. Once you understand the mechanics, you can route around them.

What Actually Happens When an Agent Turns You Away

Refusals from agents (不動産会社, fudōsan-gaisha) tend to follow a few recognizable patterns:

  • The phantom "already rented" reply. You ask about a specific listing and are told it was just taken. Sometimes that is true; Tokyo and Osaka listings move fast. But if the same unit stays online for weeks, the real reason is often that the landlord has marked it as not accepting foreign tenants.
  • Silence after the first email. You inquire in English, the agent cannot answer comfortably in English, and the inquiry quietly drops to the bottom of the pile.
  • "We don't handle foreigners." Some small neighborhood offices say this openly. They are not usually making a policy statement; they mean their landlords will not approve and they do not want to spend hours on an application that will fail.
  • Conditions you cannot meet. The agent insists on a Japanese guarantor, a Japanese-speaking emergency contact, or a long employment history, knowing you do not have one.

In every case, the agent is making a fast calculation about whether your application is likely to close. Your job is to change that calculation.

Why Agents Refuse: The Structural Reasons

The agent works for the deal, not the landlord's preference list

Most rental agents in Japan earn a brokerage fee (仲介手数料, chūkai tesūryō) only when a lease is signed. By law, the total fee is capped at roughly one month's rent plus consumption tax. That means every hour spent on an application that the landlord or guarantor company later rejects is unpaid work. If an agent believes foreign applicants are more likely to be rejected, they ration their time accordingly.

Landlords filter before agents ever see you

Many listings carry internal notes the public never sees, such as 外国人不可 (no foreigners) or 外国人要相談 (foreigners: consult first). The agent is bound by what the owner or management company allows. An agent saying no is often just relaying a decision that was made long before you walked in.

Guarantor company screening is a second gate

Even when the landlord is open, most leases now require a guarantor company (保証会社, hoshō-gaisha). Each company has its own criteria. Some will not approve applicants without a Japanese emergency contact, a long remaining period of stay, or a domestic phone number. Experienced agents know which guarantor companies their landlords use and which of those tend to reject foreigners, so they pre-reject you to save time.

Language and after-move-in worries

Landlords and agents frequently worry less about rent payment and more about what happens after move-in: garbage separation, noise complaints, subletting, sudden departure when a visa ends, or not being able to explain a water leak by phone. Fair or not, these concerns are why "can you speak Japanese?" often comes up early.

No broad legal requirement to accept you

Japan does not have a comprehensive law that specifically bans housing refusal based on nationality. Courts have occasionally awarded damages in clear-cut discrimination cases, and MLIT (the ministry overseeing real estate) has published guidance and multilingual materials encouraging agents to serve foreign residents, but in daily practice there is little a renter can do to force an agent to take on an application. That is why switching strategy works better than arguing.

Key tip: Agents decide in the first two minutes whether you are "easy" or "risky." Lead with your visa expiry date, your employer or school, your monthly income, and the fact that you can use a guarantor company. Answer their unspoken questions before they ask.

Common Mistakes That Make Agents Say No

  1. Inquiring about one listing at a time. A single-listing inquiry gives the agent an easy exit: "that one is gone." Ask instead what they have that fits your budget and area and accepts foreign applicants.
  2. Using a big general portal only. Large portals aggregate listings from offices that may never respond to English inquiries. You end up chasing the same rejecting offices over and over.
  3. Hiding the visa situation. If your status of residence expires in seven months, the agent will find out at application time anyway. Being upfront lets them pick landlords and guarantor companies that accept shorter remaining periods.
  4. Showing up with no documents. Arriving without your residence card, proof of income, or a passport tells the agent the process will be slow. Agents prioritize customers who can apply the same day.
  5. Taking the first "no" as the market's answer. One office refusing says very little. A different office two streets away may have a completely different landlord network.

Step-by-Step Fix: Getting an Agent to Work With You

  1. Prepare a one-page "renter profile." Include your name, nationality, status of residence and expiry date, employer or school, monthly take-home income, desired move-in date, budget, preferred areas, and whether you have a Japanese emergency contact. Have it in English and, ideally, a simple Japanese version. Email it with every inquiry. It turns a vague request into a ready-to-process file.
  2. Target foreigner-focused agencies first. Several agencies specialize in international clients, employ English (or Chinese, Korean, Vietnamese) speaking staff, and maintain lists of landlords who accept foreign tenants. They exist because ordinary offices turn foreigners away. Their fees are usually the same legally capped brokerage fee.
  3. Ask the right question in Japanese. Instead of asking about one unit, ask: "Gaikokuseki demo mōshikomi kanō na bukken wa arimasu ka?" (外国籍でも申し込み可能な物件はありますか？ "Do you have properties where foreign nationals can apply?"). This shows you understand the system and invites the agent to search their own database rather than defend a single listing.
  4. Say up front that you will use a guarantor company. Tell the agent you are happy to use whichever guarantor company the landlord requires, and ask which ones accept foreign applicants. If you do not have a Japanese emergency contact, ask whether a coworker, school office, or the guarantor company's multilingual support line can fill that role.
  5. Bring your employer or school into it. A short letter or email from HR or your international student office confirming your position and expected duration carries real weight. Some employers will sign a corporate lease (法人契約, hōjin keiyaku), which removes most of the agent's concerns.
  6. Widen your filters. Older buildings, units a few minutes further from the station, and properties managed by large management companies tend to be more flexible. Ask the agent which of those they would try first.
  7. Use a no-agent route as a parallel plan. Share houses, serviced apartments, and public UR housing all bypass the typical agent-and-landlord filter. For example, [PR] CrossOneRoom furnished Tokyo apartments are set up for international residents, and the UR route is explained in our UR housing guide for foreigners. A short-term place also lets you keep apartment hunting without pressure.
  8. Follow up once, politely, then move on. If an agent goes silent, send one follow-up after two or three business days. If there is still no answer, drop them. Time spent chasing an uninterested office is time not spent with one that will help.

If an agent did take your application but the landlord or guarantor company rejected it, the problem is different. Our guide on why apartment applications get rejected covers that stage.

Documents to Have Ready Before You Contact Any Agent

  • Residence card (在留カード, zairyū kādo), front and back
  • Passport with your current landing permission or visa
  • Certificate of employment (在職証明書, zaishoku shōmeisho) or certificate of enrollment (在学証明書) for students
  • Recent payslips (給与明細, usually the last two or three months) or employment contract showing salary if newly hired
  • Tax certificate (課税証明書, kazei shōmeisho) if you have lived in Japan for over a year
  • Certificate of residence (住民票, jūminhyō) if you are already registered at a Japanese address
  • Name, phone number, and relationship of an emergency contact in Japan (緊急連絡先)
  • Japanese phone number and bank account details, if you have them
  • Your one-page renter profile in English and Japanese

City and Region Differences

Tokyo has the largest pool of foreigner-friendly agencies and English-speaking staff, especially around Minato, Shibuya, Shinjuku, and areas with many international offices. Competition for good units is also fiercest, so agents are quickest to drop slow applicants. Being document-ready matters most here.

Osaka has fewer English-focused agencies but a long history of residents from Korea, China, and Southeast Asia, so many landlords in areas like Ikuno, Naniwa, and Nishinari are used to foreign tenants. Local agents may be more relaxed but less likely to answer email in English; calling or visiting with a Japanese-speaking friend can change everything. Our Osaka renting guide goes deeper.

Kyoto, Fukuoka, Nagoya, and smaller cities often rely on small family-run offices with long-standing landlord relationships. These can be the hardest to crack cold, but a referral from your school, employer, or a coworker's agent often opens the door immediately. In university towns, the student cooperative or international office frequently keeps lists of agents that work with foreign students.

Frequently Asked Questions

Is it legal for a real estate agent in Japan to refuse me because I am a foreigner?

Japan has no comprehensive law that specifically prohibits private landlords or agents from refusing tenants based on nationality, although courts have sometimes ruled against particularly blatant cases and government guidance discourages it. In practice, the faster solution is to work with agents and landlords who already accept foreign tenants rather than trying to force a reluctant one.

Will speaking Japanese make agents more willing to help?

Basic Japanese helps, mainly because it reassures the agent that you can handle calls from the management company and follow building rules. You do not need to be fluent. Bringing a Japanese-speaking friend or coworker to the first visit, or using a multilingual agency, achieves much the same effect.

Do foreigner-focused agencies charge more?

Not necessarily. The brokerage fee is capped by law at around one month's rent plus tax, and most reputable foreigner-focused agencies charge within that limit. Watch the other move-in costs instead, which vary by property; see our breakdown of hidden costs when renting in Japan.

An agent said the apartment was taken, but it is still listed. Should I push back?

Listings sometimes stay online for a few days after a contract is agreed, so it may be true. If it stays up for weeks, the more likely explanation is a landlord restriction. Rather than arguing, ask the agent whether they have similar units in the same area that accept foreign applicants. That keeps the relationship intact and moves you forward.

What if every agent wants a Japanese guarantor?

Very few leases truly require an individual Japanese guarantor today; most use guarantor companies. If an agent insists, it usually means that particular landlord is inflexible. Ask specifically for properties that accept a guarantor company alone, and read our guide to renting without a guarantor for alternatives.
