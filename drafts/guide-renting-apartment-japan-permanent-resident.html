Permanent Resident Rejected for Apartment in Japan? Why It Still Happens and What to Do

You have lived in Japan for years. You earned permanent residency (永住者), you pay Japanese taxes, and maybe you speak the language well. Then an apartment application comes back with a rejection, or the agent quietly says the owner "prefers Japanese tenants." It is deeply frustrating, because permanent residency was supposed to remove exactly this kind of barrier. The good news is that PR status really is a strong card in the rental market. It just needs to be played the right way, and some barriers have nothing to do with visas at all.

What Actually Happens

Permanent residents typically run into one of these scenarios:

  • Blanket refusal at the listing stage. The agent checks with the owner and is told "no foreigners" (外国籍不可) before even looking at your status.
  • Guarantor company rejection. The screening fails, often with no explanation, despite your stable status.
  • A request for a Japanese guarantor. Even with a guarantor company, the landlord asks for a Japanese joint guarantor (連帯保証人) as well.
  • Problems at a life change. Applying after retiring, changing jobs, becoming self-employed or divorcing — moments when income documents look weaker.
  • Applying for a family home. Larger apartments and houses are often owned by individual landlords who are more cautious than corporate owners.

Why It Still Happens

Some owners filter by nationality, not status

Some landlords decide "no foreign nationals" as a blanket rule and never look at the residence status. Your residence card says 永住者, but the owner only hears "foreign tenant." Japan does not have a specific nationwide law that clearly bans this kind of refusal in private rentals, so it still occurs. Many agents and local governments discourage it, and the situation has improved, but it has not disappeared.

Guarantor companies care about credit and income, not visas

PR solves the "visa period shorter than lease" problem, but guarantor companies also look at other things. Card-affiliated companies can check credit bureaus such as CIC and JICC. Long-term residents are more likely than newcomers to have a credit history — which means more chance of an old late payment on a phone, card or loan showing up. Read our credit check guide if you suspect this.

Income documents at a transition point

If you recently left a company, started freelancing or retired, your most recent tax certificate may not reflect your current situation. Screening is based on documents, and documents lag behind life.

Joint guarantor demands are a custom

Some landlords still want a personal joint guarantor, usually a relative with income in Japan. Many permanent residents have no relatives in Japan who can sign, so this requirement blocks them even when everything else is in order. See our guide on what to do when a guarantor is refused.

Older landlords and age-related screening

Older permanent residents may also face the same age-related caution Japanese seniors experience, especially for single-person households. This is a separate issue from nationality, but the two can combine.

Common Mistakes Permanent Residents Make

  1. Not stating PR status upfront. If the agent just tells the owner "a foreign applicant," your strongest advantage is lost. Your status should be mentioned at the first inquiry.
  2. Assuming PR guarantees approval. It removes one risk but not credit, income or guarantor issues. Prepare the full file anyway.
  3. Using an agent who gives up easily. Some agents stop at the first "no foreigners" answer. You need one who will search owners who welcome long-term residents.
  4. Applying right after a job change. If you can, apply before leaving your old job, or wait until you have your new employment certificate and a few pay slips.
  5. Taking refusals personally and arguing with the agent. The agent usually is not the decision-maker. Use them as an ally to find a better owner.

Step-by-Step Fix

  1. Lead with your status in Japanese. When you contact an agent, say clearly: 「永住権を持っています。在日〇年で、日本語でのやり取りも可能です。」 ("I have permanent residency, have lived in Japan for X years and can communicate in Japanese.") Ask them to pass this exact information to the owner before the owner decides.
  2. Ask the agent to pre-check the owner's policy. Before you view, ask: 「オーナー様は永住者の入居は問題ないでしょうか？」 This avoids wasting a viewing on an owner with a blanket rule.
  3. Prepare "Japanese-resident equivalent" documents. Provide what a Japanese applicant would: certificate of residence (住民票) — which for foreign residents shows your status — tax certificate, certificate of employment and a Japanese emergency contact. Presenting a familiar, complete file reassures cautious owners.
  4. Replace the joint guarantor. If a joint guarantor is demanded, ask whether the guarantor company alone can be accepted, or offer a higher guarantor plan, an extra month of deposit, or rent paid in advance. Many landlords accept a guarantor company in place of a person when asked directly.
  5. Fix any credit issue. If you were rejected without explanation, request your records from CIC and JICC. If there is a negative entry, ask the agent for properties using non-card guarantor companies.
  6. Target corporate owners and foreigner-friendly properties. Large management companies, newer buildings owned by funds, and UR public housing tend to apply standard rules rather than personal preferences. UR does not require a guarantor and permanent residents are eligible — see our UR application guide.
  7. Use a temporary base if you are under time pressure. If you must move quickly — after a sale, a divorce or a job relocation — a furnished apartment or share house can bridge the gap while you search properly. [PR] CrossOneRoom furnished Tokyo apartments are aimed at foreign residents.
  8. If you believe you faced discrimination, ask for help. Local governments and international associations (国際交流協会) in many cities offer housing consultation for foreign residents. The Ministry of Justice human rights counseling service also accepts consultations. They cannot force a landlord to rent to you, but they can advise and sometimes mediate.
Key tip: Ask your agent to write "永住者" and your years in Japan directly in the application memo sent to the owner. Many owners who refuse "foreigners" in general will accept a permanent resident once they see it in writing.

Required Documents

  • Residence card (在留カード) showing status 永住者, with valid card dates
  • Certificate of residence (住民票), recently issued
  • Tax certificate (課税証明書) or income certificate (所得証明書) for the latest year
  • Withholding slip (源泉徴収票) if employed
  • Certificate of employment (在職証明書) or, if self-employed, your tax return copy (確定申告書の控え)
  • Pension or savings documentation if retired (年金証書, bank statements)
  • Emergency contact (緊急連絡先) in Japan — spouse, friend or colleague who has agreed
  • Personal seal (印鑑) and seal registration certificate (印鑑証明書) if a joint guarantor is involved

City and Region Differences

Tokyo

Tokyo has the largest number of foreign residents and the most agents used to working with them, so permanent residents generally have more options here. Corporate-owned buildings in areas such as Shinagawa, Koto and Chuo often follow standardized screening where PR status helps clearly. Older, individually owned buildings in quiet residential wards are where blanket refusals are more common. Family-sized units are competitive, so be ready to apply quickly.

Osaka and Kansai

Osaka has long-established communities of foreign residents, including many permanent and special permanent residents, and many local agents are experienced with them. Contract terms such as security payments and shikibiki differ from Tokyo; see our Osaka guide.

Regional areas

In rural and smaller regional areas, foreign residents are fewer and owners may be less familiar with residence statuses. On the other hand, vacancy rates are often higher, so owners have stronger incentives to accept a stable long-term tenant. Personal introductions through your employer or local community can be very effective. Some municipalities also run support programs for people who struggle to find housing, sometimes called 居住支援 services.

Frequently Asked Questions

Can a landlord legally refuse me because I am a foreigner, even with PR?

Japan does not have a broad, specific law that clearly prohibits private landlords from refusing foreign tenants, so refusals still happen. Such refusals are widely discouraged, and in some cases courts have found discriminatory treatment unlawful, but outcomes depend on the facts. Consult a housing advice service or lawyer if you want to pursue it.

Do permanent residents need a guarantor company?

Usually yes, in the same way Japanese tenants do. Guarantor companies are standard for most rentals. PR status helps you pass, but it does not remove the requirement. UR housing is a notable exception that does not use one.

Does PR help with the credit check?

It helps with visa-related risk, because there is no stay period to worry about. But credit checks look at income and credit records too. A late payment in CIC or JICC can still cause a rejection even for a permanent resident.

I recently retired. How can I rent as a permanent resident?

Prepare pension certificates and bank statements showing you can cover rent for a long period, and consider offering advance rent. UR housing has savings-based options for applicants without regular salary, and some guarantor companies specialize in older tenants.

Should I mention PR when I first contact the agent?

Yes, always. Mention it in your first message along with how long you have lived in Japan and your job. This lets the agent present you correctly to owners from the start rather than as a generic foreign applicant.
