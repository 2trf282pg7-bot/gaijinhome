Denied by docomo, SoftBank or au as a Foreigner? Why the Store Refused You and What to Do Next

You waited at the counter, handed over your residence card, picked a plan, and then the staff member came back with an apologetic face and said something like "moushiwake gozaimasen" — we are very sorry, we cannot proceed. No clear reason, no written explanation, and you walk out still without a Japanese phone number. Without that number, you cannot finish a bank account, a rental application, or half the apps you need to live here.

This happens to a lot of newly arrived foreigners, and it is rarely about you personally. Japan's three major carriers — docomo, SoftBank and au (KDDI) — run fairly strict checks, especially when a handset is bought on installments. The good news is that a refusal at one store is usually fixable, and there are several paths that do not depend on passing the big-carrier screening at all. This article focuses specifically on being turned down by the big three. If you want the broader picture of phone contract rejections, see our article on why phone contracts get rejected in Japan.

What actually happens

A denial at a docomo, SoftBank or au shop usually takes one of these forms:

  • Refused at the counter before any check. Staff look at your residence card or payment method and say they cannot accept it. This is typically a document or payment problem, not a credit problem.
  • The line contract is fine, but the phone installment is refused. You are told you can sign up for service only if you buy the handset outright, or bring your own phone. This is a credit screening result on the installment part.
  • "The screening did not pass" (shinsa ni tooranakatta). The application goes to the carrier's back office and comes back declined. The shop is usually not given — or not allowed to share — the specific reason.
  • Asked to come back later. For example, after your address is registered, after you have a bank account, or after your residence card is updated.

The most confusing part is that the staff often cannot explain. Shop employees in Japan are frequently franchise staff working from a fixed checklist, and when the system says no, they have little room to negotiate. Pushing harder at the same counter rarely changes the outcome. Changing what you bring, what you buy, or where you apply usually does.

Why it happens

Understanding the structure behind the refusal makes it much easier to pick the right fix.

1. Handset installments are a form of credit

When you buy an expensive smartphone over 24 or 48 months, you are effectively taking out a small loan. That installment is screened like credit, and the result can be recorded with a Japanese credit bureau. If you have no Japanese credit history, the system has nothing to judge you on. If you have had late payments on a previous phone or card in Japan, that can show up too. Many foreigners pass the line contract but fail the installment screening for exactly this reason.

2. Your residence period vs. the contract length

Carriers look at how long you are allowed to stay in Japan. If your period of stay ends before a 24-month or 48-month installment plan would finish, the carrier sees a risk that you will leave with an unpaid balance. Students, people on short work contracts, and anyone whose residence card expires within the next year or so are the most affected. The exact rules differ by carrier and change over time, so the shop may simply say the installment is "not available" for your status.

3. Payment method

The big carriers generally want monthly payments from a Japanese bank account (automatic debit) or a credit card that their system accepts. Some foreign-issued cards are not accepted for monthly billing, and debit or prepaid cards may be rejected depending on the carrier and plan. If you do not yet have a Japanese bank account, this alone can stop the application. This creates the classic loop: the bank wants a phone number, the carrier wants a bank account.

4. Address and document mismatch

Your address on the back of the residence card must match your current address. If you just moved and have not registered at the ward or city office, or the address is written differently from what you entered on the form, the application can be stopped. Some carriers also ask for a supplementary document showing your address, such as a utility bill or a certificate of residence.

5. Past unpaid phone bills

Japanese carriers share information about customers who left serious unpaid balances. If you, or someone using your identity, cancelled a phone contract in Japan without paying the final bills, a new contract with another major carrier can be refused. This is less common but worth thinking about if you lived in Japan before.

6. Language and verification concerns

Contracts involve important explanations that staff are required to give. If staff feel they cannot confirm that you understood the terms, some shops will hesitate or ask you to bring a Japanese speaker. This is not a legal rule against foreigners, but it does make some branches more cautious than others.

Common mistakes

  • Trying to buy the newest flagship phone on installments on day one. This is the single most common reason for a refusal. The installment screening is the hardest part to pass with no Japanese credit history.
  • Applying before your address is registered. A residence card with no address on the back, or an old address, is a frequent stopper.
  • Bringing a foreign credit card and assuming it will work. It may be accepted for the first payment at the shop but not registered for monthly billing.
  • Going to several shops of the same carrier on the same day. If the decline came from the carrier's central screening, another branch of the same carrier will usually get the same answer.
  • Ignoring the sub-brands and online plans. Many people think the only options are the big three shops, when the carriers' own low-cost brands often have simpler requirements.

Step-by-step fix

  1. Find out which part was refused. Ask the staff politely: "Kaisen no keiyaku wa dekimasu ka? Tanmatsu no bunkatsu dake ga dame desu ka?" (Can I make the line contract? Is it only the handset installment that is not possible?). This one question tells you whether you have a credit problem or a document/payment problem.
  2. If only the installment failed, remove it. Ask to sign up for a SIM-only contract and bring your own unlocked phone, or buy the handset in full. Say: "SIM dake de keiyaku shitai desu" (I would like a SIM-only contract). Most phones sold in other countries in recent years work in Japan, but check that your phone is unlocked and supports Japanese bands.
  3. Fix the documents. Make sure the address on the back of your residence card is current. If you recently moved, go to your ward or city office first. Bring your passport as well as your residence card, plus a certificate of residence (juminhyo) if you have one. If your card is close to expiry or being renewed, see our article on residence card renewal problems.
  4. Fix the payment method. If your card was the issue, ask which methods they accept: "Shiharai houhou wa nani ga tsukaemasu ka?" A Japanese bank account is the most reliable option. If you are stuck in the bank-phone loop, our article on bank account rejections explains which banks are more flexible.
  5. Try the carrier's sub-brand or online plan. Each big carrier runs a cheaper brand or online-only plan: docomo has ahamo, SoftBank has Y!mobile and LINEMO, and au has UQ mobile and povo. Rakuten Mobile is another network operator. Requirements and accepted payment methods differ, so read the sign-up page carefully, but these are often easier because you are not buying a phone on credit.
  6. Use an MVNO (budget carrier). Budget carriers rent network capacity from the big three and often sell SIM-only plans with simpler checks. Some accept credit cards only; a few are known for being foreigner-friendly with English support.
  7. Get connected right now with a travel or data eSIM. While you sort out the contract, a data eSIM gets you online immediately for maps, messaging and email. Note that a data-only eSIM usually does not give you a Japanese voice number, so you will still need a proper contract later for banks and rental applications. [PR] JAPAN&GLOBAL eSIM is one option for data access while you wait.
  8. Come back to the big three later if you need them. After a few months of paying a SIM-only plan, a bank account, and possibly a credit card on time, an installment application has a better chance. Building that history also helps later with apartment guarantor companies — see our article on getting a credit card with no credit history.
Important: Do not sign up for a phone using someone else's name or let someone else use a contract in your name. Lending or transferring phone contracts is taken very seriously in Japan, and it can cause major problems for your record and your residence status.

Required documents

Bring everything on this list, even if you think you will not need it. Being sent home for one missing paper is the most frustrating outcome.

  • Residence card (在留カード) with your current address written on the back
  • Passport
  • Certificate of residence (住民票), issued recently, if your card address was just updated or if the shop asks for proof of address
  • Japanese bank cash card (キャッシュカード) and bank book (通帳), or a credit card in your own name for monthly billing
  • My Number card (マイナンバーカード) if you have one — some shops accept it as identification
  • Your unlocked smartphone, if you plan a SIM-only contract
  • For a number transfer from another carrier: your MNP reservation number (MNP予約番号) or use the carrier's one-stop transfer process if available
  • For students: student ID (学生証); some plans for minors or students may ask for additional documents

City / region differences

The screening rules themselves are set by each carrier nationally, so in theory the result is the same anywhere in Japan. In practice, the experience varies a lot by location.

  • Tokyo: Large flagship shops in areas with many foreign residents and visitors are more used to residence cards and foreign applicants, and some have English-speaking staff or interpretation tablets. Waits can be long, so book online if the carrier offers reservations.
  • Osaka and Kobe: Big shops near major stations are usually experienced with foreign customers. Smaller neighborhood franchise shops may be less familiar. If you are setting up life in Osaka, handle your address registration first, then bank and phone.
  • Nagoya, Yokohama, Fukuoka, Kyoto: Central station-area shops generally handle foreign applications well. Suburban branches may be more cautious and more likely to ask you to bring a Japanese speaker.
  • Rural areas: Shops may rarely see foreign applicants, so staff may be unsure about visa types or documents. Applying online for a sub-brand or MVNO can be much easier than explaining your situation at a local counter.

Electronics stores (large chains near stations) also have carrier counters. They sell the same plans, but sometimes have more staff and shorter waits. The screening result itself will be the same as at the carrier's own shop.

Frequently Asked Questions

If docomo refused me, will SoftBank and au refuse me too?

Not necessarily. Each carrier runs its own screening. If the problem was a document or payment method, it will likely repeat everywhere until you fix it. If it was the handset installment, another carrier may also decline. Serious unpaid bills from a past contract can affect all major carriers. The fastest test is to ask whether a SIM-only contract is possible.

Can I get a contract if my visa expires in a few months?

A SIM-only contract is often still possible, because there is no long installment involved. Long handset installments are the part most likely to be refused when your period of stay is short. If you are about to renew, it may be easier to apply after you receive the updated residence card.

Will a refusal hurt my credit record in Japan?

A declined application is not the same as an unpaid debt. The main thing that damages your record is missing payments on installments, cards or loans. Paying your phone bill on time every month is one of the simplest ways for a newcomer to start building a positive history.

Do I need a Japanese phone number to rent an apartment?

Almost always, yes. Agents, landlords and guarantor companies need a Japanese number to contact you, and screening can stall without one. A data-only eSIM is useful for getting online, but it usually does not provide a voice number.

Is it rude to ask the staff for the reason?

No, it is fine to ask politely. Just understand that staff may not be allowed to share the details of the screening result. Asking a narrower question — whether the line contract or only the installment failed, or which payment methods are accepted — usually gets you a more useful answer.
