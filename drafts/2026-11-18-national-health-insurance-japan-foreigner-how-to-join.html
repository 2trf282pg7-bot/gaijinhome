Turned Away at the Ward Office? What to Do When Joining National Health Insurance in Japan as a Foreigner

You went to city hall to sign up for health insurance, waited for your number to be called, and then the clerk told you that you can't join yet, that you're at the wrong window, or that you owe months of back premiums you never knew existed. Now you're walking around without coverage, worried about what happens if you get sick, and unsure what you did wrong.

This is one of the most common paperwork problems for foreign residents in Japan. National Health Insurance, called Kokumin Kenko Hoken (国民健康保険), is run by your local municipality, and every ward or city applies the same national rules with its own forms, premium rates, and habits. The good news: almost every refusal has a clear reason, and almost every reason has a fix. This article walks you through what is actually happening and how to get enrolled.

What actually happens

Most foreign residents who run into trouble with National Health Insurance hit one of these situations:

  • "You need to register your address first." The clerk won't start enrollment because your resident registration (moving-in notice) hasn't been processed.
  • "You should be on your company's insurance." You told them you have a job, and they refused because your employer is supposed to enroll you in employee health insurance instead.
  • "We need proof that your old insurance ended." You quit or changed jobs, and they won't enroll you without a document from your former employer.
  • "Your premiums start from the month you arrived." You joined late, and your first bill covers the months you weren't enrolled, even though the insurance didn't pay anything during that time.
  • "Your status of residence isn't eligible." You're in Japan on a short-term visa or a specific category that's excluded from the system.
  • You never received a plastic insurance card. You were enrolled, but you got a different document or were told to use your My Number Card, and you're not sure if you're actually covered.

None of these mean you've been permanently refused. They mean a prerequisite is missing, or you've been pointed at the wrong system.

Why it happens

National Health Insurance is tied to your resident registration

NHI is administered by the municipality where you are registered as a resident. If your moving-in notice (転入届, tennyu todoke) hasn't been filed, the system literally has no record of you as a local resident, so the insurance section can't create an account. In most offices, the resident registration window and the insurance window are in the same building, often on the same floor, and you can do both on the same day in the right order.

Japan has two separate health insurance systems

People who work full-time (or close to it) at companies that are required to provide social insurance are supposed to be covered by employee health insurance (健康保険, kenko hoken, often called shakai hoken). NHI is for everyone else who lives here: students, freelancers, part-timers below the threshold, the unemployed, and people whose employers aren't covered. You can't be in both at once. When you mention a job, the clerk has to check which system you belong in, and they will often send you back to your employer rather than enroll you.

Coverage is based on eligibility, not on when you sign up

Once you're registered as a resident and not covered by employee insurance, you are legally supposed to be in NHI from that date, and you're expected to notify the office within 14 days. If you sign up three months later, premiums are calculated from the month you first became eligible. The insurance generally does not reimburse medical bills from the period before you enrolled. This is why late joiners feel "penalized" twice.

Some statuses of residence are excluded

People on Temporary Visitor status, people staying three months or less, and a few specific Designated Activities categories (such as those for medical treatment or long-stay sightseeing) generally can't join. If your residence card shows a period of stay of three months or less, expect a refusal unless the office confirms you're expected to stay longer.

Paper insurance cards were phased out

Japan stopped issuing new paper health insurance cards in December 2024. The My Number Card can now be registered as your insurance card. If you don't have a My Number Card linked for insurance use, the municipality issues a document called a certificate of eligibility (資格確認書, shikaku kakunin-sho) that you show at clinics instead. Many newcomers expect a plastic card and think their enrollment failed when it didn't.

Language and forms

Forms are usually in Japanese, and the questions about income, household members, and previous insurance can be confusing. A wrong box ticked (for example, saying you're employed when you're a contract worker without social insurance) can send you to the wrong process.

Common mistakes

  1. Going to the insurance window before registering your address. Always do the moving-in notice first, then health insurance, then pension, in that order.
  2. Waiting until you're sick to sign up. You'll pay back premiums anyway, and you'll pay 100% of any medical bills from before your enrollment date.
  3. Joining NHI when your employer should be covering you. If you later get enrolled in employee insurance for the same period, you'll need to cancel NHI and sort out refunds, which takes extra trips.
  4. Not filing an income declaration. NHI premiums are based on the previous year's income. If the office has no income data for you, you may not receive the low-income reductions you'd otherwise qualify for. New arrivals and students with little or no Japanese income should ask about filing a simple income declaration (住民税申告, juminzei shinkoku).
  5. Leaving Japan without notifying the office. If you move abroad without filing a moving-out notice, premiums can keep accruing, and unpaid bills can cause problems if you return later.

Step-by-step fix

  1. Confirm which system you belong in. If you have a job, ask your employer directly: "Am I enrolled in shakai hoken?" (社会保険に加入していますか?). If yes, you don't need NHI. If your employer says no and you work full-time hours at a larger company, that's worth questioning, but in the meantime you still need coverage, so NHI is the safe option.
  2. Register your address first. At your ward or city office, file the moving-in notice with your residence card and passport. Once it's processed, ask: "Kokumin kenko hoken ni kanyu shitai desu" (国民健康保険に加入したいです, "I'd like to join National Health Insurance"). The section is often called 保険年金課 (Hoken Nenkin-ka) or 国民健康保険課.
  3. If you left a job, get the coverage-loss document. Ask your former employer for the certificate of loss of insurance eligibility (資格喪失証明書, shikaku soshitsu shomeisho). If they're slow, tell the ward office; some offices can accept other proof or start the process and let you bring the document later.
  4. Ask about back premiums before you panic. If you were eligible earlier, say: "Itsukara no hokenryo ni narimasu ka?" (いつからの保険料になりますか?, "From which month will premiums start?"). Ask whether you can pay in installments. Most municipalities have a consultation process for people who can't pay a lump sum.
  5. Request income-based reductions. If you had little or no income in Japan last year, ask: "Keigen no taisho ni narimasu ka?" (軽減の対象になりますか?, "Do I qualify for a reduction?"). You may need to file a short income declaration, which can often be done at a nearby window the same day.
  6. Check how you'll prove coverage at clinics. Ask whether you'll get a certificate of eligibility in the mail, or whether to register your My Number Card for health insurance use. Until something arrives, ask if they can give you a temporary proof of enrollment.
  7. Set up automatic payment. Premiums are usually paid in installments across the fiscal year. Paying by bank transfer from your account prevents missed bills, which matters because unpaid premiums can come up later, for example in a permanent residency application.
  8. Bring help for the language if you need it. Many offices have multilingual support, but it's not guaranteed. If you'll be handling ward office visits, insurance letters, and renewals for years, basic Japanese for paperwork pays off quickly. Required documents

  • Residence card (在留カード, zairyu card) showing a period of stay longer than three months
  • Passport
  • My Number notification or My Number Card (マイナンバーカード) if you have it
  • Proof that previous employee insurance ended (資格喪失証明書, shikaku soshitsu shomeisho), if you left a job
  • Student ID or certificate of enrollment (在学証明書, zaigaku shomeisho), if you're a student
  • Bank cash card or passbook and registered seal (印鑑, inkan) or signature, if you want to set up automatic payment
  • Information on last year's income (or a statement that you had none in Japan), for the income declaration

Requirements vary slightly by municipality, so it's worth checking your ward's website or calling ahead. Bringing more than you need is better than making a second trip.

City / region differences

The national rules are the same everywhere, but the experience varies:

  • Tokyo: Each of the 23 wards runs its own NHI and sets its own premium rates, so moving from one ward to another means a new enrollment and possibly a different bill. Large wards with many foreign residents tend to have more multilingual materials, but busy periods (March to April) bring long waits.
  • Osaka: Osaka City handles NHI at ward offices across the city. Like Tokyo, spring is crowded. If you're new to Osaka, our Osaka page covers the rest of the move.
  • Yokohama, Nagoya, Kyoto, Kobe, Fukuoka: These cities administer NHI citywide, with enrollment at your local ward office. Premium rates differ between cities, so your bill can change noticeably if you relocate.
  • Smaller towns: Fewer English materials, but staff may have more time to walk you through the forms. Bring a translation app and your documents ready.

Whatever the city, premium amounts are not something you can compare from a single national figure. For people with no prior Japanese income, premiums are often modest, roughly a few thousand yen per month after reductions, but your municipality's calculation is the only reliable number. Frequently Asked Questions

Do I have to join National Health Insurance if I already have travel insurance?

If you're a registered resident staying more than three months and you're not in employee health insurance, enrollment in NHI is mandatory regardless of private or travel insurance. Private insurance can supplement NHI but doesn't replace the obligation.

Why is my first bill so high?

Usually because premiums were calculated back to the month you first became eligible, often your arrival or the month you left a job. Ask the office for a breakdown by month and whether you can pay in installments or qualify for a reduction.

I'm a student. Do I need to join?

Yes, if your stay is longer than three months. Students with little or no income in Japan often qualify for reduced premiums, especially after filing an income declaration. Many schools also require proof of NHI enrollment.

I didn't get an insurance card. Am I covered?

Possibly. New paper cards are no longer issued. You'll either use your My Number Card registered for health insurance or a certificate of eligibility (資格確認書) mailed by your municipality. If neither has arrived, contact the insurance section and ask for proof of enrollment.

What happens to my NHI if I leave Japan?

File a moving-out notice at your ward office before you leave. Your NHI coverage ends with it, and the office will settle any remaining premiums. If you skip this, premiums can keep being billed to your registered address.
