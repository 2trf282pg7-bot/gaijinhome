Apartment Rejected in Tokyo Because You Have No Guarantor? Here's What Foreigners Do

You found an apartment in Tokyo, and the agent asked who your guarantor (保証人, hoshōnin) would be. You do not have one. You have no relatives in Japan, your boss is not going to sign, and your friends are foreigners too. Or you used a guarantor company and still got a negative result. Either way, the word "guarantor" has now become the thing standing between you and a home. This is one of the most common problems foreigners face in Tokyo, and almost every foreign resident who lives here has solved it one way or another. Here is how.

What Actually Happens

"No guarantor" problems in Tokyo show up in three different ways, and it helps to identify which one you are dealing with:

  • The listing requires an individual guarantor. Some landlords still want a person, usually a Japanese relative or employer, to sign as joint guarantor (連帯保証人, rentai hoshōnin), sometimes in addition to a guarantor company.
  • The guarantor company rejected you. Most Tokyo apartments now require a guarantor company, and their screening is where many foreign applicants fail.
  • The guarantor company needs an emergency contact you do not have. Even when the company does not require a personal guarantor, it may require a Japanese-speaking emergency contact (緊急連絡先), and without one the application stalls.

Each one has a different fix, so do not treat them as the same problem.

Why the Guarantor System Blocks Foreigners

The traditional system assumes family in Japan

Japanese renting was built around family guarantors: a parent or relative who promises to pay if the tenant does not. Foreigners usually have no relatives in Japan, and a guarantor abroad is very difficult for a landlord to pursue.

Joint guarantors carry heavy liability

A joint guarantor is liable for the tenant's debts, which is why colleagues and friends often refuse. Japan's Civil Code revision that took effect in April 2020 requires a maximum liability amount (極度額) to be stated in individual guarantee contracts for leases, which makes the risk clearer but has not made people more eager to sign.

Guarantor companies replaced people, but they screen

Guarantor companies typically charge an initial fee, often around half to one month's rent, plus an annual or monthly renewal fee. In return they guarantee the rent. Each company has its own screening criteria and sometimes shares payment history information with others. Factors that commonly cause problems for foreigners include a short remaining period of stay, no Japanese phone number or bank account, recently started employment, and no Japanese-speaking emergency contact.

Tokyo competition sharpens the problem

In Tokyo, good apartments often get multiple applications quickly. If a landlord can choose between an applicant who passes easily and one who needs special arrangements, they pick the easier one. Your application has to be simple to approve.

Key tip: Ask the agent which guarantor company the property uses before applying, and whether it has a foreigner-friendly option. Some guarantor companies specialize in foreign residents and offer multilingual support, and some landlords let you choose between several companies.

Common Mistakes

  1. Asking a coworker to be a joint guarantor. It puts them in an awkward spot and rarely works. Ask your employer about a corporate lease or a company guarantee instead.
  2. Applying for properties that require an individual guarantor. If a listing states 連帯保証人要 (joint guarantor required) and you have none, skip it. Filter for 保証会社利用可 (guarantor company OK) or 保証人不要 (no guarantor needed).
  3. Applying repeatedly through the same guarantor company. If one company rejected you, applying to other properties that use the same company may give the same result. Ask which company each property uses.
  4. Leaving the emergency contact field blank. An empty field can cause a rejection on its own. Find someone suitable before applying.
  5. Not considering guarantor-free housing types. Tokyo has more share houses, serviced apartments, and UR estates than anywhere else in Japan. These options exist precisely to solve this problem.

Step-by-Step Fix: What Foreigners in Tokyo Actually Do

  1. Figure out which problem you have. Ask the agent: "Rentai hoshōnin ga hitsuyō desu ka? Soretomo hoshō-gaisha dake de daijōbu desu ka?" (連帯保証人が必要ですか？それとも保証会社だけで大丈夫ですか？ "Is a joint guarantor required, or is a guarantor company alone OK?"). If you were rejected, ask whether it was the guarantor company's screening.
  2. Ask your employer first. Many Tokyo companies that hire foreign staff offer a corporate lease (法人契約), company housing (社宅), or a housing support program. A corporate lease means the company is the tenant, which usually removes the guarantor problem entirely. Ask HR directly; it is a routine question.
  3. Use a foreigner-friendly guarantor company. Ask the agent to put your application through a guarantor company that works with foreign residents. These often provide multilingual support and can accept their own support line or alternative arrangements in place of a Japanese emergency contact, depending on the company and landlord.
  4. Find a willing emergency contact. Explain that an emergency contact is not a financial guarantor; they are only contacted if you cannot be reached. Coworkers, supervisors, Japanese friends, and school staff are much more willing to agree to this.
  5. Strengthen your screening file. Submit a residence card with as long a period of stay as possible, your certificate of employment, payslips, and a bank balance certificate. If you have been in Japan a while, a tax certificate helps. See our credit check guide for what screeners look at.
  6. Apply for UR housing. UR public rental housing requires no guarantor, no key money, and no brokerage fee, and accepts foreign residents who meet income or savings criteria. Tokyo and its suburbs have many UR estates. Our UR guide walks through the application.
  7. Use a no-guarantor home while you sort things out. Share houses and furnished apartments generally do not require a guarantor. [PR] Oakhouse share houses are a well-known no-guarantor option across Tokyo. Our Tokyo share house guide and monthly mansion guide compare these routes.
  8. Reapply after building history. After six months to a year in Japan with a phone contract, bank account, steady job, and on-time payments, many guarantor companies that rejected you before will approve you. For the full set of options, see our main no-guarantor renting guide.

Required Documents

  • Residence card (在留カード), front and back, with the period of stay clearly visible
  • Passport
  • Certificate of employment (在職証明書) or job offer letter (内定通知書) if newly hired
  • Recent payslips (給与明細), typically the last two or three months
  • Tax certificate (課税証明書) or withholding tax slip (源泉徴収票) if you have worked in Japan for a year or more
  • Bank balance certificate (残高証明書) or bank statements
  • Certificate of residence (住民票)
  • Emergency contact details (緊急連絡先): name, address, phone, relationship
  • Japanese mobile number and bank account (often needed for guarantor company payments)

Tokyo Areas and How the Guarantor Problem Plays Out

Central wards (Minato, Shibuya, Meguro, parts of Shinjuku): Most international agencies and foreigner-friendly guarantor options are here, and many corporate leases are signed in these areas. Rents are high, so passing income screening is harder for a standard salary.

East and north (Sumida, Koto, Taito, Kita, Itabashi): More reasonable rents and many older buildings managed by local companies. Landlords may be more flexible on price but less experienced with foreign applicants, so a foreigner-friendly guarantor company and a clear file matter more.

West along the Chuo and Keio lines (Nakano, Suginami, Mitaka, Chofu): Popular with students and young workers, with many share houses and small apartments. UR estates are also scattered through western Tokyo and the Tama area.

Kawasaki, Yokohama, Saitama, and Chiba commuter towns: Lower rents, more UR housing, and often easier approval because your income looks stronger relative to rent. Many foreigners start here and move closer to the center later. If you are open to other cities altogether, our Osaka guide shows how the same problem plays out in Kansai.

Frequently Asked Questions

Can I rent in Tokyo with no guarantor at all?

Yes. Most Tokyo apartments use a guarantor company instead of a personal guarantor, and UR housing, share houses, and many furnished apartments require no guarantor at all. The challenge is usually passing guarantor company screening, not finding a person to sign.

How much does a guarantor company cost?

It varies by company and property, but a common structure is an initial fee of roughly half to one month's rent, plus an annual renewal fee or a small monthly fee. Ask the agent for the exact amount before you apply, and include it in your move-in budget. Our hidden costs guide covers the other fees.

My guarantor company application was rejected. Can I ask why?

Guarantor companies usually do not disclose specific reasons. You can ask the agent whether the problem was income, period of stay, or contact information, and they can sometimes give a hint. Then fix that issue or apply through a different guarantor company.

Can a foreign friend in Japan be my emergency contact?

Sometimes. Many guarantor companies and landlords prefer a Japanese-speaking contact, but some accept non-Japanese contacts who live in Japan and can communicate in Japanese, and some offer a multilingual support service in place of a personal contact. Ask the agent what the specific guarantor company accepts.

Is it worth asking my company for a corporate lease?

Absolutely. A corporate lease is often the fastest way past the guarantor problem, since the company becomes the tenant. Even if your company does not normally offer it, HR may agree, especially if you explain that you have been rejected for lack of a guarantor.
