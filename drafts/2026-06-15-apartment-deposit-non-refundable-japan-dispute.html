Apartment Deposit Denied as Refundable in Japan? Your Dispute Rights and How to Recover It

You moved out, handed back the keys, and waited for your security deposit. Then a statement arrived: new wallpaper, full flooring replacement, professional cleaning, tatami renewal, key exchange. The total wiped out your deposit, or worse, asked you to pay more. When you questioned it, the management company said the deposit was "non-refundable" or that the deductions were "standard in Japan."

Some of those charges may be legitimate. Many are not. Japanese law and the government's own restoration guidelines are more tenant-friendly than most foreigners expect, and a calm, documented dispute recovers money surprisingly often. This guide explains what is actually happening, what you can push back on, and the exact steps to take.

What Actually Happens When a Deposit Is "Denied"

In Japan, the refundable security deposit is called shikikin (敷金). It is usually 0–2 months' rent and is held against unpaid rent and damage. It is completely different from key money (礼金, reikin), which is a non-refundable gift to the landlord paid at signing. If you are not sure which one you paid, check your contract and our explainer on what key money is.

After move-out, the management company inspects the unit (退去立会い, move-out inspection), then sends a settlement statement (敷金精算書) listing deductions. The typical complaints we hear from foreign tenants:

  • The entire deposit was deducted for "restoration" without an itemized breakdown.
  • Wallpaper for a whole room was charged because of one small mark.
  • Normal fading, sun damage, or furniture dents were billed as tenant damage.
  • The tenant was pressured to sign the inspection sheet on the spot, in Japanese, without understanding it.
  • A "cleaning fee" or "deposit deduction" appeared that the tenant did not remember agreeing to.

Being told "it is non-refundable" is often a simplification. The real question is: what does your contract say, and do the deductions match what the law allows?

Why It Happens

The legal baseline: normal wear and tear is the landlord's cost

Since the 2020 revision of Japan's Civil Code (民法), the rule is written into law: the landlord must return the deposit minus unpaid amounts, and the tenant's restoration duty does not include normal wear and tear (通常損耗) or ageing over time (経年変化). Things like sun-faded wallpaper, slight floor indentations from furniture, and marks where posters hung are generally treated as normal use.

The Ministry of Land, Infrastructure, Transport and Tourism (MLIT) publishes the restoration guidelines (原状回復をめぐるトラブルとガイドライン). They are not a law, but courts, consumer centers, and many management companies refer to them. Key ideas: tenants pay only for damage beyond normal use, the charge should be limited to the damaged area where practical, and the value of items like wallpaper declines over time, so a tenant who stayed several years should not pay the full price of brand-new wallpaper.

Special clauses (特約) change the picture

Contracts often contain special clauses: a fixed cleaning fee on move-out (クリーニング特約), a fixed deduction from the deposit (敷引き, shikibiki, common in the Kansai region), or key replacement at the tenant's cost. These clauses can be valid if they are clearly written, the amount is specific and reasonable, and you agreed to them. Vague clauses like "the tenant bears all restoration costs" are much weaker.

Why foreigners get hit harder

  • Language: the contract and explanation of important matters (重要事項説明) were in Japanese, so the tenant never knew a cleaning clause existed.
  • Timing: many foreigners leave Japan right after move-out. Management companies know a tenant overseas is unlikely to chase a few hundred dollars.
  • Signing under pressure: a tenant signs the inspection sheet without reading it, which is later used as "agreement" to the charges.
  • Cultural assumptions: the "foreigners damage apartments" stereotype sometimes leads to aggressive billing.

Common Mistakes That Cost Tenants Their Deposit

  1. No move-in photos. Without dated photos of existing scratches and stains, it is your word against theirs.
  2. Signing the move-out sheet without reading it. You can write "内容は後日確認します" (I will confirm the details later) or simply decline to sign the cost portion.
  3. Paying the extra bill immediately. Once paid, recovery is much harder. Ask for the breakdown first.
  4. Arguing by phone only. Verbal complaints leave no record. Use email or letters.
  5. Leaving Japan without a forwarding plan. Give the company an overseas email address and, if possible, a Japanese bank account that stays open until the deposit is settled.

Step-by-Step Fix: How to Dispute a Deposit Deduction

  1. Get the full itemized statement. Ask in writing for a breakdown showing each item, the area (in square meters), unit price, and the reason it is charged to you. A useful sentence: "敷金精算の内訳（項目・面積・単価・負担理由）を書面でいただけますか。" (Could you send the deposit settlement breakdown, including items, area, unit price and reason for tenant responsibility, in writing?)
  2. Pull out your contract and find every special clause. Look for 特約, クリーニング, 敷引, 鍵交換. Use a translation app on photos of each page. Note exactly which clauses you signed and what amounts they specify.
  3. Sort every charge into three groups. (a) Clearly in your contract with a fixed amount, (b) actual damage you caused (holes, burns, pet scratches, smoking stains), and (c) normal wear and tear or ageing. Group (c) is what you dispute. Group (b) you may be able to reduce by asking for depreciation and partial-area repair.
  4. Reply in writing, referencing the guidelines. Keep it short and factual: list the items you dispute, say they are normal wear and tear under the Civil Code and the MLIT restoration guidelines, and ask for a revised statement by a specific date (two weeks is reasonable). Attach your move-in and move-out photos.
  5. Escalate to the landlord if you dealt only with an agent. The management company and the owner are often different parties. Ask for the owner's name or ask the agent to pass your letter on.
  6. Call the consumer hotline: 188. Dialing 188 connects you to your local consumer affairs center (消費生活センター). Many centers can mediate rental deposit disputes, and some prefectures and big cities offer interpretation support. Ask whether they can contact the company on your behalf.
  7. Send a formal demand if ignored. A letter sent by content-certified mail (内容証明郵便) shows you are serious and creates proof of the date. Many people find the company responds after receiving one.
  8. Use small claims court as the final step. Japan's small claims procedure (少額訴訟) handles money claims up to ¥600,000, usually concluding in a single hearing. Filing is at the summary court (簡易裁判所) with jurisdiction. If you have left Japan, ask the consumer center or a legal aid service such as Houterasu (法テラス) about options before you go.
Key tip: Most disputes are won or lost on evidence. If you are still living in the apartment, take timestamped photos and video of every wall, floor, fixture and existing mark today, and email them to yourself and the management company. That single email can save you months' rent later.

Required Documents for a Deposit Dispute

  • Lease contract (賃貸借契約書), including all special clause pages (特約事項)
  • Explanation of important matters (重要事項説明書) you received at signing
  • Move-in condition checklist (入居時チェックリスト), if one was provided
  • Dated move-in and move-out photos and videos
  • Deposit settlement statement (敷金精算書) and any repair estimates (見積書)
  • Receipt or bank record showing the deposit paid (敷金の領収書 / 振込記録)
  • Copy of the move-out inspection sheet (退去立会い確認書), signed or unsigned
  • All emails and letters with the management company
  • Your residence card (在留カード) copy, for identification with the consumer center or court

City and Region Differences

Tokyo and the Kanto region

Tokyo actually has its own ordinance aimed at preventing restoration disputes, which requires agents to explain restoration responsibilities and special clauses to tenants before signing. In practice this means Tokyo contracts usually spell out cleaning fees clearly, so the dispute is less often "I never agreed" and more often "this charge is normal wear and tear." Typical deposits are 0–2 months.

Osaka, Kyoto, Kobe and the Kansai region

Kansai traditionally used a different system: a larger deposit called hoshokin (保証金) combined with a fixed non-refundable deduction (shikibiki, 敷引き). For example, a contract might say ¥300,000 deposit with ¥200,000 deducted on move-out regardless of condition. Courts have generally upheld clearly agreed shikibiki unless the amount is unreasonably high, so this is where "non-refundable" is most often actually true. Many newer Kansai listings now follow Tokyo-style terms. Check your contract carefully. More on renting in the region in our Osaka guide.

Other cities

Nagoya, Fukuoka, Sapporo and other cities generally follow Tokyo-style shikikin, but local customs vary by management company. Some regional landlords still use hoshokin. Wherever you are, the Civil Code rule on normal wear and tear applies nationwide.

How to Protect Your Deposit Next Time

Before signing your next lease, ask the agent to show you every special clause in English, and ask: "退去時に必ずかかる費用はいくらですか？" (What costs are always charged at move-out?). Get the answer in writing. Frequently Asked Questions

Is a security deposit in Japan ever truly non-refundable?

The standard deposit (shikikin) is refundable by law, minus unpaid rent and damage beyond normal use. However, key money (reikin) is non-refundable, and a clearly agreed fixed deduction (shikibiki) or fixed cleaning fee in your contract can legally be kept. Read your special clauses to see which applies to you.

Do I have to pay for wallpaper replacement?

Not for normal fading, light marks, or nail holes from hanging calendars, which are generally treated as normal wear. You may be charged for clear damage like large holes, crayon, or smoking stains, but the MLIT guidelines suggest limiting the charge to the damaged area where practical and reducing it based on how long the wallpaper had already been in use.

How long does the landlord have to return my deposit?

There is no single fixed national deadline written for all leases, but many contracts specify a period, often around one to two months after move-out. Check your contract. If nothing arrives within the period stated, send a written request and then contact the consumer hotline at 188.

I already left Japan. Can I still dispute the deduction?

Yes, though it is harder. Dispute by email, attach your photos, and reference the restoration guidelines. Some consumer centers handle inquiries by phone from overseas, and a friend in Japan with a written authorization can sometimes act on your behalf. Small claims court generally requires appearing in person, so plan before leaving if the amount is large.

The agent says foreigners always pay extra cleaning. Is that legal?

Charging someone more simply because they are a foreign national is not a valid basis for a deduction. Only what is in your contract and actual damage can be charged. Ask for the specific clause and itemized breakdown, and if you were treated differently from Japanese tenants, mention it to the consumer center.
