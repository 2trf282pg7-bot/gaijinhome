Eviction Notice Received in Japan? What to Do About Your Rights as a Foreigner

A letter arrives in Japanese, full of formal phrases you cannot quite parse, and the words 退去 (move out) or 解約 (cancellation) jump out. Or the management company calls and tells you that you have to leave by the end of next month. If you are a foreign resident, the fear is immediate: do I have any rights here, and could this affect my visa?

Take a breath. Japanese tenancy law is generally considered protective of residential tenants, and it applies to you in the same way it applies to Japanese citizens. Nationality is not a legal ground for eviction. Many "eviction notices" that foreigners receive are actually requests, not orders, and you have more time and options than the letter suggests. This guide walks you through how to tell what kind of notice you have and how to respond.

What Actually Happens: The Types of "Eviction Notice" in Japan

Before you do anything, work out which situation you are in. The response is completely different for each.

Check your contract (賃貸借契約書) to see whether it is an ordinary lease (普通借家契約) or a fixed-term lease (定期借家契約). This one detail shapes almost everything else.

Why It Happens, and What the Law Generally Says

Ordinary leases are strongly protected

Under Japan's Act on Land and Building Leases (借地借家法), a landlord who wants to end an ordinary lease or refuse renewal generally must give notice well in advance (the law sets a window of six months to one year before the lease ends for non-renewal) and must have a "justifiable reason" (正当事由). Wanting a higher-paying tenant or simply disliking a tenant is not usually enough. In practice, landlords who want a tenant out without a strong reason often offer moving compensation to make the request acceptable.

Fixed-term leases work differently

With a properly made fixed-term lease, the contract ends at the agreed date without a right to renew. However, for terms of one year or more, the landlord is generally required to notify you of the end of the term in advance (typically between one year and six months before it ends). Fixed-term leases also require a separate written explanation at signing. If these steps were not followed, the situation may be less clear-cut than the landlord claims, and it is worth asking a lawyer.

Rent arrears do not mean instant eviction

Japanese courts generally require a breakdown of the relationship of trust (信頼関係の破壊) before a lease can be cancelled for non-payment. A single late month rarely meets that bar; repeated or prolonged non-payment can. But even then, the landlord cannot simply throw you out.

Self-help eviction is not allowed

Changing the locks, removing your belongings, or cutting off utilities to force you out are generally not lawful in Japan. Removal of a tenant who refuses to leave requires a court judgment and formal enforcement (強制執行). Courts have also ruled against guarantor company contract terms that let the company treat a home as abandoned and clear it out on its own. If anyone tries these tactics, document everything and contact police and a lawyer.

Why foreigners get these notices more often

Common triggers include misunderstandings over noise or garbage rules, a friend or partner staying who is not on the contract, subletting or short-term rental listings, or late rent because a foreign bank transfer did not arrive on time. Language gaps mean small problems escalate to formal letters before anyone talks it through.

Common Mistakes Foreign Tenants Make

  1. Agreeing on the phone or signing a form immediately. A signed move-out agreement (合意解約書) can waive protections you would otherwise have. Do not sign anything you cannot fully read.
  2. Ignoring the letter. Silence makes a dispute worse, especially with rent demands or court documents, which have strict deadlines.
  3. Stopping rent payments in protest. Withholding rent hands the landlord a real breach to point to. Keep paying while you dispute.
  4. Moving out without negotiating. If the landlord initiated the request without your fault, compensation and help with moving costs are often on the table.
  5. Assuming it affects your visa. A tenancy dispute in itself is a private civil matter. What matters for immigration is that you keep your registered address up to date when you move.

Step-by-Step: What to Do When You Receive an Eviction Notice

  1. Photograph the letter and the envelope. Note the date received and how it was delivered. Content-certified mail (内容証明郵便) signals the landlord is building a formal record.
  2. Get an accurate translation. Ask a Japanese-speaking friend, your employer's HR, or a multilingual consultation service to tell you exactly what is being requested, by what date, and on what grounds.
  3. Pull out your contract. Confirm the lease type, end date, renewal clause, and cancellation clauses. Identify who your guarantor company is, since they may also contact you.
  4. Keep paying rent on time. If the landlord refuses to accept rent, ask a lawyer about depositing it with the legal affairs bureau (供託, kyōtaku), a recognized way to show you are not in arrears.
  5. Reply in writing, calmly. A short message such as "I have received your letter. I would like to understand the reason and discuss this. I wish to continue living here." (書面を拝受しました。理由をご説明いただき、話し合いをお願いします。引き続き居住を希望します。) keeps the record clear without escalating.
  6. Get free or low-cost legal advice. Options include the Japan Legal Support Center (法テラス, Hō Terasu), which offers multilingual information and, for those who qualify, free consultations; local bar association consultations; your city's consumer affairs center (消費生活センター); and the Foreign Residents Support Center in Tokyo. Bring your contract and the letter.
  7. If you are willing to move, negotiate terms. Ask for a reasonable move-out date, return of your deposit, waiver of restoration charges beyond normal wear (see deposit dispute guide), and moving compensation. Get any agreement in writing before you sign.
  8. Line up a fallback home early. Even if you plan to fight, having a backup reduces pressure. Share houses and furnished rentals can be arranged quickly without a guarantor. [PR] Oakhouse share houses are one no-guarantor option while you sort things out.
  9. Update your address after you move. File your new address at the city or ward office within 14 days of moving in so your residence card stays accurate.
Key tip: Never sign a move-out agreement or hand back your keys until you have a written statement of the move-out date, the deposit refund, and any compensation. Once you have left, your bargaining power largely disappears.

Documents to Gather Before Seeking Advice

  • The eviction letter or notice, plus envelope and any delivery receipt
  • Lease contract (賃貸借契約書) and any renewal contracts (更新契約書)
  • Important matters explanation (重要事項説明書) from when you signed
  • Fixed-term lease explanation document (定期借家の説明書面), if applicable
  • Guarantor company contract (保証委託契約書)
  • Rent payment records: bank transfer history or receipts
  • Residence card (在留カード) and passport
  • Any messages, emails or LINE chats with the landlord or management company
  • Photos of the apartment's condition, ideally with dates
  • Deposit and key money receipts from move-in

City and Region Differences

Tokyo

Tokyo has the most redevelopment, so demolition-related move-out requests are relatively common in older wooden buildings and aging apartment blocks. It also has the widest range of multilingual legal help, including the Foreign Residents Support Center near Yotsuya, where several support organizations are located in one place.

Osaka and Kansai

Osaka has many long-established private landlords and older buildings, and disputes often center on renewal and deposit terms. Kansai's traditional deposit customs can complicate move-out settlements, so check your contract carefully. See our Osaka renting guide for local context.

Other regions

In smaller cities, English-language legal help is scarcer, but prefectural international associations (国際交流協会) often run consultation desks and can point you to interpreters or lawyers. Employer-provided housing is also common; if your housing is tied to your job, check whether losing the job affects your right to stay and how much notice your contract gives.

Frequently Asked Questions

Can my landlord evict me because I am a foreigner?

No. Nationality is not a lawful reason to end a lease, and the protections in Japan's Act on Land and Building Leases apply regardless of nationality. If a landlord is pushing you out with no clear reason, get advice before agreeing to anything.

How much notice does a landlord have to give?

For an ordinary lease, notice of non-renewal is generally required six months to one year before the lease ends, and the landlord also needs a justifiable reason. For fixed-term leases of one year or more, the landlord is generally required to notify you of the term ending in advance, typically between one year and six months before. Your contract and a lawyer can confirm your specific situation.

Will an eviction affect my visa renewal?

A rental dispute is a private civil matter and does not in itself affect your residence status. What you must do is register your new address within 14 days of moving. Unpaid debts or court judgments can create other problems, so resolve them properly. See our visa guide for residence-related procedures.

The guarantor company is calling me constantly. What can I do?

If you are behind on rent, the guarantor company may pay the landlord and then seek repayment from you. Talk to them and agree on a repayment plan in writing. However, harassment, late-night visits, or entering your home are not acceptable, so keep a record and seek advice if this happens.

Am I entitled to moving compensation?

There is no fixed entitlement, but when a landlord ends a tenancy for their own reasons, such as redevelopment, moving compensation (立退料) is often paid to strengthen their justifiable reason or to reach an agreement. Amounts vary widely, so negotiate and get any offer in writing.
