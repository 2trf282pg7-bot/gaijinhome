Noise Complaint Against You in Japan? What to Do Before It Threatens Your Lease

You come home and find a printed notice taped to your door, or slipped into your mailbox, or posted in the building lobby: "Residents are asked to refrain from making loud noises at night." Nobody knocked. Nobody spoke to you. But you have a sinking feeling the notice is about you. Maybe the management company even called you directly, in fast Japanese you only half understood.

Noise complaints are one of the most common, and most stressful, problems foreign renters face in Japan. They are rarely about one loud party. They are usually about everyday sounds that feel completely normal to you: walking in shoes indoors, a washing machine at 11 pm, a video call with family in another time zone, a chair scraping the floor. This article explains what is actually going on, why it matters more than you might think, and exactly how to handle it so it does not follow you into your lease renewal.

What actually happens

In most Japanese apartment buildings, neighbors almost never complain to you directly. Confrontation is avoided. Instead, the process usually looks like this:

  1. A neighbor calls the management company (管理会社, kanri gaisha) or the landlord, not you.
  2. A general notice goes out first. The management company posts a building-wide notice or puts a flyer in every mailbox. This lets everyone save face, because nobody is named.
  3. If the noise continues, the notice becomes specific. You may receive a letter addressed to your unit, or a phone call from the management company asking you to be careful.
  4. Repeated complaints get documented. The management company keeps a record. This record can come up later when your contract is up for renewal, or when the landlord decides who they want to keep in the building.
  5. In serious, long-running cases, the landlord may refuse to renew a fixed-term contract, or in rare situations try to end the lease. This is not the normal outcome, but the possibility is why you should take the first notice seriously.

The key point: the first general notice is a polite warning, and it is the easiest stage to fix. Most cases end there if you respond well.

Why it happens

Understanding the structural reasons helps you respond calmly instead of feeling singled out.

Building construction transmits sound

Many affordable apartments in Japan are wooden (木造, mokuzō) or light steel frame (軽量鉄骨, keiryō tekkotsu) buildings. Sound travels through floors and walls far more than in reinforced concrete (RC/鉄筋コンクリート) buildings. Footsteps, doors closing, and appliances can be clearly heard by the unit below or next door. If you live in an older "apāto" style building, you may be much louder than you realize.

Quiet hours and house rules

Most buildings have house rules (often in a booklet called 入居のしおり or in the 使用細則 attached to your contract) that ask residents to keep quiet late at night and early in the morning. A common expectation is roughly 10 pm to 8 am, but this varies by building. Running a washing machine, vacuum, or shower late at night is a frequent trigger.

Landlord risk avoidance

Landlords and management companies care most about keeping the building stable and avoiding long-term tenants moving out. If a long-time Japanese tenant complains repeatedly, the easiest path for the landlord is pressure on the newer tenant. This is one reason some landlords hesitate to rent to foreigners in the first place, and why a history of complaints can make things harder for you later. Language and communication gaps

Notices are written in indirect, polite Japanese. Many foreign residents do not realize a notice is about them, so they do nothing, which looks to the neighbor and management company like ignoring the problem. Silence is often interpreted as disrespect.

Lifestyle differences

Wearing shoes or hard slippers indoors, having guests over in the evening, working night shifts, exercising at home, or calling family abroad late at night are all normal habits that can create sound at times when Japanese neighbors expect silence.

Common mistakes

  • Ignoring the general notice. Assuming "it is not about me" is the most common mistake. If you are a newer resident or have changed your routine recently, assume it might be you and adjust.
  • Confronting the neighbor directly and angrily. Knocking on the downstairs door to argue usually escalates things. Even a calm visit can backfire if there is a language barrier. Going through the management company is generally safer.
  • Arguing that the noise is "normal." Even if you are right that you are just walking around, defending yourself rarely helps. Showing what steps you have taken works much better.
  • Not keeping records. If the complaints are unfair or the noise actually comes from another unit, you need a written log to show the management company.
  • Moving out in a panic. Breaking a lease early can cost you a cancellation penalty and part of your deposit. In most cases, the problem can be solved without moving.

Step-by-step fix

  1. Read the notice carefully and translate it. Use a translation app on the full text. Look for times mentioned (for example "深夜" late at night, "早朝" early morning) and types of sound (足音 footsteps, 洗濯機 washing machine, 話し声 voices, 音楽 music). This tells you what to change.
  2. Contact the management company yourself. Do not wait to be named. Call or email and say something simple, such as: "I saw the notice about noise. If it is about my unit, I am sorry. Could you tell me what kind of noise and what time it happens? I want to fix it." In Japanese: 「騒音についてのお知らせを見ました。もし私の部屋のことでしたら、申し訳ありません。どのような音で、何時ごろか教えていただけますか。改善したいと思います。」 This one message often ends the issue, because it shows good faith.
  3. Make visible changes immediately.
    • Take off shoes indoors and use soft slippers.
    • Put down rugs or joint mats, especially in the main walking areas. These are cheap at home centers and 100-yen shops.
    • Add felt pads to chair and table legs.
    • Run the washing machine, vacuum, and dryer during daytime hours only.
    • Close doors gently, and use door stoppers or cushion tape if doors slam.
    • Move late-night calls to a room away from shared walls, and use headphones for music and videos.
  4. Report back to the management company. A week or so later, send a short follow-up: "I have put down rugs and stopped using the washing machine at night. Please let me know if there are still problems." This creates a written record that you cooperated.
  5. Keep a noise log if you think the complaint is wrong. Write down dates, times, and what you were doing. If you were not home when the noise supposedly happened, note it (train tickets, work schedule, or IC card history can help). Sound in Japanese buildings can travel in confusing ways, and the real source may be a different unit.
  6. If you are the one being disturbed, follow the same route: report to the management company in writing, with your log, rather than confronting the neighbor. For a sudden, severe disturbance late at night, the police non-emergency route or local koban is an option, but use this sparingly because it can sour relationships in the building.
  7. If complaints continue and your lease is threatened, ask the management company in writing what specifically you need to change. Keep copies of every letter. If you receive notice that your contract will not be renewed or will be terminated, read our article on what to do if you get an eviction notice and our guide to lease renewal problems. Local government consultation services for foreign residents can also help you understand your options.
  8. If the building is simply not a fit, consider moving on your own terms rather than waiting for pressure to build. Concrete buildings, ground-floor units, and share houses that are used to international residents can be much less stressful. Required documents

You usually do not need formal paperwork to solve a noise complaint, but keep these ready in case the situation escalates:

  • Your lease contract (賃貸借契約書, chintai shakuken keiyakusho), to check the contract type and renewal terms
  • House rules or move-in booklet (使用細則 / 入居のしおり), to confirm the building's quiet hours
  • Copies or photos of every noise notice you received
  • Your emails or a written record of phone calls with the management company
  • A dated noise log (what happened, when, and where you were)
  • Receipts or photos of rugs, mats, and other soundproofing you added
  • Residence card (在留カード, zairyū kādo), if you visit a consultation service
  • Contact details of your guarantor company (保証会社, hoshō gaisha), in case they are contacted about the dispute

City / region differences

Tokyo: Dense neighborhoods with many small wooden and light steel apartments, especially in areas popular with students and new arrivals. Walls are often thin, and management companies handle large numbers of units, so communication tends to be by printed notice. Getting a written reply from them matters.

Yokohama and Kawasaki: Similar to Tokyo, with many family residents in mixed buildings. Complaints about children running, early morning noise, and late laundry are common.

Osaka and Kobe: Communication can be slightly more direct, and you are a little more likely to hear from a neighbor in person. The same advice applies: stay polite and route ongoing issues through the management company. See our Osaka renting page for local tips.

Kyoto: Older buildings and traditional row houses can have very little sound insulation, and many residents are elderly with early bedtimes. Be especially careful with late-night noise.

Nagoya and Fukuoka: More new RC buildings are available at moderate rents than in central Tokyo, so if noise problems keep repeating, moving to a concrete building can be a realistic fix rather than a luxury.

Frequently Asked Questions

Can I be evicted just for a noise complaint in Japan?

A single complaint is very unlikely to lead to eviction. Japanese tenancy rules generally protect tenants, and ending a lease usually requires a serious, ongoing problem. However, repeated complaints that you ignore can affect whether a fixed-term contract is renewed, so respond early and keep records of your cooperation.

The notice did not name me. Should I still respond?

Yes, if there is any chance it is about you. A short, polite message to the management company asking what kind of noise was reported shows good faith and often closes the issue. If it turns out not to be you, you have lost nothing.

Should I go and apologize to my neighbor in person?

It depends on the building. In some places, a short, polite apology with a small gift is appreciated. But if you do not know which unit complained, or there is a language barrier, it is safer to apologize through the management company. Never visit to argue.

What if the noise is actually coming from another apartment?

Keep a dated log, including times you were out, and send it to the management company in writing. Sound travels unpredictably in Japanese buildings, and management companies do sometimes identify the wrong unit at first.

Will a noise complaint affect my next apartment application?

There is generally no shared public database of noise complaints. The bigger risk is an unresolved dispute affecting your deposit or your relationship with the current landlord. Leaving on good terms, with your deposit refunded fairly, is the best protection. See our article on deposit disputes if that becomes an issue.
