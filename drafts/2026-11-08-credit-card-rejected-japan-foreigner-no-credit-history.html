Credit Card Rejected in Japan as a Foreigner With No Credit History? What to Do Next

You filled in the online form, uploaded your residence card, waited a week, and got a short email saying the issuer "cannot accept your application at this time." No reason given. If you are a foreigner who has just arrived in Japan, this is one of the most common and most frustrating experiences there is. It can feel personal, especially if you had excellent credit back home. It is not personal, and it is usually fixable. This article explains what actually happens behind a rejection, why "no credit history" counts against you here, the mistakes that make things worse, and a step-by-step plan to get your first Japanese card.

What Actually Happens When Your Card Is Rejected

When you apply for a credit card in Japan, the issuer runs a screening process called shinsa (審査). Part of it is automated scoring based on what you wrote on the form: income, employer, years at your job, years at your address, housing type, and residence status. The other part is a check of Japan's personal credit information bureaus. The main ones are CIC, JICC, and the bankers' association credit center (全国銀行個人信用情報センター). Credit card companies mostly report to and check CIC and JICC.

Here is the key point: your credit history from the US, UK, Australia, Canada, or anywhere else does not exist in these databases. Your FICO score, your Experian file, your twenty years of on-time payments are invisible to a Japanese issuer. To the system, you look like someone who has never borrowed money in their life.

In Japan, a completely empty file is sometimes called a "white" record (often described as a "super white" profile). For a young Japanese person fresh out of school, an empty record is expected. For an adult in their thirties or forties, an empty file can look suspicious, because the issuer cannot tell whether you are new to credit or whether your old records simply expired after past problems. The result is often an automatic decline with no explanation.

Issuers almost never tell you the reason. They are not required to explain, and phone support will usually only confirm that the decision was made "after comprehensive review" (総合的な判断).

Why It Happens

Understanding the structure behind the rejection helps you choose the right fix. These are the main reasons foreigners get declined:

  • No domestic credit record. As explained above, the bureaus have nothing on you. Without any repayment history, scoring models fall back on the application details alone, and those details usually look weak for a newcomer.
  • Short time in Japan, at your job, and at your address. Scoring heavily rewards stability. Someone who has been at the same company for three years and the same apartment for two scores far better than someone who arrived four months ago, even if the newcomer earns more.
  • Residence period versus card term. If your residence card shows a period of stay that ends within a year, the issuer may worry that you will leave Japan with an unpaid balance. A longer remaining period of stay generally helps. Permanent residents are in a much stronger position.
  • Housing type. Applicants in company housing, share houses, or rentals with very short tenure tend to score lower than people who own property or have rented the same place for years.
  • Contact and language risk. Issuers need to reach you by phone and mail. If your Japanese phone number is new, prepaid, or a data-only SIM with no voice number, or if your address on the form does not match your residence card, the application may stall or be declined.
  • Too many applications at once. Each application leaves a record on your credit file. CIC keeps application records for about six months. Several applications in a short period can look like financial desperation, which makes the next issuer more likely to decline.

This matters beyond shopping. Many rental guarantor companies in Japan are affiliated with credit card companies and check the same bureaus. A thin or messy credit file can also slow down your apartment search, which is why it is worth fixing early. Common Mistakes

  1. Applying for several cards in the same week. This is the biggest one. After the first decline, many people immediately apply to three or four more issuers. Each declined application stays on record, and the pattern hurts every later application.
  2. Starting with premium or airline cards. Gold cards, travel cards with large bonuses, and cards with high limits have stricter screening. They are the wrong place to start with an empty file.
  3. Requesting a cash advance limit. Many application forms ask whether you want a cashing (キャッシング) limit. Asking for one adds a lending check and can make approval harder. For a first card, set it to zero.
  4. Inconsistent details. Writing your name in a different order than your residence card, using an old address, or listing a phone number that does not accept calls are all common causes of a silent decline.
  5. Inflating income or job tenure. Issuers may confirm employment, and false information can lead to cancellation later. It is never worth it.

Step-by-Step Fix

  1. Stop applying for six months if you have been declined more than once. Give the application records time to drop off your file. Use this period to build the basics in the steps below. If you have only been declined once, you can usually try again sooner with a more realistic card choice.
  2. Get your bank account and phone number sorted first. A Japanese bank account is required for card payments, and a voice-capable Japanese phone number makes you easier to verify. If the bank itself is giving you trouble, read why banks reject foreigners and how to fix it and our article on Japan Post Bank denials. If you still only have a data SIM, it may help to switch to a plan with a Japanese number before applying. Options include [PR] eSIM plans for people living in Japan; check whether a plan includes a voice number before you sign up.
  3. Use a debit card in the meantime. Many Japanese banks issue Visa or JCB debit cards linked to your account. There is no credit screening in the usual sense because you spend your own money. A debit card covers most online shopping, subscriptions, and many in-store purchases while you wait.
  4. Pay bills on time from your Japanese account. Your phone bill, especially if you buy a handset on installments, is often reported to credit bureaus. Paying a smartphone installment plan on time is one of the most common ways newcomers start building a record. Never miss a payment, even by a few days.
  5. Choose an entry-level card for your first application. Cards issued by retail groups, supermarket chains, online shopping platforms, and some online banks are widely reported to be more accessible for people with thin files than bank-issued premium cards. Look for no annual fee, a low starting limit, and no cash advance option. Apply for one card only.
  6. Fill in the form carefully. Match your name exactly to your residence card, use your registered address, list a phone number that accepts calls, and set the cash advance limit to zero. Include all regular income. If you have savings, some forms let you declare them.
  7. Answer the verification call. Some issuers call your phone or your workplace to confirm details. If your Japanese is limited, it can help to tell your HR team in advance that a card company may call to confirm you work there. A missed call can delay or derail the process.
  8. If declined again, wait and rebuild. Keep your debit card, keep paying your phone and utility bills on time, and reapply after six months. Each month of stable employment, address, and on-time payments improves your next application.
  9. Once approved, use it lightly and pay in full. Put a small recurring bill on the card and pay the full balance every month with the single-payment option (一括払い). After six to twelve months of clean history, upgrading or adding a second card becomes much easier.
Do not use revolving payments (リボ払い) to "build credit." Some cards push revolving repayment as a default or through campaigns. It does not help your approval odds in a meaningful way, and interest rates on revolving balances in Japan are high. Always check that your card is set to pay in full each month.

Required Documents

Exact requirements vary by issuer, but have these ready before you apply:

  • Residence card (在留カード), front and back, with your current address on the back
  • Passport, in case the issuer asks for additional identification
  • Japanese bank account (銀行口座) in your name, with cash card or online banking access for setting up automatic payments
  • Japanese phone number that accepts voice calls and SMS
  • Employer name, address, and phone number, plus your start date
  • Annual income estimate; recent payslips (給与明細) or a tax withholding slip (源泉徴収票) if requested
  • For students: student ID and, if relevant, your permission to work (資格外活動許可)
  • My Number card (マイナンバーカード) if you have one, which some issuers accept for online identity checks

City and Region Differences

Credit screening itself is national. CIC and JICC are the same whether you live in Tokyo or Fukuoka, and issuers apply the same scoring rules everywhere. What differs by region is the practical side.

Tokyo and Yokohama have the most banks and card counters with English support, and more employers are used to foreign staff applying for cards. You are more likely to find staff at a department store or retail card desk who can help you in English. Competition among issuers is also highest here, so entry-level campaigns are common.

Osaka, Kyoto, and Kobe have good coverage from national issuers, and regional banks also issue cards. English support is available at major branches but less consistent. If you are settling in Kansai, our Osaka renting page covers how a thin credit file can affect guarantor company screening in the region.

Nagoya and Fukuoka have strong regional banks with their own card brands. These banks sometimes look more favorably on customers who already hold an account and receive salary there. If your employer pays into a regional bank, ask that bank about its card first.

Outside major cities, in-person help in English is limited, so online applications with careful form filling become even more important.

Frequently Asked Questions

Can I use my credit history from my home country to get a Japanese card?

Generally, no. Japanese issuers check domestic credit bureaus, and foreign credit reports are not part of that system. Some international card issuers may consider your existing relationship with them, but for a Japanese domestic card you usually start from zero.

How long should I wait after a rejection before applying again?

If you have been declined once, you can try a more accessible card after improving your application. If you have been declined two or more times recently, waiting about six months is safer, because application records on credit bureaus generally remain for around that long.

Can I check what my Japanese credit file says?

Yes. CIC and JICC allow individuals to request disclosure of their own records, online or by mail, for a small fee. The reports are in Japanese, so you may need help reading them, but they show applications and payment history linked to your name.

Will a credit card rejection affect my apartment application?

It can. Some rental guarantor companies are credit card-affiliated and check the same bureaus, so recent declined applications or missed payments may be visible. A clean but thin file is usually less of a problem than late payments. For options, see our no-guarantor renting page.

Is a debit card good enough to live in Japan?

For most daily needs, yes. Debit cards work for online shopping, many subscriptions, and most stores that accept cards. Some services, such as certain rental guarantor payments, car rentals, or hotel deposits, may still require a credit card, so it is worth building toward one over your first year.
